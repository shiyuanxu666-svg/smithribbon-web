Ribbon Quote Comparison and Cost Normalisation — How to Tell Which Mill Price Is Actually Cheaper (2026)

Published: 2026-11-16  |  Updated: 2026-11-16  |  By Xiamen Smith Ribbon & Bow Co., Ltd.  |  Since 2004

Two ribbon quotes that look 12% apart are often the same price once both are written onto the same basis. The most common reason a cheaper quote loses money in practice is not the mill's pricing but the buyer's own failure to normalise scope, Incoterms, MOQ tier and wastage before comparing. A structured comparison takes about twenty minutes per quote and is the single highest-return step in ribbon sourcing.

Brand procurement teams are, on average, poor at comparing quotations. Not because the people involved are careless, but because the quotations they receive are not written in a comparable form. One mill quotes EXW with a separate tooling charge and a 3,000 metre minimum. Another quotes FOB with tooling included at 5,000 metres minimum. A third quotes a per-metre price that already contains a generous allowance for offcuts, a fourth quotes a tight allowance because their winding process produces almost no waste. Put those four numbers side by side and the lowest one looks like a saving until you work out what it actually buys.

This guide is a method, not a price list. It describes how to take quotations from different mills and reduce them to one comparable figure — a fully loaded cost per metre at your actual order volume, on your actual Incoterms, with your actual wastage assumption — and then how to judge which quote is genuinely the better commercial outcome rather than merely the smaller number.

Step One: Establish What Is Actually Being Quoted

Before any comparison is possible, every quote has to be expanded to a full specification. Most ribbon quotations in their raw form omit information that changes the price by more than the price difference you are trying to resolve. A quote that says "28mm grosgrain, USD 0.42/m, FOB Xiamen" is not a comparable offer until the following are known.

What must be specifiedWhy it changes the price
Material and constructionPolyester grosgrain, satin, velvet, organza, RPET and cotton tape differ in raw material cost by a wide margin. "Grosgrain" alone is not a specification.
WidthCost per metre falls with width on most constructions because the yarn cost per usable metre drops, but not proportionally. A 40mm width is not 40mm of 28mm cost.
Colour and finishStock colour, dye-to-order custom colour, and match-to-Pantone are three different cost structures, and the third is usually the most expensive by a wide margin.
DecorationPrinted, foil-stamped, woven jacquard, embossed and edge-wired are separate operations, each with its own setup and its own waste.
Winding and packingRoll length, core type, winding direction, splices per roll and carton configuration are usually quoted separately by mills that take them seriously.
Minimum order quantityThe quoted price is usually tied to a quantity tier. A price valid at 100,000 metres says very little about a 3,000 metre order.
Incoterms and named placeEXW and FOB are different obligations with different cost implications, and the named place matters.
Lead time and validityA quote valid for three days is not a basis for a sourcing decision, and a quoted lead time that ignores the mill's current order book is not a commitment.

The practical rule is that you should not put two quotes side by side until you can state, for each, exactly what you would receive and what you would pay. If the information is missing, that is not a clerical detail to chase later — it is usually a signal that the mill has not quoted the job properly, which is itself information about the mill.

Step Two: Normalise the Scope

Once the scope is known, expand each quote into a line-item sheet with the same categories for every mill. A workable structure is below; the categories matter less than the fact that they are identical across all bids.

LineWhat it captures
Raw material per usable metreYarn cost adjusted for the yield the mill's process actually achieves, not the theoretical best.
Processing per metreWeaving, dyeing, finishing and winding, apportioned across the order.
Decoration per metrePrint, foil or jacquard setup apportioned plus the decorated length actually produced.
Setup, amortisedSetup costs spread over the order quantity, with the amortisation shown separately rather than buried in the unit rate.
Wastage and offcut allowanceThe percentage of ordered metres that does not reach you as saleable product.
Packing and cartonsAt your specified roll format and carton configuration.
Quality and inspection costInspection, third-party pre-shipment inspection if required, and certification amortisation.
Freight and insurance to your doorBased on a single agreed freight assumption applied identically to all bids.
Import duty and taxApplied at the same rate to all bids, with the HS classification confirmed by your broker.

Why wastage deserves its own line

Wastage is the line most often handled inconsistently, and it is frequently the largest single reason two quotes appear incomparable. A mill that runs multi-needle weaving with a wide beam will produce a given ordered length with less offcut than a mill running a narrower loom, but that mill may charge more per metre for the base fabric. Conversely, a mill that quotes a very low rate often assumes a generous cutting allowance that lets it recover its margin in the offcut rather than in the rate.

When comparing, apply the same wastage allowance to every bid initially — say 3% — and then run a sensitivity case at 2% and 5%. If the ranking of the quotes changes between those two scenarios, wastage assumption is driving the decision, and you should ask each mill directly for the offcut recovery rate on your specific width and decoration rather than assuming.

Why setup must be shown separately

Setup costs are real and unavoidable, but they behave differently from unit cost: they are fixed for the job and they vanish as the order grows. A mill that quotes a low rate and a high setup may be entirely correct about the unit price while still being the wrong choice for a 500 metre order, and the same mill may be the right choice at 50,000 metres.

Show setup as a separate line and then model the break-even volume against the competitor's quote. This is often the most valuable output of the whole exercise, because it tells you not only which mill is cheapest but how much volume you need to commit before the cheapest mill becomes the cheapest mill. Many programmes fail because the buyer awarded the order on a one-off evaluation and then kept ordering from the winner at volumes far below the point where the fixed costs had been amortised.

Step Three: Put Every Bid on the Same Incoterms Basis

Incoterms differences are the most common source of a false price gap, and the most embarrassing, because it is obvious in hindsight.

Under EXW, the buyer takes on export clearance, the loading of the container and the inland transport from the mill gate. Under FOB, the mill handles export clearance and loading at the named port, and the buyer takes the cargo from that point. The mill may absorb part of the difference in its price and part of it may flow straight through, which is why two quotes can differ in the buyer's actual cost by far less than the quoted Incoterms suggest — or by rather more, depending on how far the mill is from the port.

The safe method is to ask every mill to quote the same Incoterms, ideally FOB with a named port, and to apply your own inland and freight assumptions on top. If a mill will only quote EXW, convert it yourself using a specific and disclosed basis: the mill's gate-to-port transport cost at a stated rate per container, plus the export clearance and documentation fee. Write that conversion down and apply it identically to any other EXW bid you receive.

Buyers should also be clear about who bears cost escalation on long shipments. On a quote valid for thirty days with a two-month lead time, the duty rate and the freight rate both move during the exposure period, and the mill has no obligation to absorb that. The quote is a snapshot of a specific date's economics, not a commitment to deliver at that price in ninety days.

Step Four: Adjust for MOQ Tier and Currency

Every ribbon mill prices in tiers, and the tier you are quoted matters more than the tier you would prefer. A quote at the 3,000 metre minimum may be 18% above the same mill's rate at 30,000 metres. Comparing that 3,000 metre quote against another mill's 30,000 metre rate is not a comparison of efficiency; it is a comparison of two different commitments.

The useful exercise is to build a small quantity-price curve for each mill and then decide which volume you are actually prepared to commit to, before asking for the final price. A buyer who is confident of 20,000 metres per year can legitimately negotiate a tier price at that volume, but should discount that price when comparing against a mill that will sell at the same volume with no commitment, because a tier price contingent on volume is only worth its discount if the volume actually arrives.

On currency, the effect is often larger than buyers expect. If a mill quotes in US dollars while its costs are in renminbi, its margin moves with the exchange rate, and a quote valid for four weeks carries exchange risk that the mill will eventually price in. Quoting in the currency of your own cost base is not always the cheapest option, but a buyer who understands the exposure can negotiate a shorter validity period and a faster order decision, which is often worth more than a small currency concession.

Step Five: Compare the Risk-Adjusted Figure, Not the Rate

The lowest normalised cost is not automatically the best purchase. Two further adjustments belong in the comparison.

First, risk-weighted cost. A quote from a mill that has not produced your specification before carries a higher probability of an order that needs rework, a colour that misses, or a delivery that slips. If the observed cost of that failure is, say, 4% of order value and the probability differs between two mills by 10 percentage points, the expected difference in cost is 0.4% of order value — which on a large programme is frequently larger than the price gap that drove the decision. This calculation rarely gets done, and it is one of the strongest arguments for paying a little more to a supplier with a proven record on your exact specification.

Second, cash and balance-sheet cost. If one quote requires 30% deposit and the other requires 100% prepayment, the difference in working capital across a year of orders has a real cost. So does a longer lead time, because inventory held longer consumes space and capital. And so does a payment term that includes a letter of credit fee, which should be added to the comparison even though it appears nowhere in the unit rate.

What survives this comparison is a single number: a fully loaded, risk-adjusted cost per saleable metre, at a stated volume, on a stated Incoterms, with a stated wastage assumption. That number is what should be compared across bids, and every assumption behind it should be written down so that the next quotation round starts from the same baseline rather than from zero.

Building a Repeatable Comparison Sheet

The value of this exercise compounds if the format is standardised so that every quotation round uses the same sheet. The minimum viable version has five columns: mill, specification reference, normalised cost per saleable metre at stated volume, lead time with validity, and notes on deviations from the specification. Add two more columns — risk rating and the confidence level attached to the rate — and the sheet doubles as a supplier scorecard that carries forward from year to year.

Keep the file. A procurement team that has three years of normalised comparison sheets knows something about its own supply base that cannot be reconstructed from the current quotes alone: which mill's rates have moved and which have not, which quote held and which had to be renegotiated at order, and where the market is genuinely tight. That institutional memory is worth more than any single sourcing win.

Working With Your Mill on the Comparison

A good supplier will help you build this. Ask for a quotation that breaks out material, processing, decoration, setup and packing separately, and ask whether the rate at your volume differs from the rate at the stated minimum. Most established ribbon factories can produce that breakdown readily, because they quote the same way internally and the question is simply whether they are willing to show it. A mill that cannot or will not provide the breakdown is telling you something important, and it is worth knowing that before you have placed an order rather than after.

Where you want a comparison you can rely on, ask each mill to quote against the same written specification, with the same named port and the same volume scenario, and give them the same deadline for response. A fast, specific request produces a considered quotation. A vague one produces a quote built on assumptions that will be renegotiated later.

The Checklist

  • Expand every quote to the same eight specification fields before comparing anything.
  • Convert every bid to one Incoterms, with the named place specified.
  • Move setup out of the unit rate and amortise it over the volume you will actually buy.
  • Apply one wastage assumption, then test it at two other values to see if it changes the ranking.
  • Match every MOQ tier to your real committed volume, and discount contingent rates accordingly.
  • Add freight, duty, inspection and certification to every bid identically.
  • Weight for the difference in risk between a proven specification and an unproven one.
  • Record the assumptions beside the numbers, and keep the sheet for the next round.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. How do I compare ribbon quotes from different factories?

Expand each quote to an identical specification first, then normalise the scope onto the same line items: raw material per usable metre, processing, decoration, setup amortised over your real volume, wastage allowance, packing, inspection, freight, duty and tax. Put every bid on the same Incoterms with the same named port, then compare the resulting fully loaded cost per saleable metre. The method matters more than the spreadsheet, because most apparent price gaps turn out to be differences in scope rather than differences in efficiency.

2. What is the difference between EXW and FOB when comparing ribbon prices?

EXW places export clearance, container loading and inland transport on the buyer from the mill gate, while FOB has the mill handle export clearance and loading at the named port. The mill may absorb part of that difference in its price and pass part through, so the real gap is often smaller than it appears. The reliable method is to ask every mill for the same Incoterms, ideally FOB with a named port, and apply your own identical inland and freight assumptions on top. If a mill will only quote EXW, convert it using a disclosed, written basis and apply that same conversion to any other EXW bid.

3. How should I treat setup fees when comparing ribbon quotations?

Show them as a separate line and amortise them over the volume you are actually going to buy, rather than letting them disappear into the unit rate. Setup is fixed for the job and vanishes as volume grows, so a mill that quotes a low rate with a high setup may be right for a large programme and wrong for a small one. The most useful output of the comparison is often the break-even volume at which the mill with the higher setup becomes the cheaper choice. Many programmes are awarded on a single evaluation and then continue at volumes far below that break-even point.

4. Does the MOQ tier a mill quotes really affect which mill is cheapest?

Very often, yes. Ribbon mills price in tiers and the difference between the rate at the stated minimum and the rate at your real volume can be substantial, commonly double-digit percentages. Comparing a rate contingent on a 30,000 metre commitment against a rate a competitor will sell at 3,000 metres with no commitment is not a like-for-like comparison. The right approach is to establish the volume you are genuinely prepared to commit to, request that tier from every mill, and then discount tier prices where the future volume is forecast rather than contracted.

5. What is a reasonable wastage allowance when comparing ribbon quotes?

There is no universal figure, which is exactly why it matters. Offcut rates depend on width, construction, decoration and the mill's loom and cutting setup, and they can differ meaningfully between suppliers running different equipment. Apply one common allowance to all bids first so the comparison starts on equal terms, then test it at a tighter and a looser value. If the ranking changes between those scenarios, wastage is driving the decision and you should ask each mill for the actual offcut recovery rate on your specification rather than assuming one.

6. Should I include freight and duty when comparing ribbon quotes?

Yes, and you must apply the same assumptions to every bid, otherwise the comparison is meaningless. Use a single freight basis — the same container type, the same route, the same rate estimate — for all mills, and confirm the HS classification with your broker so the duty rate applied is correct. Then add inspection and certification costs, which are frequently omitted from quotes but are real and are amortised differently between a first order and a repeat one.

7. How do I account for currency when comparing quotes from different mills?

Be aware of where each mill's cost base sits. A mill quoting in US dollars while paying in renminbi carries margin exposure to the exchange rate, and that exposure will eventually be priced into the quote even if it is not visible on the day you receive it. Where you can, quote in the currency of your own cost base; where you cannot, shorten the quote validity period and move faster on the order decision, which usually preserves more value than a small currency concession would have gained.

8. What if one ribbon quote is much cheaper than all the others?

Treat it as a signal to check rather than as a saving to bank. A materially lower rate usually reflects one of four things: a different specification, a narrower scope of work, a larger volume commitment attached to the rate, or an omission that will be added to the invoice later. Ask directly which it is. The best outcome is to identify that the quote is genuinely more efficient, in which case you have found a real structural saving worth defending. The most common outcome is that the gap was scope, and the price difference was never real.

9. How long should a ribbon quotation remain valid?

Short enough to reflect the mill's actual input costs and long enough for you to run a comparison and a decision, which for most programmes means two to four weeks. A quote valid for three days is not usable, and a quote dated two months ago is not a price for today. When you need longer validity than the mill wants to offer, the trade is usually available elsewhere in the commercial terms rather than in the unit rate, and is worth asking for directly.
