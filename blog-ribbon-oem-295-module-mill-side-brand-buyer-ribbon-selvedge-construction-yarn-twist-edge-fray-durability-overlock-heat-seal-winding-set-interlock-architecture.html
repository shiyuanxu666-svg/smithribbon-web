Ribbon OEM Published November 1, 2026 · Xiamen Smith Ribbon & Bow Co., Ltd. · 16 min read

The edge of a ribbon is the part of the construction nobody specifies, and it is where a disproportionate share of avoidable quality failures originate. A ribbon can be correct in width, colour, material and hand feel, and still fray in the carton - because the selvedge is the most mechanically stressed part of the fabric, because the yarn system at the edge is frequently left unspecified, and because finishing and winding both act on it in ways that only show up later. This module sets out how edge construction actually works, what the yarn parameters do to edge strength, how finishing alters it, how each failure mode presents, and how to write an edge specification that two mills can both meet.

The Edge Is the Part of the Ribbon Nobody Specifies

A brand buyer can specify width, colour, material, decoration and hand feel with precision, and still be handed a ribbon whose edges fray in the carton. This is not unusual. Edge behaviour is the property most often left out of a ribbon specification, and it is also one of the properties most likely to destroy a programme - because edge failure is not gradual. It does not degrade politely over a season. It shows up as a rejected batch, a blocked line, a rework cost, a substitution, or a customer complaint that traces back to a trim component nobody thought to check.

The technical reason is straightforward. The selvedge - the woven edge of a ribbon - is structurally the most vulnerable part of the construction. It is where the weft yarns turn around, which means the edge carries a doubled and mechanically stressed yarn system rather than a continuous one. It is where the warp yarns terminate, which means it is where tension changes direction. And it is where a ribbon is handled: tied, bent around a bow, folded over a pack edge, pulled through a bundling machine, or wound onto a spool under tension. Every one of those operations puts a disproportionate load on the two narrow strips of fabric that are, by design, not meant to carry load.

This is why edge behaviour can vary so much between two rolls of what is nominally the same ribbon. It depends on the yarn twist in the edge, on how the edge was constructed during weaving, on the finishing applied afterwards, on how the roll was wound and how long it sat under tension before it was used, and on how the finished ribbon was subsequently handled. A roll that was over-tensioned on the core and then relaxed in storage can present a completely different edge from a roll that was wound at the correct tension.

For a mill-side or brand-buyer conversation about quality, the useful question is not 'does the edge fray' but 'what exactly is the edge made of, and what happens to it under the three or four handling operations it will actually see in our own process'. Most edge complaints come from a mismatch between an edge designed for one use and an edge deployed in another.

How Selvedge Construction Actually Works

There are three practical ways a ribbon edge is made, and they behave very differently. Understanding which one you have tells you most of what you need to know about its durability before you ever test it.

The plain selvedge - sometimes called a locked or tucked selvedge - is produced by allowing the outermost warp ends to turn back into the fabric, so the edge is closed by the weaving process itself rather than by a separate mechanical operation. It looks clean and feels firm, and it is the most durable of the three for ordinary handling. The warp ends interlock with the weft at the edge, so the edge has a continuous structure and does not need to be held together by anything else.

The cut edge is what you get when a wide ribbon or tape is slit or cut through its width to produce a narrower ribbon. There is no interlocking structure at the edge at all - it is a raw cut face, sometimes with a heat-sealed or glued treatment applied afterwards to reduce fraying. A cut edge is by definition the least robust option, and the cut quality depends heavily on blade condition, feed rate, and whether the material is being cut hot, cold or ultrasonically.

The overlocked or serged edge is a separately applied stitch that binds the cut edge with thread, sometimes combined with a hem fold or a tape reinforcement. It is the most visibly reinforced option and the most repairable, and it is the standard for ribbons destined for garments, bags and any application where the edge will be exposed to abrasion and repeated bending. It also has a cost: added thread, added bulk at the edge, an added stiffness profile that affects drape, and an added appearance that may or may not suit the design.

In practice many commercial ribbons are a combination. A satin ribbon woven at width may be slit to a narrower width and then overlocked or heat-sealed at the edge. A grosgrain may be woven with a plain selvedge on one side and cut on the other. A printed ribbon on a broad substrate may have its edge cut and then taped. This is precisely why 'is the edge overlocked' is not a sufficient specification: you need to know what each edge is, separately, because a two-edge ribbon can easily have two different constructions with two different behaviours.

Yarn Twist, Yarn Count and What They Do to Edge Strength

The strength of an edge is largely determined by the yarn system used at that edge, and the two parameters that govern it are twist level and yarn count.

Twist is what holds a yarn together. A yarn with too little twist has poor abrasion resistance and will shed fibres at any stress point, including the selvedge where the warp turns. A yarn with too much twist becomes lively and hairy, develops a tendency to kink, and creates a fabric with unstable dimensions that is difficult to wind and hard to control in downstream processes. The correct twist is the one that balances fibre retention against fabric stability, and it is one of the first things a competent mill matches and one of the easiest things to get wrong in a rush order.

Yarn count sets how much material is available at the edge to absorb load. A heavier yarn - a higher denier or a higher Nm - gives a more robust edge because there is simply more material to take the tension and more surface to bind into the structure. A fine-count yarn in the same construction gives a delicate edge that will show damage sooner. When buyers specify a ribbon by appearance and material family alone, the yarn count is frequently left to the mill, and it is one of the levers that explains why two quotes for 'the same' ribbon behave differently.

The fibre blend matters as much as the count. Polyester has excellent abrasion resistance and low moisture pickup, which makes it the most forgiving edge material in storage and transport. Cotton has better grip and a softer hand but absorbs moisture, which is worth noting at the edge specifically because a cotton selvedge that has picked up moisture in a humid warehouse is materially weaker and more prone to fraying than the same edge that arrived dry. Rayon and viscose sit between the two. Nylon is the toughest of the common options and is used where abrasion is severe. Metallic and novelty yarns, which appear in trim and fashion applications, are the exception that proves the rule - a metallic weft in the edge is a discrete element that can work loose and it cannot be relied upon for edge strength at all.

This is the point at which edge specification and material specification meet. You cannot specify edge durability independently of what the edge is made of, and it is worth stating both together in the same document so the mill is not left to infer one from the other.

Finishing and Its Effect on the Edge

Finishing is where many edge problems are actually created, because finishing is applied after the fabric is formed and it acts on the selvedge as well as the face.

Calendering and heat-setting compress the fabric and stabilise its dimensions. These processes are generally good for the edge, because they lock the weave in place and reduce the movement that leads to fraying. They are also where the edge width tolerance and the ribbon's tendency to cup or curl are determined, and heat-setting at the wrong tension can introduce a curl that then concentrates all subsequent handling stress on one edge.

Sizing and chemical finishes affect the edge in a way that surprises people. A size that improves the body and drape of the face can leave the edge slightly stiffer and more brittle, and a chemical finish that improves colour fastness or hand feel can reduce the fibre's tolerance for repeated flexing. Where an edge is going to be bent sharply and repeatedly - which is the normal life of decorative ribbon - this trade-off is worth knowing about before it shows up as a crack.

Heat-sealing and edge-coating are the most common remedial treatments for cut edges. They work by melting or fusing the edge fibres, which stops them from pulling out. They are fast, they are cheap, and they work well on synthetic ribbon in a controlled environment. The limitations are equally well defined. A heat-sealed edge has a hard, sometimes glossy line that can be visible on a finished pack and can crack if the ribbon is subsequently bent sharply or stored under pressure for a long period. It also cannot be undone and cannot be overlocked later.

Waxing, calendaring under high pressure and heavy calendaring can all embrittle an edge if taken too far, and any of them can be the reason a ribbon that passed inspection frayed in the customer's warehouse rather than on your line.

The practical implication for a specification is this: state the edge treatment explicitly, state the maximum acceptable line width of any visible seal or coating, and state whether the edge may be subsequently sewn, folded or heat-pressed. A ribbon with a sealed edge that the customer intends to sew into a bow has a different requirement from one that will be knotted, because a knotted sealed edge concentrates stress at a single point.

How Edge Failure Actually Shows Up

Recognising the failure mode tells you which lever to pull, so it is worth being precise about the presentation.

Edge fray. Fibres pull out along the cut edge as a progressive shedding, typically starting at the point of highest stress - the tie point, the fold, the feed through the bundling machine. It is usually accompanied by a visible fuzz line and by increasing lint in the packing environment. This is the classic symptom of an inadequate yarn system, an inadequate edge treatment, or a heat-sealed edge that has been overstressed.

Edge curl or cupping. The edge lifts or rolls away from the flat of the ribbon. This is a tension and heat-set problem rather than a yarn problem, and it makes handling worse because a curled edge does not lie flat when it is tied or folded, and the curl concentrates the load.

Edge ribboning or striping. Narrow longitudinal strips separate from the body of the ribbon, sometimes only visible when the ribbon is stretched. This indicates insufficient warp density at the edge or an excessive number of weft turns creating a lumpy edge profile that has been pulled apart in handling.

Edge breakage at the bow or knot. The edge fails where it is bent hardest, sometimes with an audible crack. This points to over-finishing, to an over-stiff edge treatment, or to a fibre blend that has been degraded by a chemical finish.

Slipping at the knot. The ribbon cuts into itself or the knot runs loose. This is an interaction problem rather than an edge problem - it depends on the surface texture of the face, the body of the ribbon, and the tension applied, and an over-stiff edge treatment makes it worse because it cannot conform into the knot.

Edge abrasion wear. A fuzzy, thinned edge where the ribbon has been dragged against packaging or a machine guide. This is why a very high-twist, very stiff edge treatment can be a poor choice for a ribbon that will be fed through automated packing equipment, even though it looks robust on the shelf.

How to Test Edge Durability Before You Commit

There is no single industry-standard edge test that settles the question for every application, because edge behaviour is application-specific. What there is, however, is a set of practical tests that a buyer can specify, and a disciplined way to write them so that two mills are actually being compared.

Specify the test, not the property. 'Good fray resistance' is not a specification. 'No visible fibre loss after 50 rub cycles with a 500 g load on a 20 mm wide cotton-backed abrasive pad at a 45-degree angle' is a specification, and it can be run by any competent lab or by any competent mill with a fixture.

Use the right test for the failure mode you fear. For fray, a rub-and-count test with fibre loss assessed under magnification is the standard approach. For edge breakage at a bend, a mandrel test - bending the ribbon around a mandrel of a stated diameter at a stated number of cycles and inspecting for cracks - is far more predictive than a visual examination of a fresh sample. For curling, a flatness measurement on a conditioned specimen under stated temperature and humidity is the useful test. For knot performance, an actual knot test with a stated number of tie-untie cycles at a stated tension.

Condition before you test. This is the step that is most often skipped and it changes results materially. Ribbon that has been stored in a warehouse at 30 degrees and 75 percent relative humidity, or that has been wound under excess tension and then relaxed, will not behave like ribbon that has been conditioned to a standard atmosphere. Specify a conditioning step - a defined temperature and humidity and a defined dwell time - before any physical test, and require the test to be performed on specimens taken from the production lot rather than from a development sample.

Test after the real process, not before it. The most predictive test for a trim component is the one that runs the ribbon through the customer's own downstream operation. For a ribbon that will be tied into bows, that means tying bows and cycling them. For a ribbon that will be wound onto spools, that means winding and unwinding under the actual tension. For a ribbon that goes through bundling machinery, that means running the bundler. This catches the failures that no laboratory rub test will predict, because it reproduces the real stress geometry rather than a standardised approximation of it.

State the conditioning of the sample's history. A ribbon that has been wound onto a core under high tension, stored for several weeks and then rewound behaves differently from one that has been wound and used immediately, and this difference is often attributed to the ribbon when it is really the process. If your programme involves ribbons being held as stock for months before use, test the aged condition - and be aware that some edge treatments degrade measurably with age.

Rolling, Winding and the Edge Damage You Do Not See Yet

A surprising proportion of edge failures are created before the ribbon reaches the customer, in the winding and packing stages, and they do not become visible until the ribbon is handled.

The mechanism is edge-to-edge compression in the roll. When a ribbon is wound onto a core under tension, the layers press against each other, and the pressure concentrates on the two edges, which are the stiffest and least compliant parts of the section. Over time, and especially over a long storage period at temperature, this compressed edge takes a set. When the ribbon is then pulled off the roll, the edges are the first part to release, and the first bend or tie exposes the deformation.

The controls for this are well understood. Core diameter and core rigidity matter - a narrow core creates a high curvature at the point of winding and a more severe set in the layers near it. Winding tension needs to be as low as the process allows, and tension should be constant rather than varying across the roll, because a varying tension produces a loosely wound section that subsequently slumps and creases. Roll length should be chosen so that the roll is not so long that the inner layers are under sustained high compression for months. Storage should be flat, not on edge, and away from heat. And the conditioning step in testing should replicate the storage profile you actually have.

For a mill-side conversation this matters because it explains a class of complaint that arrives with no obvious cause. The ribbon looks correct on receipt, passes inspection, and then fails in the customer's process three weeks later. The cause is frequently a set that was put into the roll during winding, and the corrective action is in the winding room rather than in the weave room.

The same applies to carton design. A ribbon packed in a carton with insufficient internal support will bow and develop a set at the fold lines, and the fold line is precisely where the edge takes the most stress when the carton is opened and the ribbon is pulled out.

Writing an Edge Specification That Two Mills Can Both Meet

A workable edge specification has six components, and it is worth writing all six because each of them closes a different route to failure.

Construction type - plain or locked selvedge, cut edge, overlocked and stitched, heat-sealed, taped, or a stated combination, specified separately for each edge of the ribbon. Where the ribbon is symmetrical, say so and specify one construction for both. Where it is not - and a ribbon with a woven selvedge on one side and a cut edge on the other is entirely normal - specify both, because they will not behave the same way.

Edge treatment and its parameters - the thread count and type for any overlock, the seal line width for any heat seal, the tape specification for any taped edge, and whether any edge may be left untreated. Also state whether the treatment may be visible on the face of the ribbon, because an edge seal line that encroaches on the printable area is a frequent and easily avoidable rejection.

Yarn system at the edge - the yarn count, the twist level and the fibre composition used in the edge, to the extent that these are specified for the body of the ribbon. Where the body is specified by fibre family and construction but the edge is not, the edge becomes the weak point of an otherwise controlled specification.

Edge width tolerance - the permitted variation in finished edge position and in total width at the edge. This is where the interaction with the cut edge matters: a slit ribbon's edge position depends on blade alignment, and a mill with a good slitting operation holds this tightly while one without it does not.

Performance requirements - the actual tests, with parameters: rub cycles, load, abrasion medium, mandrel diameter and cycle count, knot cycle count and tension, and the acceptance criterion in each case. Include the conditioning step.

Downstream compatibility - whether the edge will be sewn, knotted, folded, glued, heat-pressed or fed through automated equipment, and any prohibition on edge treatments that would interfere. This is the clause that prevents a mill from over-solving the edge problem with a treatment that then causes a failure somewhere else in the customer's line.

Two drafting points that save time later. First, make the specification say which properties are characteristics and which are critical characteristics, because a mill treats them differently and a buyer treats them differently - and a buyer who discovers after three orders that the edge construction was never actually a critical characteristic has discovered it too late. Second, state the sampling regime for edge assessment within an incoming-goods inspection. Edge quality is a visual and tactile judgement, and without a defined sample size and a defined visual reference, two inspectors will disagree and the mill will argue.

What to Ask a Ribbon Mill About Edge

These are the questions that reveal whether a mill has genuinely controlled its edge, rather than merely inherited it from a loom setting.

How is the edge constructed on this construction, and is it the same on both edges? What happens to the edge at the point where a wide ribbon is cut down to the finished width - and is the cut edge subsequently treated? What is the edge yarn count and twist, and is it the same yarn as the body or a deliberate upgrade? How do you control edge position and edge straightness after slitting, and what is the tolerance you hold? What tension do you wind to, and what core do you use? What is your storage profile for finished goods, and what is the maximum time you recommend between winding and shipment? How do you assess edge quality yourself - what do you look at, and what do you record? If I send you a rejected edge, what would you measure to find the cause - and in which part of your process would you expect it to be?

And the question that tells you the most: can you send me a length of the finished edge, so I can look at it rather than look at a photograph? A physical edge sample tells you the construction, the yarn, the treatment and the finish in a way that no description and no photograph can, and it takes ten minutes to send and will save a rejection.

The Edge Is Not a Detail

The edge of a ribbon is small, it is cheap to specify properly, and it is where a surprising proportion of avoidable quality failures originate. It is also the property most likely to be left implicit in a brief, because it is the one nobody thinks about until it fails.

The mills that control their edge well are not doing anything exotic. They are running a construction that suits the application, choosing an edge yarn that suits the load, applying a treatment that suits the downstream process, winding to a tension and a core that do not pre-damage what they have made, and specifying all of it in a document so that the next order and the next buyer get the same thing.

Ask for the edge sample. Put it in the specification. Test it after the real process. And when a programme is stable, check it again - because the edge is the part of the ribbon most likely to have been quietly changed by something that looked like an improvement.

The rest of the ribbon is what the customer sees. The edge is what the customer blames.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a plain selvedge and an overlocked edge?

A plain selvedge is closed by the weaving process itself, with the outermost warp ends turning back into the fabric so the edge interlocks structurally. It is firm, clean and durable, and it is the most robust option for ordinary handling. An overlocked edge is a separately applied stitch binding a raw cut edge, usually with a thread of its own. It is more visibly reinforced and more repairable, at the cost of extra bulk, extra stiffness at the edge, a visible appearance, and the fact that a bad overlock can itself be a failure point. The right choice depends on how the ribbon will be handled and whether the edge will be visible.

Why does a cut edge fray when a woven selvedge does not?

Because a cut edge has no interlocking structure. The warp yarns simply terminate at the cut, and the weft yarns turn back at that face with nothing holding them. A woven selvedge is closed by the interlacement of the outermost warp ends with the weft, which distributes stress across the edge rather than concentrating it at a raw face. A cut edge can be made considerably more resistant with a heat seal, a coating or an overlock, but the underlying structure remains a cut structure and the treatment is mitigation rather than equivalence.

How do I specify edge fray resistance so two suppliers are actually comparable?

Specify the test rather than the property. For example: a rub-and-count test with a stated abrasive medium, stated load, stated angle and stated cycle count, with acceptance defined as no visible fibre loss under a given magnification. Add the conditioning step - a defined temperature, humidity and dwell time before testing - and require the specimens to be taken from the production lot rather than a development sample. Also state the construction type and any edge treatment, because a rub test on an unsealed cut edge and a rub test on an overlocked edge are not the same test.

Does heat-sealing the edge of a ribbon solve the problem?

It mitigates it, effectively and cheaply, and for many applications it is entirely sufficient. The limitations are that the seal is visible on some constructions, that it is hard and slightly glossy, that it can crack if the ribbon is subsequently bent sharply or stored under pressure for long periods, and that it prevents any later edge treatment such as sewing or overlocking. It also cannot be inspected for the internal condition of the fibre, because the seal hides it.

Can edge problems be caused by winding and storage rather than by the ribbon itself?

Frequently, yes. Winding under excess tension concentrates pressure on the edges, which are the stiffest part of the ribbon section, and can put a set into the edge that only becomes visible weeks later when the ribbon is pulled from the roll and bent. Core diameter, winding tension, roll length, storage orientation and ambient conditions all contribute. If your programme holds ribbon as stock for months before use, test the aged condition rather than the freshly delivered one - the two can behave very differently.

How much does edge construction affect drape and hand feel?

More than buyers expect, particularly with heavier treatments. An overlock adds thread at the edge, which increases edge stiffness and thickness slightly and makes the ribbon feel marginally crisper along its length. A heat seal adds a hard line. A narrow or tightly wound core changes the roll profile and can introduce a slight curl at the edges, which changes how the ribbon falls. Where drape is a design driver - fashion, bridal, gift wrap - specify the edge treatment with the same care as the face fabric and require a physical sample to be approved.

What is the most useful thing I can ask a ribbon supplier about edge quality?

Ask them to send a physical length of the finished edge so you can inspect the construction, the yarn, the treatment and the finish directly, and ask what they measure internally when they investigate a rejected edge. A supplier with a defined measurement routine can tell you where in the process the failure originates; one without will simply say the edge was acceptable at final inspection. The physical sample is worth more than any specification discussion, and it takes minutes to send.

Should edge construction be a critical characteristic in our specification?

If the edge is visible, is going to be tied, knotted, sewn, folded under pressure or fed through equipment, then yes - it is a critical characteristic and it should be stated as one, with its own acceptance criteria and its own sampling regime. If the ribbon is a narrow decorative accent with the edge entirely hidden, a critical characterisation may be unnecessary, though it is still worth stating the construction so that a future substitution cannot quietly change it. The important discipline is deciding which category a property falls into deliberately, rather than letting it default to unstated.
