The metre price is the only figure most buyers compare, and it is the least informative of the four.

The four costs hiding behind one metre price

When a buyer compares two ribbon suppliers on unit price, they are comparing one of four costs and ignoring the other three. This is not a criticism of buyers — it is how the request arrives, because the request arrives as "what is your price per metre". But a unit price that ignores packing, freight, duty and yield is not a comparable number, and choosing on it can select the more expensive option by a wide margin.

The four components are:

The mill price. The cost of the ribbon itself as it leaves the factory floor, which may or may not include the packaging you asked for, and which may or may not reflect the specific width, construction and finish you actually need.

The yield loss. Not every metre produced becomes usable product. Ribbon that comes off a loom with an intermittent fault, ribbon that fails a colour tolerance, ribbon damaged in handling, and ribbon that is produced in a width you cannot use all of are all real costs that never appear as a line item but quietly inflate the cost of the metres you do use.

The packing and handling cost. How the ribbon is made into a shippable unit, and how many units fit in a carton. This is where the density question lives, and it is more consequential than most buyers assume.

The logistics and duty cost. Freight, insurance, customs clearance and duty, which scale with the volume shipped and the volume of space consumed rather than with the metres themselves.

The rest of this guide works through these four in turn, with particular attention to packing density, which is the component that most often changes the answer.

Component one: the mill price and what is actually inside it

A mill price is only comparable when it covers the same thing. The differences that quietly move the number are worth listing.

Width and construction. A price per metre for 10mm single-faced satin is not comparable to a price per metre for 38mm double-faced grosgrain, because the second consumes far more fabric per metre of product. Width changes material consumption roughly in proportion, and construction changes it again. A quotation that does not state the width and construction in full is not a quotation.

Finish and packaging inclusions. Does the price include winding onto your specified spool size, or onto the mill's standard spool? Does it include polybags, cartons, and printed labels? A price excluding packaging is a price that will be added to later by a packaging supplier, often less efficiently.

Dye lot and colour matching. If the order requires a lab dip approval and a production shade match to an earlier lot, that work is part of the cost and should be stated as included. A supplier quoting a spot colour directly from a shade card without a lab dip step is quoting a lower price for a lower probability of matching.

Whether the price includes set-up charges. Die costs, plate charges, printing set-up and new colour development are sometimes bundled into the unit price and sometimes invoiced separately. A supplier quoting a lower unit price with a high one-off set-up may be the better option for a long programme and the worse one for a single order. Ask for both numbers explicitly: unit price, and one-off charges, and the minimum quantity at which the set-up is amortised.

Component two: yield loss and the true usable metre

Yield is the most commonly ignored component because it is invisible until it is measured, and it is often larger than buyers expect. There are three distinct sources.

Length variation. Ribbon is manufactured in continuous lengths determined by the loom and the dye batch. A reel may be 200 metres, or 240, or 180. If your cutting room needs specific lengths for a particular product, the tail of each reel becomes leftover that you have paid for. With reels averaging a few hundred metres and your requirement being, say, 12 metres, the arithmetic of this waste is significant, and it is entirely a function of the relationship between reel length and your cutting pattern.

Width waste. If you order 38mm ribbon and your product needs 30mm, you have paid for 8mm of ribbon per metre that becomes trim. Sometimes that trim is recoverable as a narrow accessory or as a lace, sometimes it is scrap. Whether it is recoverable depends on the construction: continuous woven goods can often be recut, whereas printed or heavily finished ribbon generally cannot.

Defective length. Faults, shade variation across a dye batch, creasing and transit damage all remove usable metres. A supplier that inspects and sorts before shipment reduces your loss; a supplier that ships as produced passes the loss to you. This is a specification item, not a supplier-character item, and it belongs in the order.

How to make yield visible. Ask the supplier for the standard reel length for your specification, and compare it against your cutting pattern. Then ask what the historical defect or reject rate has been for that construction and width, expressed as a percentage of metres produced. A supplier that gives you a number has a system. Ask also whether width is cut to your dimension or whether you order the standard mill width, because the answer determines whether the trim is avoidable.

Component three: packing density, the component most buyers get wrong

This is where most of the hidden cost in ribbon sourcing sits, and it is a logistics problem rather than a price problem. A carton of ribbon is not a box of a fixed size with a fixed quantity in it; it is a volume filled according to how the ribbon is wound and what you asked the supplier to put in the carton.

Cartons are volumetric, and volume is what you pay for. Freight on an ocean container or an air shipment is charged by volume or weight, whichever is greater. Ribbon is relatively light, so volume almost always governs. A carton packed at 60% of its volumetric capacity costs the same to ship as one packed at 95%, and you pay for the difference in every shipment you will ever make. This is arithmetic, not negotiation, and it is entirely within your control.

The spool size decision has a large effect on density. Ribbon wound on a large spool occupies more physical volume per metre because of the core, the gaps and the winding structure. Small spools generally give better carton density, but they create handling overhead downstream and more units to pick and pack. The right answer depends on how the ribbon is used after it arrives: a ribbon that goes straight onto an automated cutting or winding line is often better on large spools, while ribbon that goes into manual assembly is often better on small spools that a person can handle one-handed. Ask the supplier to quote both, with carton dimensions and total carton count, and compare the resulting freight efficiency rather than the unit price.

Carton quantity and carton weight interact with freight limits. Cartons that exceed a maximum weight create a handling problem and sometimes an additional surcharge, and cartons that are too small increase the number of pieces to handle at both ends. A supplier who already packs to a sensible carton specification is worth something, and a supplier who will pack to your specification when you provide it is worth more.

The packaging material itself has a landed cost. Corrugated cartons have a freight cost of their own, they occupy cube, and in some markets they are subject to extended producer responsibility obligations that appear as a cost on the import declaration. Plastic film and polybags add their own weight and waste. Ask what packaging is used, what the carton dimensions are, and how many cartons a standard production quantity occupies. Then calculate freight per metre and see whether a modest change in packing instruction materially improves it.

Component four: freight, duty and the landed cost calculation

With the mill price, the yield and the packing understood, the landed cost follows. The method matters more than the inputs.

Build the comparison on a common unit. The most useful denominator for a ribbon programme is usually the finished unit that uses the ribbon, not the metre. A metre of ribbon feeding into a finished product should be compared against the total landed cost of that finished product. This keeps the packaging and logistics effort proportionate to the value of what you are making.

Remember that freight has a fixed and a variable component. A small shipment carries the same fixed costs — origin handling, documentation, customs entry, destination drayage — as a large one, distributed over fewer metres. This is why per-metre freight cost falls steeply with shipment size, and it means the optimal order size is a function of the freight curve rather than of the ribbon. Ask the supplier for the freight cost structure at your realistic volume and at a doubled volume, and check whether the incremental volume actually earns a meaningful per-unit improvement. Suppliers who are willing to model this are doing useful work for you.

Duty is a per-unit cost that changes with classification and origin. Treat it as part of the landed cost from the beginning rather than as something discovered at the border, and confirm the classification and origin with the supplier in writing before the first order.

Include the cost of variability. A shipment that arrives two weeks late because of a packing instruction error costs more than the freight saved by optimising the carton. Optimise packing, but not to the point where the goods are damaged in transit or the supplier is cutting corners on how they are secured.

How to run the comparison properly

Once the components are understood, the comparison is straightforward, and it is worth doing it in writing because the conclusions are frequently counter-intuitive relative to the initial price ranking.

Get a written quotation with all components separated. Unit price, one-off charges, packing specification, carton dimensions and count, standard reel length, and defect or reject expectations. A quotation that does not separate these cannot be compared, and comparing them is where the savings are.

Normalise the quantities. Compare on the same finished quantity, the same width and the same construction. Comparing a quotation for 5,000 metres against a quotation for 10,000 metres without normalising produces a false result that usually favours the larger order for reasons that have nothing to do with your programme.

Add the internal costs you are also changing. If the better packing option means more cartons to receive and inspect, or a different spool size that does not suit your downstream line, factor that in. The most cost-effective option is the one that works with your operation, and this is the point where a purely spreadsheet-led comparison falls apart.

Model two or three shipment sizes rather than one. The relationship between order size and landed cost is not linear, and a single point comparison can pick the wrong answer. Model a smaller trial order, your normal running order, and a peak-season order, and look at the total cost across the year rather than the cost of any single shipment.

What changes the answer most

Across the programmes we quote, three adjustments consistently move the landed cost more than anything else, and none of them involve negotiating a lower mill price.

Ordering the correct width rather than the nearest standard width. If a standard mill width is wider than you need, every metre carries trim. Ordering the exact width, or choosing a construction where the standard width matches, removes that cost entirely. It may mean a different price per metre, and the net is usually strongly in your favour.

Matching reel length to the cutting pattern. This is a purely arithmetic adjustment and it requires no negotiation at all. Ask for the reel length, measure it against your pattern, and ask whether a different length is available. The reduction in tail waste is frequently larger than any discount you could negotiate.

Specifying carton fill and carton count. A buyer who tells the supplier the target carton dimensions and the maximum gross weight gets a better freight outcome than one who leaves it to default packaging. This is a two-line change to the order with a measurable effect on every future shipment.

Each of these costs nothing in negotiation terms. They are specification decisions, and they are the reason that the cheapest quoted metre price is frequently not the cheapest delivered.

Questions that make the quotation comparable

Ask: what is the unit price at my specification and volume; what one-off charges apply and at what quantity do they cease to apply; what is the standard reel length for this construction; is the width supplied at my dimension or at the nearest standard mill width; what are the carton dimensions, the net and gross weight, and the number of cartons for my quantity; what is the historical defect or reject rate for this specification; how does the freight cost per metre change at double my order quantity; and what is the shipping weight for customs and duty purposes. A supplier who answers all seven precisely has a costing system, and their quotation will hold up when the goods arrive.

Frequently Asked Questions

How should a ribbon buyer compare two supplier quotations fairly?

Compare on the same specification, the same finished quantity and the same delivered basis. The comparable figure is the landed cost per usable metre, which combines the unit price, the yield loss, the packing and freight cost and the duty, rather than the quoted metre price alone. A quoted price that excludes packaging, is based on a different reel length or a wider mill width than you need, or applies set-up charges that change with volume, is not comparable to one that includes them.

Why does ribbon packaging affect the price so much?

Because freight on light goods is charged by volume rather than weight, and an under-filled carton costs the same to ship as a full one. Carton fill ratio, spool size and the number of cartons per shipment therefore change the freight cost per metre without changing anything about the ribbon. This is a specification decision rather than a negotiation: telling the supplier your target carton dimensions and maximum gross weight typically produces a better result than negotiating a lower mill price.

How much ribbon is lost to production waste and unusable lengths?

It depends on reel length, width and defect rate, and it is usually larger than buyers assume. The main sources are tail waste on each reel relative to your cutting pattern, trim where you order a wider mill width than you need, and metres removed by faults or shade variation. The remedy is arithmetic: ask for the standard reel length for your specification and compare it against your cutting pattern, then ask whether the exact width you need can be produced rather than the nearest standard width.

Does a larger order always reduce the landed cost per metre?

Not always in proportion. Freight has a substantial fixed component covering origin handling, documentation, customs entry and destination drayage, so per-metre cost does fall as volume rises, but the improvement is not linear and it diminishes at larger volumes. The right comparison is to model two or three realistic shipment sizes and look at total annual cost, because a single point comparison can select an order size that suits the freight curve but not your cash flow or your storage.

What one-off charges should I ask about in a ribbon quotation?

Ask about die and tooling costs, printing plate or screen charges, colour and lab dip development, and any charge for custom labelling or packaging, and ask separately for the unit price so the amortisation is visible. A supplier quoting a higher unit price with a modest one-off charge is often better value on a long programme than one quoting a lower unit price with a large set-up cost. For short programmes, prefer the supplier with no set-up charge at all.

Should I specify the carton dimensions, or leave packaging to the supplier?

Specify them, at least for your main programmes. A supplier's default carton specification reflects their shipping habits rather than your shipment size and handling arrangements. Giving a target carton dimension, a maximum gross weight and the number of cartons you want gives you a better freight outcome and a shipment that is easier to receive. Leave the internal packing material and labelling details to the supplier unless your quality system requires otherwise.

Does defect rate really vary enough between ribbon suppliers to affect the comparison?

Yes, and it varies more than most buyers expect, particularly across widths, constructions and dye batches. The difference between a mill that inspects and sorts before shipment and one that ships as produced is the difference between your receiving team sorting through the delivery and not doing so. Ask each supplier for their historical reject rate for your specific construction and width as a percentage of metres produced, and treat a supplier who cannot supply that figure as a supplier whose rate is unknown.

How do I make sure the quotation I receive reflects the ribbon I actually need?

Put the full specification into the request: material, construction, width, finished width, colour reference, printing method, edge treatment, spool size, packaging, carton quantity and the delivery terms. Then request the unit price, the one-off charges, the reel length, the carton dimensions and count, and the freight effect at your volume as separate stated items. A quotation that returns only a metre price has not engaged with the question, and it will not hold up at the point where the shipment arrives.
