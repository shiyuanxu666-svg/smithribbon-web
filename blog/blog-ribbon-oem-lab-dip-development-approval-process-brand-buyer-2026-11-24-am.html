Why the lab dip stage decides the economics of the order

A ribbon order is priced on the specification you approve, and the specification is set at the lab dip stage, not at the purchase order stage. Once a buyer signs off a colour, a width, a construction and a finish, every price quoted afterwards is a price for that exact specification. Everything negotiated afterwards is about volume, delivery and payment. This is why the development stage carries more commercial weight than most buyers give it: a rushed or loosely specified lab dip does not save time, it locks in cost.

The failure pattern is consistent across the industry. A buyer supplies a hex code or a photograph, the mill produces a sample, the sample is approved over email, and three production runs later the bulk ribbon does not match what was approved. The dispute that follows is rarely about colour theory. It is about the fact that the approval was made against an uncontrolled reference, with no specification of illuminant, no defined tolerance band, and no record of which physical sample the approval was actually granted against.

This guide covers the development sequence in the order it actually happens: colour brief, lab dip, physical sample, bulk approval and pre-production gate. It is written for brand buyers and product developers who need a process they can hand to a supplier and trust, rather than a sequence of informal approvals that nobody can reconstruct a year later.

The colour brief: what to specify before anything is dyed

The quality of the lab dip is bounded by the quality of the brief. A mill can only match what it has been told, and the most common reason a lab dip misses is that the brief was ambiguous rather than that the dyeing was wrong. A usable brief is short and specific. It should state the base material and construction, the colour reference and how it is to be interpreted, the finish, the width, the quantity to be developed, and the acceptance criteria.

Material and construction come first. The same colour reference behaves differently on polyester satin, on velvet, on grosgrain and on organza, because each has a different surface, different light scatter and different dye affinity. A Pantone reference is a numerical description of a colour under a defined viewing condition; it is not a description of how a particular ribbon should look. Brief the colour against the substrate that will actually be used.

Reference standards need an interpretation rule. Say whether the reference is a physical swatch, a printed card, a photograph on screen, or a colour reference number, and say which viewing condition applies. A photograph is the weakest option by a wide margin: every screen, every phone and every compression pipeline shifts colour, and the buyer's own device is often the only one the approval will be judged on. Where a physical reference exists, ask the mill to return the sample in a clear pouch with the reference enclosed so the comparison happens side by side rather than from memory.

Acceptance criteria beat adjectives. Terms like 'matching' or 'close to' leave the decision entirely to whoever looks at the swatches next. The useful version is a tolerance that both sides agree on in advance, plus the reference environment in which that tolerance is judged. That is the subject of a separate section below, because the mechanics matter and they are not what most buyers expect.

Lab dips: the small-batch dye trial that produces your reference

A lab dip is a small quantity of ribbon dyed to the brief, produced on production-equipment settings where possible, so that the colour can be judged before bulk quantities are committed. It is the point where the mill demonstrates what the specification actually produces, rather than what it should produce in theory. It is also the point where a competent mill will push back, and that pushback is a signal worth listening to.

Expect the mill to ask for a physical reference. If the brief is a photograph or a screen image, a good supplier will ask for something physical before running the dip. That request is not pedantry. Matching a screen image is genuinely not possible to a controlled standard, and a mill that quietly accepts a photograph-based brief and then reports a 90% match is telling you something about how it handles the rest of your programme.

Ask what changed between the dip and production. A good lab dip is produced on the same yarn lot family, the same dye house and, ideally, the same dyeing line as the bulk run. The most frequent causes of a lab dip to bulk mismatch are changes in yarn supplier, dye house, machine, or the addition of optical brighteners and softeners that were not in the brief. Asking directly which of those are held constant gives you a clear answer about how much confidence the dip deserves.

Order enough to test, not enough to impress. Lab dip quantities are typically measured in metres rather than the hundreds of metres of a bulk order, and there is no value in ordering a dip large enough to be a finished ribbon on its own. The useful quantity is enough to assess colour, hand, width, edge quality and printability, and enough for the buyer to retain a signed-off reference for years. That retained sample is the single most useful artefact in the whole development process, and skipping it is the most expensive shortcut available.

Treat the lab dip as a specification test, not a colour test. The dip is also the first opportunity to confirm construction. Is the selvedge clean? Does the weave match the stated construction? Does the ribbon lie flat or curl? Is the width correct within tolerance, and does it behave consistently on both edges? All of these are much cheaper to change at dip stage than after 5,000 metres have been dyed.

Physical samples and the strike-off

A lab dip confirms the colour direction. A physical sample confirms the finished product. The distinction matters because the two answer different questions, and approving only the dip leaves a gap that bulk production routinely exposes.

The sample that a buyer should work with is the strike-off: a length of ribbon finished to the full specification, including the edge treatment, the finish, the printed artwork if the item is printed, and the final packaging format. A strike-off is what the buyer should take to a product review, to a fit assessment on the finished good, or to a customer presentation. It is also what a quality team should hold as the reference against which the bulk is measured.

Print and artwork need their own approval gate. For printed ribbon, the printed strike-off introduces a second variable that a plain lab dip cannot address: how the artwork sits on the substrate. Print registration, colour saturation on the specific weave, edge placement, repeat length and the readability of fine detail all change between a drawing and a printed ribbon. Where the artwork has fine detail, metallic elements or a logo with tight tolerances, the approval should reference a printed sample explicitly, not the plain colour dip that preceded it.

Check behaviour, not just appearance. A ribbon that is approved on the table can still fail in use. Depending on the application, that may mean testing curl recovery over a period of time, drape, knot-holding strength, surface friction against adjacent materials, wash or rub fastness, or compatibility with the packaging film or box it will be presented in. A ribbon used as a decorative bow on a gift box behaves very differently from the same ribbon used as a hair tie or a pet accessory loop, and the sample should be tested in the condition it will actually ship in.

Approval: converting a good-looking sample into a controlled specification

This is where most development processes fail, and the failure is a documentation failure rather than a technical one. An approval that records only 'looks good' leaves three questions open: approved against which physical sample, judged under what viewing conditions, and acceptable within what tolerance. Those three answers are what a quality dispute a year later will be settled on.

Reference the physical sample by identifier. The approval document should cite a sample number, a batch or lot reference and a date, and the buyer should retain a length of that exact approved sample. A supplier who holds the sample understands that production will be measured against it, and it removes the possibility of the parties arguing about what was approved when no physical record existed.

Set the viewing condition. Colour assessment is only meaningful under a defined light source and against a defined surround. A mill's QC room, a buyer's office with warm afternoon light and a warehouse under fluorescent fittings can read differently from one another, and a colour approved under one and produced under another is the classic start of a dispute. Agreeing a single reference environment, written into the approval, removes most of that ambiguity.

Agree a tolerance in a form both sides can apply. Commercial tolerances are often expressed using a recognised colour-difference method, which gives a numerical result that two people in different rooms can agree on rather than arguing about whether two swatches 'match'. The important part is that the tolerance is agreed before approval, in writing, and that it states the conditions under which the measurement is made. Where the buyer has no measurement capability, the practical fallback is a defined visual standard with a fixed lighting condition and a documented rule for who decides.

Record what was explicitly excluded. Approvals routinely carry implicit exceptions: this batch's shade is acceptable but the striping is not, or the colour is approved for the main body only and the binding tape must still be matched separately. Writing the exclusions down prevents them from resurfacing as a rejection claim on the bulk shipment.

The pre-production gate: the last checkpoint before bulk dyeing

Between the approved sample and the bulk order there is a gate, and its purpose is to confirm that nothing has changed since approval. Most buyers know this gate exists in principle and skip it in practice. It is the point at which the order is confirmed, the price is confirmed, and the mill confirms that it is still able to produce to the approved specification.

Re-confirm the specification, not just the order. The order confirmation should restate the full specification: material, construction, colour reference with the approved sample identifier, width, quantity, finish, artwork version, and any tolerances agreed. Restating it serves a practical purpose — it catches the case where the buyer assumed a change that was never made and the mill assumed the original brief still stood.

Confirm the production parameters. Ask which yarn lot and which dye house will run the bulk, and whether any of them differ from the ones used for the dip or the strike-off. This is a reasonable question and a normal answer is reassuring: the same dye house, the same yarn family, the same line. If the answer involves a change, it is worth asking why and what the consequence for the approved colour is.

Schedule a pre-shipment inspection as standard. The inspection window is the only point at which a colour or construction deviation can still be corrected before the goods leave. It is far cheaper to hold a bulk run at the mill than to receive 5,000 metres of ribbon that is a shade off, because the remedy after shipment is limited to credit, replacement or rework, and each of those carries a schedule penalty that the cost of an inspection does not.

Agree the number of bulk pieces that constitute a lot. This is the unit that defect rates, tolerances and traceability are measured against, and it is often undefined in a purchase order. Defining it in advance removes a recurring source of confusion when a shipment arrives in several cartons or pallets and the buyer needs to know which metres belong to which lot.

Common ways the development process goes wrong

The mistakes below are not exotic. They appear in most programmes, they are individually easy to avoid, and together they account for a large share of the colour complaints and sample rework that buyers attribute to 'the factory'.

Approving from a photograph of a physical sample. A phone photograph of a ribbon loses the surface texture, shifts the colour, and introduces the phone's own processing. It is not a reference. If it must be circulated, it should be circulated alongside the physical sample, never instead of it.

Approving one ribbon and ordering a set. A set contains components that are dyed, woven, cut and assembled separately. The main ribbon and the binding tape are frequently produced from different yarn lots and different dye runs. Unless the set specification addresses matching across components, a set can arrive with a visibly mismatched bow and a body.

Approving a colour on the wrong substrate. A colour that reads correctly on a matte grosgrain will not read the same on a lustrous satin. Where a programme uses more than one material, the approval has to be made per material, because there is no single reference that governs all of them.

Treating the first bulk run as the trial. Bulk is the expensive trial. The whole purpose of the dip and the strike-off is to move the trial upstream to where the cost of being wrong is measured in metres rather than cartons.

Letting the development stage drift under time pressure. A launch date creates pressure to skip the sample gate. The cost of skipping it is a specification that cannot be enforced later, which is a considerably more expensive problem than a delayed launch of one week.

What to ask a ribbon supplier before you start

A short set of questions, asked before development begins, tells you more about how a supplier will handle your programme than a long conversation about capability usually does.

Can you show me a lab dip produced on the same dye house and yarn family that will run the bulk? This question separates suppliers who have a repeatable process from suppliers who have a good colour library and a lucky stock.

How do you record approvals, and can I get a physical sample back of the approved lot? The answer should describe a retained sample with an identifier, not a photograph in a chat thread.

What is your normal lab dip and strike-off turnaround, and what does an extra round cost? Expect more than one round for a new colour. A supplier who guarantees one round is either not testing properly or not allowing for the feedback loop that makes the approval mean anything.

Which tolerances and which colour-difference method do you work to, and can we agree them in the order? A supplier with no answer to this is working to 'it looked right' — and so is every dispute that follows.

Do you run pre-shipment inspection, and at what stage relative to the production start? This is also the moment to ask about production capacity and the realistic lead time for the first bulk run, which is usually longer than the quoted figure because the material and dye house slot must also be secured.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a ribbon lab dip and how is it different from a bulk sample?

A lab dip is a short length of ribbon dyed to your brief on production-equipment settings, used to confirm colour direction, construction and finish before bulk quantities are committed. A bulk sample is a length of the finished product produced on the same line as the order. The lab dip is the trial; the bulk sample is the reference the bulk shipment is judged against. Both are useful, and they answer different questions.

How long does a ribbon lab dip and sample approval cycle usually take?

For an established colour on a standard construction, a lab dip is typically a matter of several working days and a physical sample follows shortly after. A genuinely new colour, a new construction or a printed design with fine artwork usually needs more than one development round, because the first round is what reveals whether the brief was precise enough. Any supplier promising a single round for new development is not allowing for the feedback loop.

Can I approve a ribbon colour from a Pantone number or a photograph?

A Pantone reference describes a colour under a defined viewing condition but does not describe how a specific substrate will render it, so it works as a starting point rather than as a specification. A photograph is weaker still, because screens and phone processing shift colour, and the buyer's own device is often the only one the approval will be judged on. For a controlled result, supply a physical swatch and have the approval made against a physical sample under a defined light source.

What colour tolerance should a ribbon brand buyer specify?

Specify a tolerance expressed as a numerical colour-difference value with the measuring method named, together with the viewing or measurement conditions it applies under, and agree it before approval rather than after a dispute. Where the buyer has no measurement capability, the practical alternative is a defined visual standard with a fixed lighting condition and a documented rule for who makes the call. The important point is that both sides apply the same rule.

Why does bulk ribbon sometimes not match the approved lab dip sample?

The usual causes are changes made between the dip and the bulk run: a different yarn lot or supplier, a different dye house or machine, or softeners, optical brighteners or finishing treatments that were not specified in the brief. This is why the pre-production confirmation should ask which yarn lot and dye house will run the bulk and whether they differ from the development stage, and why a mill that answers that question with a clear, reassuring answer is generally the safer supplier.

How many lab dip rounds should I expect before approval?

For a new colour developed from a physical reference, one or two rounds is normal, with the second round usually confirming a correction. More rounds than that usually indicates the brief is under-specified rather than that the mill is inefficient, so it is worth revisiting the brief before approving further rounds. Time pressure at this stage is the main reason specifications get locked in without a real reference.

What should be included in a ribbon sample approval record?

At minimum: the physical sample identifier and lot reference, the date, the full specification including material, construction, colour reference, width and finish, the artwork version where the ribbon is printed, the viewing condition or measurement method used, the agreed tolerance, and any explicit exclusions from the approval. The buyer should also retain a length of the approved physical sample, because that artefact is what any later dispute is settled against.

What is the minimum sample a ribbon brand buyer should order for evaluation?

Enough to assess colour, hand, width, edge quality and printability, plus enough to retain a signed-off reference for the life of the programme. That is typically measured in metres rather than the hundreds of metres of a bulk order. For printed ribbon, order a printed strike-off at full specification, because artwork registration, detail readability and edge placement cannot be assessed from a plain colour dip.
