Buying Ribbon Overseas: Payment Terms, Incoterms and Contract Terms

Published: 2026-11-23  |  Updated: 2026-11-23  |  By Xiamen Smith Ribbon & Bow Co., Ltd.  |  Since 2004

Why the commercial terms matter: a ribbon quotation tells you what the goods cost. It says almost nothing about what happens if the goods are late, the wrong colour, or never arrive. The terms that decide who carries that risk are the payment terms, the Incoterm and the clauses in the supply contract — and for most buyers, these are the least negotiated documents in the entire relationship.

Experienced sourcing teams know that the technical work is rarely what causes a ribbon dispute. Colour tolerance arguments happen, but the expensive conversations are commercial: who pays for a second shipment when the first was non-conforming, who owns the goods while they sit on a vessel, how long a supplier can sit on an undisputed invoice, and what happens to the tooling that was paid for when the order is cancelled.

This guide covers the three documents that together govern the commercial side of overseas ribbon sourcing: the payment terms, the Incoterm, and the supply contract. It is written for the buyer who wants to build a relationship that survives a difficult quarter rather than squeeze the last few points of price in a comfortable one.

Payment terms: what you are really negotiating

Payment terms are usually presented as a schedule — 30% deposit, 70% against copy of bill of lading — and treated as a fixed condition of sale. For a buyer with a genuinely established relationship, that is unnecessarily rigid. For a buyer placing a first order, it is probably the safest available structure. The important thing is to understand what each element is actually doing.

The deposit. A deposit does three jobs. It covers the mill’s material commitment, it proves the buyer is serious, and it places the buyer at the front of the mill’s queue. It is also the buyer’s single largest unsecured exposure, because it is the only money that has moved before the buyer has received anything. On a first order that money is genuinely at risk and should be treated that way. On a fourth order with a supplier the buyer has audited, the deposit is closer to a scheduling device than a payment.

Balance against documents. Paying the balance against a copy of the bill of lading is the classic structure and it works because the document proves the goods were loaded, not that they arrived. That gap is why the Incoterm and the inspection clause have to be right: the bill of lading confirms shipment, while your inspection rights are what confirm acceptance. A buyer who pays against documents should make sure they have appointed a third-party pre-shipment inspection at origin, because after the bill of lading is issued the buyer’s practical ability to refuse goods has effectively disappeared.

Credit terms. Open account terms — paying thirty or sixty days after invoice — are the mark of a mature, trusted relationship and they are also the mechanism by which a supplier’s financial stress becomes your problem. Open credit is a real service and it is worth paying for, but it should be earned rather than granted at the first quotation, and it should sit in a contract that specifies what happens if the supplier is late: a stated interest rate on overdue sums, and a right to suspend production on future orders.

Currency. Quote and settle in a single currency, and be aware that a contract in a currency other than the one you pay in transfers exchange rate movement into a cost or a windfall that has nothing to do with ribbons. Where the invoice currency and the payment currency differ, say explicitly who bears the conversion cost and at which date the rate is fixed. This sounds like a small clause and it is not, because over a year of monthly shipments it is a recurring line item.

Bank charges and documentary compliance. The cost of a transmission is small against the cost of a rejected documentary credit. State who bears bank charges, and more importantly state what happens if the documents the mill prepares do not match what the bank requires. Document discrepancies are the most common and most avoidable cause of delayed payment in cross-border trade, and they are almost always a drafting failure rather than a commercial dispute.

Incoterms: choosing who carries risk, not who appears cheapest

The Incoterm is the single most consequential and most misunderstood line in a ribbon purchase order. It does not set a price — it allocates costs and, far more importantly, it allocates risk at each point in the delivery. The wrong Incoterm does not just cost money; it changes who bears the consequences when something breaks, and the consequences can be considerable.

EXW — Ex Works. The buyer takes responsibility from the factory gate. The buyer arranges export clearance, collection, loading and international carriage. On paper it is the lowest-price term because the supplier quotes ex-factory. In practice it is rarely the cheapest, because the buyer now must nominate a forwarder who can collect from a mill in another province, complete export documentation correctly, and handle a consignment the buyer has limited visibility of. It also puts the buyer in a weak position on the mill’s production readiness: the goods are ready when the buyer’s truck eventually arrives, which may be days after the agreed production completion date. EXW makes sense for a buyer with its own export operation and an established presence in the region. For most first-time buyers, FOB is materially easier to control.

FOB — Free On Board. The seller handles export clearance and loading onto the vessel, and risk transfers once the goods are on board. The buyer takes the ocean transit, insurance, customs clearance on arrival and inland delivery. This is the most common structure in ribbon sourcing and it is a reasonable default. The important detail is the port: FOB is defined by the named port of shipment, and the buyer should confirm whether the mill’s port is a direct sailing port or requires transhipment, because a transshipment leg adds time and a failure point. Also state clearly who arranges export customs clearance. The word “FOB” on an order is sometimes used to cover both origin and destination costs by custom, which is a different arrangement under a different Incoterm and should be named explicitly.

CIF — Cost, Insurance and Freight. The seller pays freight and insurance to the named destination port. Risk still transfers at the point of shipment, so the buyer gets a delivered-to-port price without taking the risk during transit. This is useful where the buyer wants cost certainty on the freight leg, and it is worth remembering that the seller’s insurance is typically a minimum cover, so a buyer with high-value goods should specify the required cover explicitly rather than assume.

DDP — Delivered Duty Paid. The seller carries everything to the buyer’s door, including duty and clearance. It is convenient and removes a great deal of administrative work from the buyer. It is also frequently the most expensive term, because the seller builds the duty, the clearance and the inland leg into the unit price, and because the seller must be confident in its own classification and valuation. Where DDP does make sense, agree in writing how the duty and any taxes are to be treated if the authorities reclassify the goods after delivery — because that risk does not disappear simply because the invoice said DDP.

A useful test when comparing quotations: for each term, ask who pays the transport, who pays the duty, who insures the goods, and who carries the risk if something is lost or damaged. Two quotations that look identical on unit price can sit on entirely different risk positions, and the one that appears cheaper may simply have moved risk onto you.

What a ribbon supply contract should actually cover

A purchase order is an instruction to buy. A supply contract is a framework that governs what happens when the instruction is not straightforward. The difference between the two documents is where most of the protection in a long-term ribbon relationship lives, and many buyers operate for years with nothing more than a purchase order and a friendly email thread.

Specification and the order of precedence. Attach the approved specification, colour standard and construction details, and state which document governs when they disagree — the purchase order, the contract, the specification sheet or the approved sample. Most ribbon disputes are not really about quality or money; they are about which document was supposed to govern, and naming the precedence order in advance removes an entire category of argument.

Delivery terms. State the delivery window, the Incoterm and named port or place, the routing requirement, the latest acceptable date and the remedy for delay. Include a clause on partial shipments — whether they are permitted, how they are invoiced, and who bears the additional freight. Without that clause, a supplier who splits a shipment in the middle of a tight launch can create a serious problem for the buyer who assumed one delivery of one quantity would arrive together.

Inspection and acceptance. State the inspection standard and level, who performs inspection and where, the timing relative to shipment, and the period during which the buyer may inspect and reject non-conforming goods after arrival. Critically, state the consequence of rejection: replacement, credit, refund or rework, and who pays the freight in each case. This clause is what converts a quality complaint into a remedy rather than a conversation.

Tooling and intellectual property. If the buyer pays for moulds, dies, plates or bespoke artwork, the contract should say clearly that these are paid-for assets, should identify them, and should state what happens to them if the programme ends or the relationship terminates. The supplier should also warrant that goods will not infringe third-party rights, and the buyer should warrant that any artwork, logo or design it supplies is legitimate to reproduce. These clauses are routine in tooling-intensive industries and their absence is a common source of late conflict.

Confidentiality and exclusivity. If the supplier will produce goods that only the buyer can sell — a branded print, a customer-specific colour — consider whether the buyer wants exclusivity and, if so, over what territory and for how long. Conversely, if the supplier will also make similar goods for a competitor, the buyer may prefer to know that in advance. Both parties are better served by an explicit term than by an assumption.

Compliance and traceability. Require compliance with the stated material and chemical standards, a duty to notify any change in raw material supplier, dye house, production site or process, and the obligation to retain batch records and make them available for audit. Many buyers’ own compliance obligations run to their suppliers, and they cannot discharge them if the contract does not give them the access and the notice they need.

Force majeure, liability and dispute resolution. State how force majeure is defined, how long it may last before either party may terminate, and what happens to deposits already paid for work not performed. Cap liability where appropriate and identify what is carved out. Finally, choose the governing law and the dispute forum — and choose it knowing that an enforcement clause in a jurisdiction where the supplier has no assets is not worth very much. An arbitration clause with an institution that both parties can access is usually a more practical protection than litigation in a distant court, and it is considerably cheaper than it sounds because it avoids a jurisdictional fight first.

Structuring the relationship so problems stay solvable

Most ribbon supply relationships are sound and most disputes are resolvable by people who can talk to each other. The purpose of formalising these terms is not distrust; it is to make sure that when a relationship is under pressure — and every long relationship eventually is — there is a document both sides can point to, and neither side has to remember precisely what was agreed eight months ago.

Build a documented order acknowledgement. A disciplined order acknowledgement that restates the specification, the quantity, the price, the Incoterm, the delivery window and the payment terms prevents the majority of disputes before they start, because it gives both sides a chance to catch a mistake while it is still cheap to fix.

Keep a single channel for order communication. Not because email should be abolished, but because a supplier whose floor team, merchandiser and sales manager all read the same thread reaches decisions faster than one where each of them has a different version of the order.

Escalate deliberately, and in writing. When an issue is not resolved, move it up a level with a summary of the facts, the remedy requested, and a response deadline. Keep the written record. Most escalations are resolved not because the higher level knows something new, but because the specificity of the request forces a decision.

Never use payment as the only lever. Withholding payment on a disputed invoice is a powerful short-term lever and a poor long-term one, because it damages the trust on which every future discount, every priority slot and every goodwill concession depends. Where a genuine dispute exists, the right move is to pay the undisputed portion, state the disputed amount precisely, and settle it through the mechanism the contract provides.

Review the relationship periodically. A short annual review covering lead time performance, defect rates, document accuracy, responsiveness and any change in the supplier’s ownership or production capacity catches drift early. A supplier that has changed ownership, changed dye houses or changed production site is a materially different risk profile from the one you qualified, and the only reliable way to know is to ask.

Keep a second source for critical items. Not as a threat, but as insurance and as negotiating reality. A buyer who can credibly say that any programme can be moved is negotiating from a different position, and more importantly is protecting itself against a supplier failure that no contract clause can make harmless.

None of this is about distrusting a partner. It is about the fact that cross-border supply relationships fail for reasons that have nothing to do with anybody’s intentions — a factory fire, a port closure, a banking problem, a currency control, an insolvency — and the documents are what determine how much of that failure the buyer carries.

Frequently Asked Questions

What deposit terms are normal for ribbon OEM orders?

A common structure is 30% deposit with the balance against a copy of the bill of lading, though terms vary by order value, supplier and relationship history. The deposit covers the mill's material commitment and secures queue position, but it is also the buyer's largest unsecured exposure, so on a first order it should be treated as genuinely at risk rather than treated as routine.

Which Incoterm is best for importing ribbon from China?

FOB is the most common and usually the most manageable default, because the supplier handles export clearance and loading while the buyer controls the ocean leg and can nominate a forwarder. EXW offers the lowest quoted price but puts export clearance and collection on the buyer. CIF gives freight cost certainty. DDP is convenient but normally the most expensive. The right choice depends on the buyer's own logistics capability, not on which term produces the lowest headline price.

Who should arrange export customs clearance for ribbon shipments?

State it explicitly in the purchase order, because the terms overlap in practice. Under EXW the buyer is responsible; under FOB the seller is. Leaving it ambiguous is one of the most common causes of delayed shipments, since the cargo sits while the two parties work out who is supposed to be filing the export declaration.

What should a ribbon supply contract include?

At minimum: specification and order of precedence, delivery terms with the Incoterm and named port, inspection and acceptance periods with the remedy for rejection, payment terms and currency, tooling and intellectual property ownership, compliance and traceability with change notification, force majeure, liability, and governing law with a workable dispute resolution forum.

How should a supplier be required to handle a non-conforming ribbon shipment?

Define it in the contract rather than at the point of dispute: identify and quarantine the non-conforming goods, state who performs the assessment, specify whether the remedy is replacement, credit, refund or rework, and state who bears the return and replacement freight. A quality agreement with a clear corrective action process is the document that handles the underlying cause; the supply contract is what settles the commercial remedy.

Is open account credit common with ribbon suppliers?

It is common with established, high-volume relationships and less common on first orders. Open terms are valuable because they signal trust and often accompany better terms, but they also mean a supplier's financial difficulty becomes the buyer's problem. It is worth earning rather than granting immediately, and pairing it with a stated interest rate on overdue sums and a right to suspend production on future orders.

Who owns custom moulds, dies and printed plates paid for by the buyer?

The buyer should own them, and the contract should say so explicitly and identify them. Without an express clause, ownership of buyer-funded tooling can become genuinely unclear, and recovering a mould from a supplier who has moved on to another customer is far harder than specifying it at the outset. The contract should also cover what happens to the tooling if the programme is cancelled or the relationship ends.

What is the best way to resolve a dispute with an overseas ribbon supplier?

Start with a written escalation that states the facts, the remedy requested and a response deadline. Many disputes settle at this stage simply because specificity forces a decision. If the matter escalates, a contract clause naming an arbitration institution both parties can access is usually more practical than litigation in a distant jurisdiction, since it avoids a jurisdictional fight before the substantive issue is ever reached.
