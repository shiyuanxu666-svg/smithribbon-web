Ribbon Slitting and Width Conversion: A Yield Management Guide for Brand Buyers

Published: 2026-11-28  |  Updated: 2026-11-28  |  By Xiamen Smith Ribbon & Bow Co., Ltd.  |  Since 2004

Ask a ribbon buyer how much of their ribbon they end up using, and the honest answer is usually that they do not know. The quantity appears on the purchase order, the invoice, the carton label and the delivery note, and it is the same number in all four places. It is also frequently not the number of metres of usable ribbon in the box, because a meaningful proportion of what a mill counts is edge trim, setup waste or the difference between nominal and actual roll length. For a buyer paying by the metre, that difference is not an accounting abstraction; it is money.

This guide explains how ribbon width conversion actually works inside a mill: why wide webs are woven rather than narrow ones, what happens during slitting, where the losses come from, why yield varies so much between programmes, and which parts of the yield question a buyer can genuinely influence when the purchase order is written.

Why Mills Weave Wide and Sell Narrow

The economics of weaving are lumpy in a way that shapes everything about how ribbon is made. A loom has a fixed width, and it costs very little more to run that loom with a wide web than with a narrow one, because the dominant costs are the machine hours, the setup and the labour, not the width of the material being produced. A mill that weaves a 38 mm ribbon on its own equipment has to stop, rethread, reset the reed and restart for every single width in the programme, and each of those restarts costs an hour or more of machine time that produces no sellable ribbon at all.

So the industry has converged on a simple and rather elegant answer. The mill weaves a single wide web, often 1.4 m to 1.6 m across, which is close to the usable width of a modern wide-width loom. That web is then cut into narrower ribbons by a slitting machine. One weaving run can therefore yield many finished widths, and the mill can serve a buyer needing 10 mm, a buyer needing 25 mm and a buyer needing 50 mm from the same web in the same production run, at a fraction of the setup cost.

For the buyer this is good news on price and important context on yield. The yarn in a 25 mm ribbon sliced from a wide web is the same yarn, in the same dye lot, with the same colour, as the yarn in the 40 mm ribbon beside it on the same roll. There is no quality difference between two widths cut from the same web. The difference is entirely in how many metres of the web each width consumes and how much of each cut is edge.

The Slitting Process and Where the Ribbon Actually Goes

Slitting is the mechanical process of cutting a wide web lengthwise into narrower strips. The machine holds the web under tension and passes it beneath a set of circular blades positioned at the required intervals, so that in a single pass the wide web becomes several ribbons side by side. It is a fast process and, with a sharp blade and correct tension, a clean one.

Every mill's web is wider than the sum of the finished widths it is cutting, because each blade destroys a narrow band of material. A blade does not cut cleanly to a line; it consumes a small kerf of fibre along the cut. The practical loss at each cut is typically a few millimetres, and it multiplies across the width of the web rather than adding to it.

Beyond the kerf, there are two other losses that a buyer should understand because they are the ones that get argued about.

  • Edge trim. The outermost strips on each side of the web are usually not a finished width but a trim of a few centimetres each side, because the outermost edge of a wide web is not as straight, as dense or as square as the interior. Those strips are sold as a lower-grade product or scrapped as fibre waste.
  • Setup and start-up waste. The first few metres off any slitting run are not usable. The machine has to be threaded, the blade positions set and verified, the tension established and the web threaded through every guide and roller. During that time the ribbon is being produced, but it is not yet to specification: width may be off, edges may not be square and the tension may still be settling.

There is also a subtler loss that experienced buyers watch for, which is the ribbon cut from the edge of the web rather than from its interior. Edge ribbon can be slightly narrower than nominal, slightly more prone to fraying and, in a wide web, occasionally slightly off-squareness. Most mills manage this well, but it is worth confirming which positions in the slitting pattern your width occupies.

How to Calculate Yield, and What the Mill Is Counting

The calculation is straightforward once the terms are separated, and most disputes about yield come from a disagreement about which of two different numbers is being discussed.

The input is the length of the wide web consumed. The outputs are the finished lengths of each cut width, plus the trim and waste. The yield ratio is the sum of the finished lengths of one cut width, divided by the length of web consumed, expressed as a percentage. Where a web yields six widths and one is the buyer's width, that buyer's yield is the length of their own ribbon divided by the total web length consumed, not divided by the sum of all six outputs.

The two numbers that buyers mix up are net yield and commercial yield. Net yield is what comes off the machine and passes inspection. Commercial yield is what the buyer receives and can use. The gap between them is the part that matters commercially, and it includes not only trim and setup waste but also any portion of finished ribbon that does not meet the specification and is downgraded rather than rejected.

There is a third number worth asking for, which is yield by position. Because a slitting run produces several widths at once, the mill knows the yield for each position across the web. A programme that runs forty metres of 12 mm ribbon out of a web will have a different yield for each of the other widths in the pattern, and some of those positions will be running a lower-grade material. A mill that quotes yield as a single blended percentage across the whole web is telling you less than a mill that quotes it per position, and the blended number can flatter the programme considerably.

The Slittable Width Matrix

The most useful single artefact a buyer can request before placing a ribbon order is a slittable width matrix: a statement of which finished widths can be cut from the web the mill is running, and in what quantities, given the web width and the trim allowance.

The logic is straightforward but the consequences are not, and this is where a great deal of avoidable cost sits. A mill running a 1.4 m web with 40 mm of edge trim on each side has about 1.32 m of usable width. That 1.32 m can be divided between three 40 mm ribbons with room for two blades, or eleven 12 mm ribbons with room for ten blades, or a combination. The point is that the widths in a programme compete for the same 1.32 m, and a programme that includes a width the mill cannot fit into the pattern will either force a separate production run or force the mill to change the web width.

Two commercial consequences follow from this. First, a programme with many narrow widths and a programme with few wide widths may need to run on different webs, or on different machines, and the setup cost differs accordingly. Second, and more usefully, a programme can often be re-engineered to improve yield: choosing a width that fits the existing pattern, or shifting quantities between two widths that share a pattern, can reduce trim loss without changing the product at all. This is a question worth asking a mill before ordering, and it is one most buyers never ask because they do not know the question exists.

There is also a hard limit worth understanding: a mill cannot make a ribbon wider than its loom allows. For very wide ribbons, in the region above 100 mm and particularly above 150 mm, the web has to be woven in multiple widths and joined, which introduces a seam. A joined wide ribbon is a legitimate product but it is a different product, and a buyer should know before sampling whether the ribbon in front of them has a join in it.

Narrow Ribbon, Micro-Trim and the Limits of Slitting

Slitting works well down to a point and then stops working, and knowing where that point is saves a great deal of time in sampling.

Below roughly 10 mm the geometry becomes difficult. Each cut removes kerf from a narrow strip, so the strip nearest the blade is measurably narrower than nominal, and the tension required to hold a 6 mm strip flat and straight through the slitter is high enough that the strip distorts. At these widths many mills move to a different process entirely, weaving narrow ribbons directly on a narrow loom or using a special multi-track weaving arrangement, because the waste from slitting 6 mm strips out of a wide web is substantial enough to destroy the economics.

The practical rule of thumb is that below about 8 mm, a buyer should ask directly how the ribbon is made rather than assuming it is slit. The answer changes the yield calculation completely, and a buyer who assumes slitting and receives a directly woven narrow ribbon has been given the wrong mental model for everything from pricing to lead time.

There is also a middle band, roughly 10 mm to 20 mm, where slitting works technically but the trim loss per metre is proportionally high, because the kerf at each cut is a fixed width and the finished ribbon is narrow. If a programme sits in that band, it is worth asking whether a different width would materially improve the economics. That is a conversation about the product, not just the cost, and it should be framed that way.

What a Buyer Can Actually Control

Most of yield is determined by the mill's production decisions, but a buyer who understands the mechanics can influence a meaningful fraction of it. These are the levers that work.

  • Align widths to the pattern. Before the order, ask which widths share a slitting run. A programme designed around two or three widths in a single pattern will have a materially better yield than one with eight unrelated widths.
  • Check the width matrix before ordering. Confirm that every width in the programme can physically be cut from the web the mill intends to run, and what the trim allowance is. If a width forces a second run, that cost belongs in the quotation.
  • Order in roll lengths that suit the pattern. A programme that needs 800 m of one width and 60 m of another is a request for a pattern with a short run at the end, and the tail of a slitting run is where waste concentrates.
  • Decide early whether to take narrow web. Some buyers buy wide web and cut in-house. This shifts the work to the buyer and eliminates the mill's trim loss from the supplier's yield calculation, but it also transfers the blade, the operator and the defect risk. It is worth modelling honestly before choosing it.
  • Order quantity and width together. Total metres, width mix and delivery schedule interact. A programme that asks for 500 m of each of six widths across two deliveries will not slitting-run the way a programme asking for 3,000 m of the same six widths will.

What a buyer cannot usefully control is the mill's internal yield practice, its blade maintenance regime or how it allocates setup waste between programmes. Trying to manage those by specification tends to produce arguments rather than results. The correct question to ask is not how much waste the mill generates, but how much usable ribbon the buyer receives per order, and whether the quoted price reflects it.

How to Ask About Yield Without Getting a Sales Answer

The most common failure in a buyer-supplier conversation about yield is that the question is too general to produce a useful answer. Asking what percentage yield a programme achieves invites a number that has been optimised for the conversation. Specific questions get specific answers.

A buyer who wants real information should ask: what web width will this programme run; what is the edge trim allowance on each side; which finished widths will be cut in the same pass; what length of set-up waste applies to the run; how many slitting passes are needed for the full width mix; and what the net usable length per roll is after trim and set-up, expressed for the buyer's own width rather than as a blended average.

The last of those questions is the one that matters most and the one least often asked, because it asks the mill to report on the buyer's own position in the pattern rather than on the programme as a whole. A mill that answers it directly is a mill that has modelled the programme properly, and the answer is usually the single most useful number in the whole negotiation.

It is also worth asking how the mill handles the tail of a run. A buyer whose order sits in the middle of a pattern has a different position from one whose order closes the run, and the difference in waste is real. Where a programme is large enough to justify it, buyers increasingly ask for the yield to be quoted per order rather than per programme, which makes the number a contractual figure rather than an estimate.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does ribbon slitting actually mean in production terms?

Slitting is the process of cutting a wide woven web lengthwise into narrower finished ribbons using a slitting machine with circular blades. A mill typically weaves a single wide web, often between 1.4 and 1.6 metres, and slits it into every width required by the programme in one pass. The commercial reason is that loom setup time dominates cost for narrow products, so producing many widths from one wide run spreads that setup across all of them. The consequence for buyers is that several different widths in one order usually share a single weaving and dyeing run, which means they share a dye lot, a colour and a quality level.

Why does the width of my ribbon affect the price per metre?

Because the usable width available from a given web is finite and each cut consumes a small band of material. The narrower the finished ribbon, the more cuts are needed across the web, and the more the total trim and blade kerf takes as a proportion of the finished output. Narrow widths below roughly 8 millimetres are often produced on a narrow loom rather than slit, precisely because the slitting loss at that width would be disproportionate. Wider ribbons above a certain point are sometimes woven in multiple widths and joined, which introduces a seam. The effect is a non-linear price-to-width relationship, and a buyer who requests a width purely because it is a convenient round number can be paying materially more per metre than the width analysis would suggest.

How should I ask a supplier about trim loss and yield?

Ask for figures that describe your own position in the pattern rather than a programme-level average. Specifically: the web width that will be run, the edge trim allowance on each side, which finished widths will be cut in the same pass, the set-up waste for the run, how many slitting passes the width mix requires, and the net usable metres per roll for your width after trim and set-up. Ask the final question as a number rather than a percentage, because a percentage invites a blended figure that includes widths you are not buying. A supplier that answers these questions with specific numbers has modelled the programme, and the final figure is usually the most useful number in the negotiation.

Is it cheaper to buy wide ribbon and cut it myself?

Sometimes, and it is worth modelling honestly rather than assuming. Buying wide web and slitting in-house removes the mill's trim loss from the purchase price, and it allows widths to be cut to the buyer's own schedule rather than to the mill's pattern. Against that, the buyer takes on the blade cost and maintenance, the operator time, the need for the cutting equipment itself, and the risk that an in-house cut is less square or less straight than a mill cut. It also usually makes sense only where the buyer's volume is large enough to justify the equipment and the volumes are predictable enough to schedule. For most brand buyers at typical volumes, the mill's slitting run is the more economical option and the yield question is better handled at the specification stage.

What happens to yield when a programme has many different widths?

Yield generally falls as the number of distinct widths in a programme rises, because each additional width means additional blades, additional trim at the web edges and a greater probability that the width mix does not divide cleanly into the available web width. The effect is not simply additive, because the loss is concentrated at the edges and at the transitions, and it compounds with short runs. A programme of three widths sharing one pattern is a different economic proposition from a programme of nine widths, even where the total metreage is identical. The productive move for a buyer is to ask which widths genuinely share a run and to consider consolidating or adjusting quantities so that they do. That conversation usually saves more than negotiating unit price does.

Does ribbon width affect how a programme should be sampled?

It should, and it is commonly missed. Because a wide web is slit into several widths in one pass, a width cut from the edge of the web can behave differently from one cut from the interior. Edge ribbon can be marginally narrower, less square and slightly more prone to fraying than interior ribbon, and a buyer who approves a sample drawn from the interior of the pattern may receive ribbon that was cut from the edge. Where a programme contains several widths, request the sample for each width to be drawn from the position in the pattern that the production run will actually use, and specify the acceptable width tolerance for each. For narrow widths under about 8 millimetres, ask how the ribbon is produced, because if it is woven directly rather than slit, the whole model of the programme changes.

Can you slit ribbon at the mill to widths that are not standard?

In most cases yes, within the limits of the web the mill is running and the position available in the cutting pattern. The constraint is that every additional finished width needs its own blade position, and each blade consumes a small band of material, so the slittable width matrix for a given web is finite and depends on the edge trim allowance the mill applies. The mill should be able to tell you which widths can share a pass. If a required width cannot fit into the pattern, the options are usually to run it separately, to adjust the quantity mix so that another width occupies the position, or in some cases to change the web width. That is why asking for the matrix before ordering, rather than after a sample has been approved, is worth doing.

How does slitting affect lead time for a ribbon order?

Lead time is affected less by slitting itself than by how many production runs the width mix forces. Widths that share a slitting pass add no significant time to the schedule, because the web is already there and the machine simply makes one more pass over it. Widths that cannot fit the pattern require either a separate weaving run, which carries the full setup cost including the dyeing and finishing of another web, or a separate slitting pass. The practical consequence is that a programme with a coherent width mix and a programme with the same total metres in a scattered width mix do not have the same schedule. This is why the width mix belongs in the early conversation with the mill rather than arriving as a completed purchase order.
