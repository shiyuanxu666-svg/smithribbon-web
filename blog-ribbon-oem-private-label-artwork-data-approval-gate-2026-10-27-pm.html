Ribbon OEM Private Label: Artwork Data Handover, Brand Asset Control and the Pre-Production Approval Gate 2026

📅 October 27, 2026 ⏱ 13 min read 🏭 Smith Ribbon OEM since 2004 by Smith Ribbon

The approval gate is the mechanism that stops a production line from running with artwork nobody properly signed off. It is not bureaucracy — it is the cheapest insurance available in a branded packaging programme, because a roll printed and finished with the wrong artwork is not a defective product that gets discounted. It is a completed product, consumed by your customer, photographed, and impossible to recover.

\n\n

The five stages

\n\n
    \n
  1. Stage 1 — Pre-flight and printability review. Before any physical sample is made, the factory reviews your files and returns a written printability note: what will work, what is at risk, what needs adjusting, what the process will not achieve. Nothing moves forward until this is returned. This stage is free and fast, and it prevents the majority of downstream problems.
    2. \n\n
  2. Stage 2 — Lab dip and colour master. Actual production material is dyed to your colour and returned as a physical swatch, cut from production stock, not from a showroom book. This swatch, once approved, becomes the master referenced in all later stages. Approval is by named person, in writing, with the master code recorded.
    3. \n\n
  3. Stage 3 — Pre-production sample. A full printed sample on production material, to scale, with the actual ribbon width and repeat, produced using the intended production settings. It arrives with a batch reference and a photograph of the actual production run under standardised lighting.
    4. \n\n
  4. Stage 4 — Counter-sample from the production run. A second sample cut from the actual production run against which the order will be made. This is the last realistic chance to catch a drift, and it is the stage that most programmes skip and most regret. If the counter-sample diverges from the approved pre-production sample, the run stops.
    5. \n\n
  5. Stage 5 — Written production release. A signed release naming the artwork version, the colour master code, the batch reference, the quantity, the delivery date and the agreed tolerance. The factory cannot start production against anything else. Changes to artwork after release are a new cycle, not an informal email.
    6. \n
\n\n
\n

Version control is the gate. Every artwork file needs a version number and a date, and every approval must reference that version. The most common dispute in branded production is not about quality — it is about which version was approved. \"I thought we changed that\" is expensive. A version reference on the release document ends the conversation.

\n
\n\n

Who should approve what

\n\n

A common weakness is that a designer approves a colour they chose, a buyer approves a cost, and nobody with production knowledge reviews the artwork as it will actually be printed. Then the sample comes back and it is not what was expected, and everyone points at a different person.

\n\n

A workable allocation of authority:

\n\n

Write rejection criteria before you need them

\n\n

Approval criteria agreed during a dispute are not trusted. Set them at the start of the programme, in writing, as objective as the process allows: minimum line weight, minimum type size, colour tolerance against the master, acceptable edge fray, acceptable weft or warp marks, allowable print defects and their frequency per roll, and whether a specific number of marks per roll is acceptable or grounds for rejection.

\n\n

Ribbon is a textile, not a printed sheet. Expect natural variation in weave, minor weft marks, occasional slubs, and slight edge fraying. A specification that allows zero of these will generate a rejection argument on every delivery. A specification that permits unlimited variation is meaningless. The useful position is to define the frequency and severity that your product and your customer can live with, and to say so plainly.

\n\n

Part Five — Managing Repeat Orders and Re-Orders

\n\n

The approval gate governs the first run. What governs the next eight is order administration, and the same discipline applies: every repeat order should reference the artwork version, the colour master code and the approved counter-sample, so that a re-order in month seven produces the same ribbon as the sample approved in month one.

\n\n

For brands with seasonal programmes, plan a re-approval trigger rather than assuming continuity. A colour master should be re-confirmed when the master swatch has been in service for a defined period, when the raw material supplier changes, when the factory changes a machine or process, or when you receive a counter-sample that diverges. Any of these is a reason to run a fresh colour match and re-issue the master.

\n\n
\n

One master, one record. Keep a single controlled colour master record for each programme: swatch code, Pantone reference, construction, face, illuminant, tolerance, approval date, approver name, and the production runs it governs. When a new master supersedes an old one, retire the old one explicitly and record which production runs remain valid against it. Most colour arguments in private-label ribbon are not about how good the colour is — they are about which standard was in force.

Frequently Asked Questions

What artwork files should I send to a ribbon OEM manufacturer?

Send vector artwork (AI, EPS or PDF/X) wherever the design permits, placed raster images at 300 dpi at final print size, a colour specification sheet mapping each element to a named Pantone reference, the print size with ribbon width and repeat length stated, and a reference photograph of the physical product you want to match. Text should be outlined. Always send the physical approved sample as well — a file describes the design, but the sample is the actual standard that will be measured against.

How do you control colour consistency across seasons on private-label ribbon?

Create one named colour master per programme: a sealed cut length of approved production ribbon, a labelled Pantone chip, and a written colour specification record stating construction, Pantone reference, process, agreed tolerance, lighting illuminant and which face was approved. Retain the master with both parties, reference it by code on every order, and re-confirm it whenever the raw material supplier, machine or process changes.

Who should approve the pre-production sample for a branded ribbon programme?

Split the approval across the functions that each hold different kinds of authority. The factory's technical manager reviews printability and feasibility, the brand designer or colour manager approves the colour master, the designer plus category buyer approve the pre-production printed sample, procurement plus quality approve the counter-sample taken from the production run, and the category buyer signs the production release that commits scope, quantity, date and tolerance.

What should a ribbon OEM contract say about ownership of my artwork and brand assets?

At minimum: that your artwork, logos and derived production files remain your property; that the supplier may use them only for your orders, with no sampling, self-promotion, stock lines or third-party use; that any sub-contractor is disclosed and bound by the same terms; that files are stored only for the programme plus a defined re-order period and deleted on request with written confirmation; and that on termination all copies including physical swatches are returned or destroyed. Include a clause governing sample cuttings for commercially sensitive designs.

Why is a counter-sample from the production run important?

A pre-production sample shows what the process can do. A counter-sample shows what the actual production run did. The two can diverge — different dye lot, different machine, different setup — and only the counter-sample reveals that before the goods ship. It is the last inexpensive point at which a bad run can be stopped, and skipping it is the most common reason branded ribbon disappoints on repeat orders.

What is the minimum order quantity for private-label printed ribbon?

Custom printed ribbon typically starts at 1,000 metres per colour per width, with 500 metres available on selected constructions. Sample and prototype quantities are much smaller, but they are not production quantities and the unit economics differ substantially. Bespoke constructions, custom widths and complex multi-colour prints are quoted case by case, and we will confirm whether your design sits on a stocked construction or requires a new setup.
