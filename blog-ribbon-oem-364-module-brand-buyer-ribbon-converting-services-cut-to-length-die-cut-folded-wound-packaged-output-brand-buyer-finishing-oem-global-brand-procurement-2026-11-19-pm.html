Ribbon Converting Services Explained — Cut-to-Length, Die-Cut, Folded and Packaged Output for Brand Buyers (2026)

Published: 2026-11-19  |  Updated: 2026-11-19  |  By Xiamen Smith Ribbon & Bow Co., Ltd.  |  Since 2004

Most ribbon mills sell ribbon by the metre on a reel. Most brand buyers, particularly those running a fulfilment operation rather than a packaging line, do not want a reel. They want ribbon already cut, folded, wound or packaged, counted into cartons they can pick from without a knife and a spare afternoon. That gap between mill output and ready-to-use output is called converting, and it is where a great deal of the cost, the tolerance and the lead time of a ribbon programme actually live.

A mill that weaves satin ribbon all day and sells it by the kilometre is not, by default, the business you want if you buy 3,000 metres of ribbon and need 20,000 finished pieces at a fixed length. Between the reel and the ready-to-use piece sits a set of operations called converting, and they are the part of the ribbon supply chain that buyers most often under-specify, under-price and under-time.

Converting sounds like a simple trade step. It is not, and the reason it is not is that every conversion adds a tolerance, a set-up, a piece of tooling or a counting decision that has to be made by somebody. A 15 mm ribbon cut to 200 mm is trivial. The same ribbon cut to 200 mm ± 2 mm, folded at a specific point, wound onto a 25 mm card with a printed header and bagged in fifties is a genuinely different product with a genuinely different cost and a genuinely different failure mode.

This guide covers converting as a buyer should treat it: the main conversion routes, what each one actually involves, how the costs build, how to specify each step so it is repeatable, and the questions that separate a converter who can do the job from one who is guessing.

What Converting Actually Covers

Converting is everything that happens to ribbon between the mill and the point where it enters your workflow. It is often presented as a single line item on a quotation, which is why buyers compare converting quotes badly. It is really a menu of operations, and a quote that names them is worth more than one that shows a single conversion fee.

Two observations from this table that change how a buyer reads a quotation. First, the only conversion that needs dedicated tooling is die-cutting, and everything else runs on general equipment. That means the marginal cost of a conversion depends almost entirely on how long the machine or the operator spends per piece, which is why the number of pieces per hour is the most important piece of information a converter can give you.

Second, note which conversions add tolerance risk. Cutting and folding add dimensional variation. Die-cutting adds a registration relationship between the shape and the ribbon edge. Heat-sealing adds a risk of damage to the ribbon surface if the temperature is set wrong. Every conversion that adds value also adds something to inspect, and a converter who does not tell you what they check is telling you something.

Cut-to-Length: The Workhorse

Cut-to-length is the conversion most buyers need first, and the one most often specified too loosely. The operation itself is simple — a ribbon is fed through a cutter and sectioned at a fixed length — but the specification determines whether the output is usable.

Three variables matter. The length and its tolerance, which should be stated as a nominal with a plus-or-minus band. The cut edge, which is either a clean hot-cut (heat-sealed as it is cut) or a cold guillotine cut, and which looks and frays differently. And the count, both per bundle and per carton.

The tolerance band is worth thinking about carefully, because it is where a converter's capability is tested. A band of plus or minus 2 mm on a 200 mm piece is a modest demand. A band of plus or minus 0.5 mm on a 200 mm piece is a real precision demand that few high-speed cutters hold reliably across a full run, and a converter who quotes it without discussing it should be asked to justify it.

There is also a cost-versus-tolerance trade that buyers rarely consider. A converter running a fast guillotine cutter will hold a wide band because the machine is set to cut fast. A converter running a slower, measured bander will hold a tight band but will price the labour accordingly. If your tolerance is genuinely tight, expect to pay for the machine that can hold it, and ask to see the machine.

Die-Cutting: Where Tooling Changes the Economics

Die-cutting puts a shape into the ribbon: a notch, a tab, a slot, a pointed end, a chevron, a decorative profile. Unlike the other conversions, it requires a physical die, and that die is an asset with a cost, a lead time and an ownership question.

On cost, a die is a one-time charge amortised over the run, so the unit cost of die-cutting falls sharply with volume. The same die that adds USD 0.90 per piece at 500 pieces may add USD 0.04 per piece at 50,000. Any quotation that shows die-cutting as a flat per-piece rate without showing the underlying amortisation is hiding a volume break you should be negotiating against.

On lead time, a new die typically takes several days to a week or more to manufacture before the first pieces can be produced, and it must be approved against a proof before production starts. That lead time sits in front of your first delivery, and it is a lead time that is easy to forget because it does not appear on the ribbon itself.

On ownership, this is the question most often skipped. The die is usually made to the buyer's artwork and is specific to that buyer's shape. Who owns it, who pays to store it, who pays to modify it, and what happens to it if the programme ends, should all be written down. A die is also the point at which a supplier's intellectual property becomes real: the shape and the cutting programme are yours, but the converter's own process knowledge in producing it is theirs. A clear written agreement prevents an argument years later.

Folding: Cheap, and Easy to Specify Badly

Folding turns a length into a presentation form. It is inexpensive, it adds a great deal to how a product looks, and it is specified with surprising frequency by guesswork.

For a loop fold, the specification needs the ribbon length before folding and the finished loop circumference, plus the number of folds. For a flat fold with adhesive, it needs the adhesive specification, the peel strength and the shelf life, because an adhesive that performs well in a warehouse in cool conditions may not perform in a hot delivery van. For a cross fold with a slot, it needs the slot dimension, because a slot that is too narrow will not open cleanly and the customer will fight with it.

Two things go wrong most often. The first is folding the ribbon so that a printed design runs directly into the fold, which is where printing cracks first. The second is folding a ribbon that is too wide for the box it wraps, which produces a fold that will not sit flat. Both are avoidable with a dimensional drawing rather than a description, and a converter who can read a drawing will usually ask for one.

Spooling and Carding: The Retail Presentation Route

Winding ribbon onto a core or a flat card is the route to a product that looks bought rather than made. It is also the route that most often brings a paper or board component into the quotation, and that component has its own minimum order quantity.

The specification needs the winding width and the length per card, the board weight and finish, the hole position and diameter if the card is pegged, and whether a printed header is attached. The header is a separate print operation with a separate minimum, and it frequently governs the minimum of the whole unit rather than the ribbon.

Winding quality is worth specifying explicitly. Ribbon wound too tightly under tension will develop a permanent curl and a memory that makes it lie flat on the shelf rather than sit square in the pack, and the curl is often visible in a photograph. Ask for the winding tension to be specified and relaxed after winding, and ask to approve a finished card rather than a wound reel.

Tipping and Edge Sealing

A cut ribbon end is a loose end. On woven-edge ribbon this is a non-issue because the selvedge holds the construction together, which is one of the strongest practical arguments for specifying a woven edge when the ribbon will be cut. On cut-edge ribbon, heat sealing the end — tipping — prevents fraying and gives a better appearance at a printed end.

The specification should state the seal method and the seal length, and should note whether the ribbon material tolerates heat. Polyester satin seals easily. Some technical constructions with metallic film, elastic core or a fusible adhesive face do not, and a converter who applies a standard tip to an elastic ribbon without checking will cause you a problem that only appears after the ribbon is in the box.

Bowing and Tying: The Labour-Intensive Conversion

A tied bow is a different business from a cut ribbon, because the work is hand labour rather than machine time. A bow is typically made from a short ribbon length, folded, looped and secured, and the cost per bow reflects the seconds of skilled labour in it.

Two decisions matter commercially. Whether the bow is pre-tied and shipped flat, or attached to the product, changes the labour and therefore the price. And whether the bow is hand-tied or machine-tied changes both the cost and the consistency: a hand-tied bow has natural variation, which some brands want and some do not, and a machine-tied bow is uniform but can look stiff in a photograph.

For buyers evaluating bows, the useful comparison is not unit price but cost per finished appearance in the customer's hands. A bow that has to be tied by the customer at unboxing is a value item; a bow that arrives pre-attached to a tag is a packaging cost. The two are priced very differently and should be specified very differently.

How Converting Costs Build

Converting cost is built from a small number of variables, and a quote that shows them is far more useful than one that shows a single rate.

Cost elementWhat drives itHow to reduce it
Set-upEach distinct operation configured on the lineConsolidate operations; order fewer distinct formats
Machine time per pieceLine speed and handlingMove to a faster route; accept standard lengths
Labour per pieceHandling, counting, knotting, tyingPre-tie and pack flat; automate counting

Scrap and yieldOffcuts from length cutting and die cuttingPlan cut lengths to nest within the master width
InspectionTolerance checks, count verificationDefine the checks you actually require
Outward freightCarton density and destinationAgree a carton specification that cubes well

The row buyers most often forget is scrap. Converting a 200 mm piece from a 1000 m reel wastes a fraction of the reel unless the master width nests the pieces efficiently, and on narrow widths the waste can be significant. A converter who has thought about nesting will tell you the most efficient way to cut your length from their available widths, and that conversation can be worth more than any negotiation on the unit rate.

How to Specify a Converting Programme

A converting specification that works contains seven things, and any one of them missing produces a quote you cannot compare with another quote.

  1. Master material and construction. Not just "satin" — the construction, the width, the finish and the edge treatment of the reel that is being converted.
  2. Piece geometry. Nominal length and tolerance, fold specification, or the card or core format if it is being wound.
  3. Cut and edge treatment. Clean heat-cut, guillotine, or die-cut, with the shape and dimensions if die-cut.
  4. Counts and packing. Pieces per bundle, bundles per carton, carton dimensions and weight, and pieces per carton.
  5. Component specification. Card weight and size, bag material and dimensions, header print specification — each with its own minimum.
  6. Inspection requirement. Which checks are performed, at what frequency, and what evidence is provided. Count verification is separate from length tolerance, and it should be asked for explicitly.
  7. Tolerance and acceptance. The acceptable band for each measurable attribute, and what happens when a batch falls outside it.

Then approve against a physical golden sample of the finished converted piece, not against a ribbon reel and a description. Converting introduces variation that only exists in the finished form, and a sample of the reel tells you nothing about whether the fold is right.

Lead Time: Where Converting Sits in the Schedule

A buyer who plans lead time from the mill's weaving date will be disappointed, because converting sits between weaving and delivery. A realistic schedule separates four stages: the material, which may be in stock or dyed to order; the conversion set-up, including any die; the production run; and the inspection and packing. Each can be a week or more, and the first three can partially overlap once the process is stable.

The practical point is that a converting programme has a faster repeat cycle than a new programme does. Once the die exists, the material is available and the fold is established, a repeat order can often be turned in a small fraction of the time the first took. Buyers should plan the launch and the reorder as two different lead times, and should not quote the launch lead time to a retailer who asks about replenishment.

Mill or Converter: Do You Need Both?

There are three practical arrangements, and they suit different buyers.

One mill that converts. Simplest, fewest handoffs, fewest opportunities for a specification to be lost in translation. Availability depends on whether the equipment suits your format, and a mill that does not run your width or your fold will subcontract, which reintroduces the handoff you were trying to avoid. Ask directly whether the work is done in-house.

A mill plus a separate converter. The ribbon is bought from a specialist and converted by a specialist. This usually gives better quality in each stage and lets you choose the converter for capability rather than accepting whoever the mill uses. The cost is coordination: two specifications to keep aligned, two suppliers to manage, and an extra transit between them. It also often improves the economics, because the converter can optimise for its own equipment while the mill optimises for weaving.

A trading company with both. Convenient for a brand buying across several trim categories. The trade-off is that the specification has been translated twice by the time it reaches the line, so document it thoroughly and require that the golden sample be approved by you and held by them.

For a brand buying one material in one format, the first or second arrangement is usually best. For a brand running a multi-category trim programme, the third is worth the coordination cost.

Questions to Ask a Converter

  • Is the work done in-house or subcontracted, and if subcontracted, to whom?
  • What is the line speed for my format, in pieces per hour?
  • What length tolerance can you hold on this material, and what does tighter tolerance change in the price?
  • Who owns the die, what is the die lead time, and what happens to it if we stop?
  • What is your minimum order for this format, and what is the minimum for the card or component separately?
  • What inspection is performed, and what evidence do I receive with the shipment?
  • How do you handle a batch that falls outside tolerance, and who pays for it?
  • How much scrap does my format generate, and can nesting improve it?
  • What is the lead time for a repeat order once the process is running?
  • How is the count verified, and against what standard do you reconcile?

A Short Brief You Can Send

Most converters will quote from a short brief, provided the key dimensions are present. A workable version runs to a few lines: material and construction; master width; piece length and tolerance; fold or wound format; cut treatment; counts per bundle and per carton; component specification; inspection requirement; delivery date; indicative annual volume; and destination. Add one line on the end application, because it tells the converter what the ribbon has to survive, and it is often the detail that determines whether the specification is right.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is ribbon converting?

Converting is everything that happens to ribbon between the mill and the point where it enters your workflow. It includes slitting wide master rolls into narrower widths, cutting ribbon to fixed lengths, die-cutting shapes into the ends, folding lengths into presentation forms, winding ribbon onto cards or cores, heat-sealing cut ends to prevent fraying, tying bows, and kitting components together into packed units. A mill sells ribbon by the metre on a reel; a brand running a fulfilment operation usually wants the finished piece. Converting is the work that bridges the two.

How much does ribbon converting cost per piece?

There is no single rate, because converting cost is driven by set-up, machine time per piece, labour per piece, tooling amortisation, component cost, scrap and inspection. The two cost elements that move most in practice are labour and tooling. A bow or a hand-tied element can be several times the cost of a straight cut length because it is hand labour rather than machine time. A die-cut shape carries a one-time die cost that is spread over the run, so it falls sharply as volume rises. Ask for a quotation that separates material, conversion and component cost, because a single blended rate is impossible to compare against another supplier's.

What length tolerance should I specify for cut-to-length ribbon?

Specify it against the role the piece plays. A ribbon the customer unties and discards can vary by a centimetre without anyone noticing. A ribbon that wraps a fixed box and has to sit flat in a product photograph needs a tight band so it does not slip or pull, and a tighter band requires a measured bander rather than a fast guillotine, which is priced accordingly. A plus or minus 2 mm band on a 200 mm piece is a modest demand that most converters hold. A plus or minus 0.5 mm band is a real precision demand, and any converter quoting it should be asked to justify how they hold it across a full run.

Who owns the die used for die-cut ribbon, and what if I stop ordering?

It needs to be agreed in writing before the die is made, and most sensible arrangements assign ownership of the buyer's shape and cutting programme to the buyer, while the converter retains ownership of its own production process knowledge. Specify who pays to store the die, who pays to modify it if the shape changes, whether it can be used for another of your products, and what happens to it if the programme ends. A die is also a lead time item, typically several days to a week or more to manufacture, and that lead time sits in front of your first delivery.

Can I get one supplier to make both the ribbon and the packaging cards?

They can, and for a tightly integrated simple kit it reduces handoffs. But a mill that makes excellent printed ribbon is often a poor choice for printed paper headers and cards, because paper printing has a different minimum quantity, a different colour process and a different press schedule. Buying cards from a specialist and having the converter k them together often costs less and gives a better result. Whichever route you take, confirm the minimum order for each component separately, because the card minimum frequently governs the minimum of the whole unit rather than the ribbon.

Should the ribbon I receive be cut-edge or woven-edge?

If the ribbon is going to be cut into pieces, woven-edge is generally the better choice. A woven selvedge holds the construction together, so the cut end does not fray and needs no heat sealing. A cut edge needs tipping or sealing to prevent fraying, which adds a conversion step, adds a cost, and on some materials carries a risk of heat damage. Woven edge also survives a parcel crease better. The trade-off is that woven-edge ribbon is marginally more expensive per metre than equivalent cut-edge ribbon, so the saving on the conversion side partly offsets the material difference.

How long does a converting programme take compared with the weaving?

Converting sits between weaving and delivery, so a buyer who plans from the mill's weaving date will be disappointed. Realistically there are four stages: material sourcing or dyeing, conversion set-up including any die manufacture, the production run, and inspection and packing. Each can run to a week or more, and after the first run the stages overlap. The important point for planning is that a repeat order is much faster than the launch, because the die exists, the material is available and the fold is already established. Quote the launch lead time for the launch and a separate replenishment lead time to retailers who ask.

How do I compare two converting quotations?

Normalise them to the same scope and the same unit. Two converters quoting 'the same' programme may be quoting on entirely different scope, because the term converting covers anything from a straight cut to a die-cut, folded, carded and bagged unit. Require each quotation to specify the material and master width, the piece length and tolerance, the fold or wound format, the cut treatment, the counts per bundle and per carton, the component specification with its own minimum, and the inspection requirement. Then normalise to a per-piece landed cost and check the scrap assumptions, because narrow widths lose a meaningful percentage of the reel to offcuts unless the cut length nests well.

Is it worth using a separate converter instead of buying converting from the mill?

It often is. A mill that does not run your width or your fold will usually subcontract the work, which reintroduces the handoff you were trying to avoid. A dedicated converter lets you choose for capability rather than accepting whoever the mill uses, and it often improves the economics because the converter optimises for its own equipment while the mill optimises for weaving. The cost is coordination: two specifications to keep aligned, two suppliers to manage and an extra transit. For a brand buying one material in one format, a mill that converts in-house is simpler; for a multi-category trim programme, a trading company that handles both is worth the coordination cost.

How do I make sure the converted ribbon arrives in the quantity I ordered?

Ask for count verification as an explicit inspection item, separate from dimensional tolerance, and ask what standard the count is reconciled against. The usual control is a batch count taken at the point of conversion plus a verification count at packing, reconciled against the work order quantity before despatch. Also agree what happens if a carton is short or over at receipt, and how a discrepancy is settled. For a programme running at high piece counts, a systematic counting error is more likely than a quality failure and is much more expensive if it is not caught at packing.
