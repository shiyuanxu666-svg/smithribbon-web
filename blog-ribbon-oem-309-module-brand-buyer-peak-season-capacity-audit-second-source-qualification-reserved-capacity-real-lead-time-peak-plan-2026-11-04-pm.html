The peak season question nobody asks until it is too late

Every year, a brand buyer discovers in September that the ribbon they need for the Christmas season cannot be made in time, and every year the discovery is treated as a supply failure. It is usually not. It is the predictable result of a capacity question that was never asked, in a document that never needed to contain the answer, between two parties who both behaved reasonably and both expected the other to have done something.

The uncomfortable truth about peak-season ribbon capacity is that it is decided in the spring, or earlier. Loom slots, dye capacity, printing cylinders, hand-tie assembly labour and finishing equipment are all scheduled well in advance of the demand they will serve. A mill that receives its October orders in August has no physical capacity to add them. It is not unwilling. It is full, and the work in it has already been promised to someone else.

This guide is about the capacity conversation itself: how to audit whether a mill can actually take your volume, how to read the answers you get, what a realistic lead time looks like at different points in the year, how to qualify a second source so that it is a genuine alternative rather than a document, and how to build a peak plan that survives a bad month. It is written for the buyer who has been burned by a late delivery and wants to make sure it does not happen twice.

Auditing capacity: what "we can handle it" actually means

When you ask a mill whether it can handle your volume, you will usually get an affirmative answer, and the answer will usually be true. The useful question is not whether they can handle it but under what conditions, and with what evidence. A capacity audit is a set of questions designed to make the answer specific.

Ask for the number in units of the constraint. Not "we have a good capacity", but "how many metres of 25 mm printed polyester ribbon can you run per week at peak". Constraints differ by process, so ask for the constraint that binds on your product specifically. A mill with enormous weaving capacity and a four-dye-house dyeing constraint is a poor fit for a programme with eight colourways and a tight launch date. The capacity number that matters is the one at your bottleneck, and most buyers never identify where their own programme's bottleneck sits.

Then ask what shifts that number. How many dyeing machines, how many printing cylinders, how many finishing lines, how many hand-tie assembly stations, and how many trained operators on each. A capacity number supported by one shift and a small specialist team is not a capacity number that scales, and a mill that quotes its three-shift potential without mentioning that it has trained two operators for that third shift is describing a plan, not a capability.

Ask what the historical peak utilisation was, and in what month. A mill with a 70 percent average utilisation in the busiest month of its last two years has genuine headroom. A mill that ran at 98 percent last November and is quoting you an improvement plan has a plan.

Then ask the two questions that reveal more than the others. What is the earliest date on which you could put my programme into production, and what would have to change for that to be June rather than September? And if I asked for double the volume for December, what specifically would you have to change to make it possible? The answers tell you where the real constraint is, and the second answer tells you how much of the supplier's statement is capability and how much is aspiration.

Reading the answers honestly

Capacity conversations are unusually prone to optimism, and the buyer who takes a verbal answer at face value will discover the gap in the worst month of the year. Three specific tells indicate a capacity claim that will not hold.

The first is capacity expressed as a percentage. "We have 30 percent headroom" is not a number a buyer can use, because it does not say 30 percent of what. Convert everything to metres, units or hours per week on your own product, and record it in that form.

The second is capacity that assumes no setup loss. Printing capacity is not the sum of cylinder run speeds; every colour change, every new order and every material change consumes hours that produce nothing. A mill quoting cylinder speed as capacity is quoting a theoretical maximum that will be 25 to 40 percent below in practice. Ask for the realised average, not the machine rating, and ask for the figure from the last peak season rather than from a standard month.

The third is capacity that assumes current staffing. A mill that can run four shifts because it can recruit four shifts, when the recruitment market for skilled hand-tie operators in the region is tight and the training period is eight weeks, has a capacity commitment with a lead time attached to it. Ask whether any part of the claimed capacity depends on hiring or training, and if so, whether that hiring has already happened.

The most reliable evidence, when it is available, is the mill's own peak-season record. Which programmes did it serve last November, what were their dates, and did they ship on time? A supplier with a trackable history of peak-season delivery is worth paying somewhat more for than one with an impressive capacity document and no history, and the way to establish that history is to ask for references at peak, not in general. A contact who ordered in March will tell you about March. A contact who ordered in October will tell you about October.

The real lead time is longer than the quoted lead time

Every mill quotes a lead time, and every quoted lead time is a manufacturing lead time. The manufacturing lead time covers the process: dye, print, finish, assemble, pack. It does not cover the three intervals that cause the late deliveries buyers complain about, and it is worth learning to add them yourself.

IntervalTypical durationWhy it is often missed
Order-to-material1–4 weeksYarn or greige for a custom colourway must be sourced and dyed. The mill does not hold greige in your colour.
Sample-to-approval1–3 weeksBuyers frequently build this into the plan mentally and then skip it because it is "just samples". It is a real elapsed time and it is fully on the critical path.
Production-to-ship1–3 weeksFinishing, assembly, inspection, packing and documentation. Inspection is a queue as well as an activity.
Ship-to-deliver2–6 weeksSea freight is the largest and least variable of these. Air freight is faster and usually not a solution at ribbon volumes.

When a mill quotes 25 days for a printed programme in June, the honest planning number for a first delivery is somewhere between 60 and 90 days, because the quoted figure is the manufacturing leg and the buyer has to supply the other three. The arithmetic is unglamorous but it is the difference between a launch that lands and one that does not, and it is why experienced buyers work backwards from the required on-shelf date rather than forwards from the order date.

Two peak-specific adjustments apply on top. First, manufacturing leg times lengthen under load, because a mill running near capacity experiences more queueing: a dye lot waits behind other dye lots, a hand-tie station waits for material rather than for operators. A 25-day manufacturing leg quoted in June may be a 35-day leg in November at the same volume. Second, incoming material becomes less reliable before it becomes more expensive, because upstream mills in peak season cut corners on delivery to their own customers, and your greige arrives later than the paperwork promised.

Peak season has a shape, not a peak

Buyers tend to think of peak as a single spike in a single month, and they plan capacity for that spike. In ribbon and decorative trim the demand curve is closer to a plateau with a steep leading edge, and the shape determines where you need capacity and when you need it locked.

The pattern is consistent across the year. Sampling and development demand is low and steady through the spring. Capacity for the autumn retail cycle begins to bind in the late summer, because the manufacturing lead time plus the shipping time means that anything to be on a European shelf in early December must have left the factory in October. The steepest part of the curve is the four to six weeks from mid-September, when multiple programmes all reach the same production window and share the same dyeing, printing and finishing capacity. The demand does not collapse in January, but the urgency disappears, because the next buying cycle is spring and the immediate pressure is off.

Two consequences follow. The first is that reserving capacity in August for an October delivery is a substantially better proposition than reserving it in October for an October delivery, because in August the mill can still schedule; in October it can only triage. The second is that the peak is decided by the last Thursday of September, not by the first Thursday of December. Everything that was not decided by then will be decided by whoever calls the factory that week.

Qualifying a second source properly

Second-sourcing is routinely presented as a risk mitigation strategy and routinely executed as a document exercise: a second mill is identified, a capability deck is collected, a sample is requested, and the buyer retains a sense of security that does not correspond to any real capability. A second source that has never made your product at your volume is not a second source. It is a hope.

A genuine second source needs four things, and the first is the one most often skipped.

It needs a qualified product, not a qualified mill. The second source should have made your specification at commercial volume, or at minimum a production-intent run against your own golden sample, with a colour and construction match you have measured rather than admired. A second mill that has demonstrated capability in a neighbouring specification is a partial mitigation, and buyers should know which kind they have. The honest middle position, which many buyers reach and few state, is a second source qualified at pre-production scale and intended to absorb a share of volume rather than to absorb a total failure.

It needs its own tooling and its own material chain. If your second source shares your first source's printing cylinder, your dye house, or your primary greige supplier, it is not a second source; it is a second front door to the same building. The sub-tier dependency question applies to resilience exactly as it applies to compliance, and it is worth mapping explicitly for each programme: which steps of the second source's process are genuinely independent.

It needs capacity reserved, not hoped for. A second source that has never been given a reserved slot will allocate its peak capacity to whoever asks first. A meaningful second-source arrangement involves a capacity reservation, even if the volume is modest, because the reservation is what creates the physical possibility of switching. This is the least comfortable item in the list because it costs money for an option that should never be exercised, and it is also the item that determines whether the option has any value.

It needs a switching plan with a tested trigger. Decide in advance what happens: the trigger condition, the notification period, the quantities that transfer, the tooling transfer time, the artwork and colour approval implications, and the customer communication. A switch that has never been rehearsed takes weeks to execute even when the capacity was genuinely reserved, because colour approval, first-article inspection and customer notification all have to happen. Rehearse it at least once with a real, small quantity.

Building a peak plan that survives a bad month

The purpose of a peak plan is not to prevent disruption. It is to make disruption survivable, and the two are different enough that a plan designed only for the ideal case is not much use.

The first element is a required-on-shelf date, stated as a date rather than a season. "For Christmas" is a planning input that means four different things to four different people. "On shelf in our European distribution centres by 1 October" is actionable, and it is the date that the whole plan is built backwards from.

The second element is a materials calendar that names the critical input for each programme and the date by which it must be committed. This is the exercise that catches problems early, because the material requirement has a longer lead time than the manufacturing requirement. If the yarn for a colourway takes four weeks to source and the printing slot is two weeks away, the programme is already late and nobody has noticed.

The third element is a cut-off date per programme, agreed with the mill and written down, after which changes are not accepted for that season. This sounds like an operational annoyance and it is the highest-leverage control in the entire process. Every unplanned change in October consumes capacity that a committed programme needed, and the cost of that consumption is paid by the programme that gets displaced. A firm cut-off, enforced by both sides, is what protects the committed programmes from the changes that always arrive with good intentions and no capacity.

The fourth element is a prioritisation rule for when capacity genuinely conflicts, decided in advance. Not "the customer who shouts loudest", but a stated sequence based on a defined criterion such as on-shelf date, order value, or strategic significance. A rule decided in advance is rarely needed; a rule needed to be decided in advance is frequently needed, and the whole point of the audit earlier in this guide is to avoid needing it.

The fifth element is a defined buffer, and this deserves a word because buyers tend to treat buffers as waste. Buffer in a peak plan should be expressed as schedule, not as inventory. It means that the programme is planned to arrive three weeks before it is needed, or that 5 percent of the quantity is planned as a second small shipment rather than one large one. Shipping 80 percent of the quantity early and the balance later gives the mill a chance to correct problems on a small lot, and it gives the brand a partial stock in the warehouse when the balance is delayed. It is the cheapest insurance in the business and the one most often declined because it looks like indecision rather than planning.

Questions to ask, and what good answers sound like

When you are assessing a ribbon mill for peak-season capability, these questions separate the mills that will deliver from the mills that will try. What was your single largest programme last November, and did it ship on time? What is your average dye-house utilisation in October, measured rather than estimated? How many weeks would it take you to increase capacity, and what specifically would you need to change? If I gave you my programme in June, when could it ship, and what would have to happen for that to be April? Which of my process steps are done in-house, and where are the others done? Do you hold greige in my colourways, or would that need to be dyed? What is your experience with a first-time programme in peak season, and what did you learn? And what happens to my programme if your dye house is down for a week in November?

The last question is the sharpest one, and the quality of the answer tells you almost everything. A mill that treats it as a hypothetical will offer reassurance. A mill that treats it as a real scenario will tell you which lots are exposed, what the recovery plan looks like, how much of the programme can be re-sequenced, and what they would need from you to make it work. That answer is worth more than a capacity number, because capacity numbers are estimates and the behaviour under stress is what you are actually buying.

Our own experience in Xiamen since 2004 is that peak planning works when it starts in the spring, when the conversation is still about shapes and quantities rather than about apologies. By September the conversation is no longer a planning conversation, and the only honest options are to expedite, to substitute, or to miss the season. A mill that engages with the shape of your demand in April and reserves a slot in August can still tell you in July whether it is going to work. A mill that first hears from you in August can only tell you it will try.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I audit whether a ribbon mill can really handle my volume?

Ask for capacity in units of your own constraint rather than as a percentage: metres per week at peak for your width, your print route and your colourway count. Then ask what shifts that number, how many machines and how many trained operators sit behind it, what the historical peak utilisation was in the busiest month of the last two years, and what the earliest production date for your programme would be. Finally ask the two most revealing questions: what would have to change for that date to be June rather than September, and if you asked for double the December volume, what specifically would change to make it possible. The answers locate the real bottleneck and separate capability from aspiration.

How long does a ribbon programme really take from order to delivery?

Considerably longer than the quoted manufacturing lead time, because the quoted figure covers only the production process. Four intervals sit on the critical path: order to material, typically one to four weeks, because custom colourway greige must be sourced and dyed; sample to approval, one to three weeks, which buyers routinely omit because it feels like a formality; production to ship, one to three weeks, where inspection is a queue as well as an activity; and ship to deliver, two to six weeks by sea. A mill quoting 25 days for a printed programme in June is describing the manufacturing leg, and the honest planning number for a first delivery is closer to 60 to 90 days.

What is a genuinely qualified second source for ribbon?

A second source that has made your specification at commercial volume, or at minimum a production-intent run measured against your own golden sample. It also needs its own tooling and its own material chain, because a second mill sharing your printing cylinder or dye house is a second door to the same building; a capacity reservation rather than good intentions, because unreserved capacity goes to whoever asks first; and a switching plan with a defined trigger and a rehearsed execution, since a switch that has never been tested takes weeks to perform even when the capacity was genuinely held.

When should I reserve peak-season capacity with a ribbon mill?

Early enough that the mill can still schedule rather than triage, which in practice means booking in the late summer for an October delivery, and ideally looking at shapes and volumes in the spring. The peak is decided by the last week of September, not by the first week of December. Capacity reserved in August can be released if it is not needed; capacity requested in October is allocated by whoever contacts the factory first. Reservation should state the fee, the release conditions and, most importantly, the priority sequence used when capacity is genuinely constrained.

Is a capacity percentage a useful answer during a ribbon supplier audit?

No, and it is one of the three clearest signs of a capacity claim that will not hold in practice. A percentage does not say a percentage of what, and it cannot be converted into a plan. Two other tells indicate the same thing: capacity expressed as machine rating rather than realised output, which ignores the setup loss that consumes 25 to 40 percent of printing capacity in setup, colour change and material change; and capacity that assumes staffing which has not yet been recruited or trained, particularly for hand-tie assembly where the training period is typically eight weeks.

Why does peak-season demand affect manufacturing times as well as prices?

Because a mill running near capacity experiences queueing. A dye lot waits behind other dye lots, a finishing line waits for material rather than for operators, and a printing cylinder waits for a colour change. A 25-day manufacturing leg quoted in June can become a 35-day leg in November at the same volume, without any change in the underlying efficiency. Inbound material also becomes less reliable before it becomes more expensive, because upstream mills in peak season cut corners on delivery to their own customers, so your greige arrives later than the paperwork promised.

How should I structure a peak-season plan for a ribbon programme?

Five elements. A required-on-shelf date stated as an actual date rather than a season, because the whole plan is built backwards from it. A materials calendar naming the critical input for each programme and the date by which it must be committed, because material lead times run longer than manufacturing lead times. A firm cut-off date per programme, after which changes are not accepted for that season, which protects committed programmes from unplanned changes. A prioritisation rule for genuine capacity conflicts, decided in advance. And a buffer expressed as schedule rather than inventory, such as shipping 80 percent early and the balance later, which gives a small lot the chance to be corrected.

Should a buyer use air freight to solve a late ribbon delivery?

At ribbon volumes, rarely, and it is worth understanding why before assuming it is an option. Ribbon is light and compressible, so it fills a disproportionate share of air freight capacity, and the freight cost per unit is high. More importantly, air freight addresses the transit interval without addressing the interval that usually caused the lateness, which is the order-to-material and production queue time inside the mill. If the goods do not exist, shipping them faster is not available at any price. The exceptions are a genuine shortfall against an otherwise complete order, and a partial shipment as a replacement for a split delivery, which is where air freight genuinely pays for itself.

How do I tell a real second source from a document exercise?

By asking three questions that a genuine second source can answer and a hypothetical one cannot. Can I see a measured colour and construction comparison against my golden sample, with numbers rather than impressions? Which of the process steps in your quotation are done in-house, and which are subcontracted, and where? And has a slot actually been held for my volume, or is my order a hopeful entry into a queue? If the answers are a capability deck, a sample couriered on the day of the visit and a verbal assurance about peak capacity, the second source is a document, and it should be reported to the risk register as such rather than counted as coverage.
