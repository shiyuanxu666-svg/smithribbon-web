Mill-Side Brand-Buyer Ribbon QC Sampling, Lot Identity & Release Protocol Interlock Architecture — AQL, Lot Traceability, Retained Samples and Disposition 2026

Ribbon OEM Published October 30, 2026 · Xiamen Smith Ribbon & Bow Co., Ltd. · 16 min read

Most quality disputes between a brand buyer and a ribbon mill are not disputes about the ribbon. They are disputes about which ribbon. A buyer sends back a complaint about a colour that drifts, and the mill looks at the returned piece and cannot tell whether it came from the first dyeing lot or the fourteenth, whether it was woven on the line that was running the wider selvedge last Tuesday, or whether it sat in a warehouse for two months before the customer opened it. None of those possibilities can be separated after the fact. All of them can be separated beforehand, if the mill has defined its lots properly and the buyer has agreed to sample against those lots rather than against a shipment in the abstract.

Why most ribbon quality disputes are traceability failures, not quality failures

When a buyer raises a ribbon quality complaint, the mill is asked a question that sounds simple and is almost impossible to answer after the fact: which material is this? A complaint arrives as a piece of ribbon attached to a finished product. Between the loom and that piece of ribbon there may have been a dye lot, a warping batch, a loom run, a finishing batch, a slitting shift, a packing shift and four months in a warehouse. If any of those boundaries were not recorded, or were recorded on a piece of paper that nobody retained, the mill cannot exclude any of them.

That is why the release protocol matters more than the sampling plan. Sampling tells you whether a lot is acceptable at the moment you inspect it. Traceability tells you which material a customer is holding. The second is what makes the first useful, and it is what turns a complaint into a correctable fact rather than a dead argument.

The governing idea: a lot is not a shipping convenience. It is a defined population of material that was made under one set of conditions, that can be physically separated from every other lot, and that carries its identity all the way to the customer. Sampling only has meaning against such a lot.

Defining the lot: the single most consequential decision

A lot should be the smallest practical quantity of ribbon that can be treated as homogeneous. Smaller lots give finer traceability and make a failure isolable to a smaller quantity of material. Larger lots are simpler for the mill to manage and reduce the administrative burden. The right answer is not a philosophical position but a function of what actually varies in the process.

For woven ribbon, the natural process boundaries are these:

  • Dye lot. The batch of yarn dyed in one operation, with one recipe and one master batch. This is where colour variation originates, and it is the most common source of a dispute that appears on the shelf as a colour difference between two deliveries of the same SKU.
  • Warp and weft batch. Which cone lots went into which yarn set. This matters when a material change occurs mid-production, and for heavier constructions where yarn quality affects tensile and drape behaviour.
  • Loom run. The production run on one loom, typically defined by a shift or a fixed length. Width stability, edge behaviour and selvedge condition are loom-run attributes, so a defect that is loom-specific is only isolable at this level.
  • Finishing batch. Dyeing, washing, heat setting, coating, printing and slitting. Print registration, shade, shrinkage and width after finishing are all finishing-batch attributes, and a printed ribbon cannot be sampled meaningfully without at least this boundary.

The mill should be able to state, for any given roll, which of these it came from, and to demonstrate that the rolls of one lot are physically grouped. For most programmes, dye lot plus loom run plus finishing date is a workable definition. For printed ribbon, the print run should replace the loom run because it dominates the variation.

What an AQL number does and does not do

An acceptable quality limit is a threshold on the number of defects permitted in a sample, expressed in terms of a defect class. It is one line in a sampling plan, and treating it as the plan is the most common technical misunderstanding in ribbon procurement.

A sampling plan that actually controls quality needs at least these elements, and all of them should be agreed before the first shipment rather than during the first complaint:

  1. Lot definition - the boundaries described above, agreed in writing.
  2. Sample size - how many units are drawn, drawn from which positions in the pack-out.
  3. Inspection level - the statistical confidence the sample size buys.
  4. AQL by severity - separate thresholds for critical, major and minor defects.
  5. Defect classification - what counts as critical, major and minor. This is the document that prevents the most arguments, and it needs photographs.
  6. Disposition rules - what happens on acceptance, on rejection, and on conditional acceptance.

Defect classification deserves particular attention for ribbon, because the severity of a given visual deviation depends on what the ribbon is for. A half-millimetre of bow asymmetry is invisible on a 3 mm ribbon and obvious on a 40 mm ribbon used as a gift wrap element. A slight shade difference is invisible on a matte grosgrain and obvious on a lustre satin. A classification written once, generically, for all products will produce the wrong answer for at least half of the programme. Classifying by defect type is workable; classifying by defect type and product category is better.

Lot identity: what has to be recorded and what has to survive

Lot identity exists in two forms, and they fail differently. The mill needs an internal record that lets it narrow a problem to a production cause. The buyer needs a mark on the goods that survives shipping, storage and the customer's own handling.

On the goods themselves, the practical mechanisms are a lot number on the carton, a batch line, a label, or a colour-coded tape mark on the roll. The requirement is that the mark survives the transit and the warehouse and can be read without opening the carton, and that it links to a record containing at minimum the SKU, the lot identifier, the production dates and the inspection result. For programmes where traceability is a regulatory or contractual requirement rather than a commercial convenience, the record should also link to the material certificates and test reports for that lot.

On the internal side, the record has to be more than a production log. It needs to link the dye lot to the yarn, the yarn to the loom run, the loom run to the finishing batch, and the finishing batch to the packed carton, in a way that can be walked in either direction. When a customer complains about one roll, the useful question is not whether the roll was bad but what else came off the same yarn and the same loom run in the same week. The record is what answers that question in an hour rather than a week.

Retained samples: purpose, method and retention

A retained sample is a piece of the actual production, kept under identified conditions, for the purpose of being compared later. It is the physical answer to the question a dispute always reduces to: was it like this when it left the mill, or did it change afterwards?

There are two things to get right, and they are frequently both wrong.

The first is what is retained. A retained sample should be a length from the actual production of that lot, not a substitute assembled from stock, and it should include the relevant finishing - printed ribbon retains the print, dyed ribbon retains the shade as dyed. It should be identified to its lot.

The second is how it is kept. Ribbon is a hygroscopic textile and it continues to change. A retained sample stored in an uncontrolled warehouse will drift in colour and in dimension, and will slowly stop representing the goods it was taken to represent. The remedy is to store retained samples in a controlled environment at a stated temperature and relative humidity, folded in the standard way with the fold protected against creasing, and to record those conditions. A sample kept properly will still show a readable comparison after a year; a sample kept badly will create a false problem.

Retention should be at least as long as the programme, and for repeat-order programmes it should be longer than the programme, because the moment a repeat order is placed against a specification, the question becomes whether the material is still the same. Keeping two years of retained samples for a stable SKU is cheap insurance against an argument that cannot otherwise be settled.

Mill release versus buyer release

These are separate decisions made by separate parties with separate consequences, and the protocol should produce two documents rather than one.

Mill release is the mill's own conformance statement. It should identify the lot, the specification and its " revision, the inspection standard applied, the results, the number of units inspected, the inspector and the date. It is a statement that the mill is willing to stand behind, and it should be issued per lot rather than per shipment, so that a shipment containing four lots carries four releases.

Buyer release is the buyer's own acceptance of the material into inventory. It records what the buyer inspected, what it found, and its disposition. It does not transfer responsibility from the mill, and it is what the buyer will need to show its own customer, auditor or regulator. A buyer that inspects nothing at arrival and files the mill's release certificate in its own records has recorded a supplier opinion as if it were a buyer verification, which is a weakness that surfaces at the worst possible moment.

Disposition: the four outcomes and what each one means

A release protocol is only complete when it states what happens next. There are four standard dispositions, and each one carries a different obligation:

  • Accepted. The lot meets the specification. Released to inventory, with the mill's release certificate and the buyer's inspection record both filed against the lot identifier.
  • Concession accepted. The lot departs from a stated requirement but is accepted for use. The concession is recorded in writing with the deviation, the measured value, the quantity affected and the authorising person, and it carries an expiry or a review date.
  • Sorted. The lot contains a recoverable proportion of good material and an identified quantity of defects, and the separation can be done at the buyer's end or returned to the mill. The sorted quantity is released and the defective quantity follows a separate path.
  • Rejected. The lot does not meet the specification and is returned, scrapped or reworked. Rejection triggers a formal notification to the mill with the evidence, and it should trigger a review of whether the lot definition and sampling plan were adequate.

The disposition that is most often missing from real protocols is the second one. Concessions are granted informally all the time - a shade slightly off on a low-value ribbon, a few bows slightly asymmetric, a carton short by a metre - and because they are informal they are undocumented. A year later, nobody can say whether the tolerance was agreed or merely tolerated, and the standard has quietly drifted.

Sampling a mixed delivery, and the trap of averaging

If a delivery contains material from several lots, the lot identities must be separated before sampling. The reason is statistical rather than procedural: a sample drawn across two dye lots produces a defect rate that is the weighted average of the two, and that number tells you about neither. It passes when both are acceptable and fails when both are bad, and in the common case where one is acceptable and one is not, it produces a result that is wrong in whichever direction is more convenient to interpret.

The correct sequence is: identify the lots from the carton marks, physically separate them, sample each lot against its own plan, and report each lot separately. Where the goods have already been mixed and cannot be separated, the sampling has to be increased to give a defined confidence level for the population as a whole, and the report has to state plainly that the result applies to the mixed delivery and not to any identifiable sub-lot. That is an acceptable position if it is declared, and an indefensible one if it is not.

Sampling effort: matching the plan to the risk

The right sampling intensity depends on the stability of the process and the stage of the programme, and the commonest error is applying a routine sample to something that is not routine.

For a stable repeat order of a single SKU with a characterised process, a modest sample with full attribute inspection on the characteristics that actually vary is appropriate, and additional effort is better spent on targeted checks - a colour measurement on the lot boundary, a registration check on the printed roll, a weight check on a converted bow - than on more units measured for less. For a new development, a restyle, a material substitution or a supplier change, the sampling should be deliberately more intensive, because the purpose is to characterise a process that is not yet characterised. And for a shipment leaving a factory where a recent failure occurred, the protocol should require an independent third-party inspection before the container is sealed, because the mill's own inspectors are necessarily working with an interest in the shipment passing.

The pre-shipment inspection report

The report is the record that the whole protocol produces, and its content determines how much it is worth in an argument. A report that says "inspected 50 pieces, passed AQL 2.5, no serious defects found" is nearly worthless, because it does not state which lot, how the sample was drawn, what the defect classification was, or what was inspected. A report that will hold up contains the lot identifiers and the number of lots in the shipment, the sample size and how it was drawn, the inspection standard and the defect classification used, the results by severity against the AQL, photographs of representative defects and of the pack-out, the name and date of the inspector, and the disposition and its basis.

One further point is worth stating because it is frequently misunderstood: the inspection has to cover the pack-out as it will ship. Inspecting three rolls drawn from the top of a carton is not a report on the delivery, because the material at the bottom of the carton is the material with the compression history and the moisture history. For a programme with a meaningful defect rate, the sampling plan should specify the positions within the pack-out from which units are drawn.

Handling a complaint after shipment

The sequence matters more than the analysis, and the first three steps are the ones most often skipped.

Capture the evidence before the goods come back. A purchase order number, the lot numbers read from the cartons, the delivery date, the customer, the date the complaint was raised, the photographs and a description of the defect. This can be done at the customer's receiving dock and takes a minute, and it is what makes the subsequent investigation possible.

Quarantine the remaining stock from the same lot, and do it before it reaches another customer. This is the step that determines whether one complaint becomes one complaint or several. A lot with a confirmed defect should be held at the buyer's dock, not placed into the next production run.

Then pull the evidence: the retained sample for that lot, the mill's production records covering the dye lot, loom run and finishing batch, and the inspection report issued at release. With those three things, the question of whether the material was correct at the mill and changed afterwards, or was never correct, is answerable. Without them, it is a matter of opinion.

If the defect is confirmed, the disposition follows from the scale of the problem rather than preceding it. A defect confined to one sub-lot with a recoverable quantity is a sorting and credit conversation. A defect that characterises the lot is a concession, a claim or a return, and it should also trigger a review of the sampling plan that allowed the lot to be released in the first place. Every confirmed quality escape is evidence about the release protocol, not only about the ribbon.

Working with the mill

The mill's role in this system is specific and worth asking about explicitly. A mill that maintains lot identity properly can tell you, when a customer complains, that the affected material came from a single dye lot woven on one loom in a single week, and can therefore tell you how much material shares the fault and how much does not. A mill that does not maintain lot identity can only tell you that the material came off a shipment, which is the same as telling you nothing.

The questions worth asking are specific: what is your lot definition, how is a lot marked on the goods, how long are retained samples kept and under what conditions, what does your release certificate contain, and can we see the production and inspection records for a specific lot if we need to. The answers reveal whether the quality system exists or whether it is a set of habits.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between an AQL number and a sampling plan?

An AQL number is a single parameter inside a sampling plan, and on its own it controls nothing. It says how many defects a lot is permitted to contain at a given severity, expressed as a percentage or per unit. It does not say how many units get inspected, how the sample is drawn from the lot, what counts as a defect at each severity level, or what happens when the sample fails. A usable plan needs all of those: lot definition, sample size, inspection level, the AQL for critical, major and minor defects, the defect classification rules, and the disposition rule. Most of the disputes over ribbon quality that buyers describe as an AQL argument are actually disagreements about defect classification - whether a slightly off-centre bow is major or minor, for instance.

How should a ribbon lot be defined?

A lot should be the smallest quantity of material that was produced under a single set of conditions that could plausibly be treated as homogeneous, and that can be physically separated from every other lot. For woven ribbon the natural boundaries are the dye lot, the loom run and the finishing batch. If a colour was dyed across four dye lots and those four lots were woven on three different looms over two weeks, then the delivery is not one lot in any meaningful sense, and it should not be sampled as one. The practical consequence is straightforward: the more finely a lot is defined, the more precisely a failure can be isolated, but also the more lot labels the mill has to manage. For most programmes a sensible definition is dye lot plus loom run plus finishing date.

What is a retained sample for, and how long should it be kept?

It is the only physical evidence that survives the argument. When a complaint arrives six months after a delivery and asks whether the colour was correct when it left the mill, the retained sample is the answer. The one complication is that a ribbon sample is not a stable artefact - it is a textile and it will continue to change under ambient conditions, so a sample kept in an uncontrolled warehouse will gradually diverge from the goods it represents and will lose much of its evidential value. The remedy is to keep retained samples in a controlled environment, folded in the standard way with the fold protected, at a stated temperature and humidity, and to note the conditioning conditions alongside the sample. Retention should run at least as long as the programme, and for a repeat-order programme it should be longer, because a repeat order against a two-year-old sample is where a drift dispute will actually happen.

How do mill release and buyer release differ?

They are different decisions with different consequences, and conflating them is a common source of friction. Mill release is the mill's own statement that the lot meets the agreed specification and that the mill accepts responsibility for it. Buyer release is the buyer's acceptance of the lot into its own inventory, which carries the buyer's own downstream obligations to its customers and to regulators. A mill release that is treated as a buyer release removes the buyer's opportunity to apply its own criteria, and a buyer release that is assumed to have been given removes the mill's ability to prove conformance. The clean arrangement is two documents: the mill's release certificate and inspection report, and the buyer's own inspection record with its disposition.

What should a concession contain?

A concession is an acceptance of material that does not meet the specification, and it should be a written record rather than a verbal agreement. It needs to identify the specific lot or delivery and the specific deviation, state which specification clause is not met, describe the actual measured result against the required limit, record the quantity affected, and record who authorised the acceptance and on what basis - typically that the deviation is not visible in the finished product, or is within an agreed commercial tolerance. Concessions should also carry an expiry or a review date, because a concession granted for a specific shipment should not silently become standing practice on the next one. A concession granted once without a record is how a quality standard quietly disappears.

How should we sample a mixed delivery?

Do not sample it as one lot. This is the most common technical error in ribbon inspection, and it produces results that are simultaneously too lenient and too harsh. If a delivery contains material from several mill lots, the lot identifiers need to be separated before sampling, because a production run that combines two dye lots will have a defect rate that is the average of the two and tells you nothing about either. Where physical separation is not possible, the sampling plan should be increased to give a defined confidence level across the combined population, and the buyer should be told explicitly in the inspection report that the result applies to the delivery as a whole rather than to any identifiable sub-lot. If the material will go into a product where lot identity matters for traceability, the separation has to happen before the shipment leaves the mill.

How many units should we inspect on a routine ribbon order?

It depends on what you are protecting against, and the honest answer is that the AQL tables assume a lot size and a defect rate that many ribbon programmes never reach. For a routine repeat order of a single SKU where the process is stable and the programme is well characterised, a modest sample with full attribute inspection is usually appropriate, and the effort is better spent on the characteristics that actually vary - colour, width, print registration, bow geometry. For a new development, a restyle, a material change or a supplier change, the sampling should be more intensive because the process is not yet characterised and the whole point is to characterise it. The mistake to avoid is applying a routine sample to a development order, or a development sample to a routine order.

What should a pre-shipment inspection report contain?

At minimum: the lot identifiers and how many lots the shipment contains, the sample drawn and how it was drawn, the inspection standard applied, the defect classification used, the results by severity level against the AQL, photographs of any defect found, the name and date of the inspector, and the disposition. It should also state the pack-out configuration that was inspected, because a report that inspected three rolls pulled from the top of the carton is not a report on the delivery. For higher-risk shipments, an independent third-party inspection at the mill is worth the cost, because the mill's own inspection is necessarily performed by people who want the shipment to pass. The value of third-party inspection is not that it is more thorough - it is that it is independent, and that it happens before the container is sealed.

How do we handle a quality complaint once the goods have shipped?

The response time matters more than the response itself. Capture the complaint detail before the goods are returned - the purchase order, the lot numbers on the cartons, the delivery date, the customer, the photographs, the date the complaint was raised. Then quarantine the remaining stock from the same lot before it reaches a customer, which is the step most often skipped and the one that turns one complaint into several. Pull the retained sample and the mill's production and inspection records for the identified lot. If the defect is real, the disposition depends on whether the affected quantity can be sorted out at your end or whether the whole lot is affected, and the commercial conversation follows from that rather than preceding it.
