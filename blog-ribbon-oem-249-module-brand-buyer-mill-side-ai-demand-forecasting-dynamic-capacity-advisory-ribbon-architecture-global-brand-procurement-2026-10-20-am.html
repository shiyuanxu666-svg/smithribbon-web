2. The 6-Stage Forecasting Cascade

The 249-module architecture stages every forecast through six ordered stages. Each stage is a single-purpose model artifact with a hard-exit accuracy gate.

StageModel ArtifactOwner
1. Consensus-DemandBrand buyer submitted 12-month rolling forecastBrand S&OP
2. Statistical-BaselineExponential smoothing + seasonal decompositionMill Planning
3. Causal-FeatureEvent, promo-calendar, tariff, and FX regressorsMill Analytics
4. ML EnsembleGradient-boosted + quantile-regression blendMill Analytics

The fifth stage — capacity-constrained feasibility — is the structural lever and the part most mills omit. A forecast that exceeds real loom capacity is not a forecast; it is a promise the mill cannot keep. A brand buyer should be able to see, before the booking window closes, which forecasted lines are capacity-feasible and which require a slot-reservation decision. Without 249-module 6-stage architecture, forecasts live in a spreadsheet and the feasibility conversation happens after the fact.

4. The 4-Pillar Forecast-Governance Layer

The 249-module governance layer ensures that every forecast number is traceable, bias-corrected, and auditable. Four pillars:

  1. Snapshot-Immutability Pillar: Every forecast snapshot is hash-stamped at issue; no number is edited after the brand buyer has seen it. Corrections are issued as a new versioned snapshot.
  2. Bias-Correction Pillar: Every forecast is scored against actuals at 4, 8, and 12 weeks; systematic over- or under-forecast is auto-corrected in the next cycle.
  3. Confidence-Banding Pillar: Every forecast is published as a P10 / P50 / P90 band rather than a single point, so brand buyers plan against a range with stated probability.
  4. Retention Pillar: Forecast snapshots, accuracy scores, and advisory records are retained 7-10 years per brand-buyer-program requirement and CBAM/CSRD evidence-of-record rule.

8. Frequently Asked Questions

8.1 Is mill-side AI demand forecasting mandatory for ribbon OEM programs in 2026?

It is not regulatory-mandatory, but Tier-1 retailers, beauty, and confectionery brands in the EU, UK, and US are inserting forecast-sharing and capacity-advisory clauses into 2026-2027 multi-year framework agreements. 8.2 What is the difference between flat-1-signal forecasting and the 249-module 6-stage cascade?

Flat-1-signal forecasting produces a single point estimate from one model with no reconciliation and no confidence band. The 249-module cascade runs consensus demand, a statistical baseline, a causal-feature layer, an ML ensemble, a capacity-constrained feasibility clip, and a purchase-order signal — each with a hard-exit gate. It publishes a P10 / P50 / P90 band rather than a single number, so the brand buyer plans against a stated probability.

8.3 Why does capacity-constrained feasibility matter so much?

Because an infeasible forecast creates a promise the mill cannot keep, which converts directly into expedited freight, air-freight substitutions, and brand-buyer penalty exposure. Clipping the forecast to real loom, dye, and finish capacity before it reaches the brand buyer is the difference between a planning tool and a liability. In the 249-module engine the advisory is explicit: Capacity-Confirmed, Capacity-Tight, Capacity-Constrained, Capacity-Shortfall, or Capacity-Surplus.

8.5 How does 249-module forecasting interact with a capacity-lock pre-booking program?

Forecast and capacity lock are complementary layers. The forecast tells the mill what is likely; the capacity lock reserves the loom slot against it. Smith Ribbon pairs 249-module forecasting with the 4-tier capacity-cascade pre-booking architecture so a brand buyer can forecast at Tier-6 granularity and lock Tier-3 slots without over-committing loom capacity.
