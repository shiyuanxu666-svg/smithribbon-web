Ribbon Fibre Content Declaration & Care Labelling — EU Regulation 1007/2011 and the US FTC Textile Rules for Trim (2026)

Published: 2026-11-06  |  Updated: 2026-11-06  |  By Xiamen Smith Ribbon & Bow Co., Ltd.  |  Since 2004

Core principle: ribbon is a textile. Whether it must carry a fibre-content declaration and a care label depends on how it is supplied and sold, not on the fact that it is trim. Get that single distinction right and most labelling questions answer themselves — get it wrong and the problem surfaces as a border detention, a relabelling invoice or a retailer audit finding rather than as a conversation with your supplier.

Why Ribbon Gets Pulled Into Textile Labelling Rules

Ribbon is not a garment. It is not bedding. Most buyers treat it as decorative trim and assume it sits outside the fibre-content and care-labelling regime that governs textiles. That assumption is the source of an expensive and entirely avoidable category of compliance failure — detention, relabelling, retailer audit findings and, in the EU, recalls.

The reality is that ribbon is a textile. It is made from textile fibres, in textile constructions, and it is sold to end consumers attached to products that carry textile labelling obligations. Whether it must carry a fibre-content declaration or a care label depends on how it is used, how it is supplied and where it is sold — and the answer is different for a narrow 6 mm decorative cord sold as a component to a gift-box assembler, versus a 40 mm ribbon supplied retail-ready and hung on a card.

This guide works through the actual rules: what triggers a labelling obligation, what the specific EU and US regimes require, what the labels physically look like in practice, and how to build a specification that satisfies the requirement without creating a new operational burden every time a buyer changes a colour.

Which Rules Actually Apply to Ribbon

Two regimes matter commercially, and they are structured differently.

The EU regime: Regulation (EU) No 1007/2011

Regulation 1007/2011 governs textile products placed on the EU market. Its core requirement is straightforward: where a textile product carries a fibre-content label, the fibre composition must be specified by weight percentage for each fibre type, and the label must state the percentage of each fibre in descending order. Where two or more fibres are present, the percentages must add to 100%.

The regulation also defines what counts as a textile product, and this is where ribbon becomes interesting. A product is treated as a textile product if it contains at least 80% textile fibres by weight, or at least 80% of the surface area is textile. Ribbon typically contains 100% textile fibre by weight, so it falls squarely inside the definition.

The crucial point is how the obligation is triggered. Article 14 requires the label to be present when the textile product is made available on the market. There is a practical exemption path: a textile product is not required to bear a fibre-content label where it is clearly identifiable as a textile product by touch and appearance, and it is part of a composite product containing at least two other materials, and and the product is not separately marketed.

That exemption is narrow. Ribbon supplied retail-ready — wound on a card, in a header bag, or as a separate hanging item in a gift shop — is separately marketed and the exemption does not apply. Ribbon supplied as a component for a manufacturer to sew into a product is frequently within the exemption, provided it is not separately marketed and is identifiable by touch and appearance.

The US regime: FTC Textile Fiber Products Identification Act and the Care Labeling Rule

In the United States the requirements come from the FTC. The Textile Fiber Products Identification Act requires that textile products be labelled with the fiber content, the country of origin and the identity of the manufacturer or distributor, in English, in a prominent and legible position on the product or on a securely attached label. If a textile product is not labelled as required, the FTC considers it a deceptive practice.

The Care Labeling Rule (16 CFR Part 423) is separate and applies to textile products covered by the Act and by the Wool Products Labeling Act. It requires a clear and permanent care label stating washing, bleaching, drying, ironing and dry-cleaning instructions, based on the product’s actual performance, and instructions must appear in English and in the language of any other country in which the product is sold.

The critical US point for ribbon suppliers: if the buyer intends the ribbon to be sold as a consumer textile product with a care instruction, the ribbon needs to arrive with a tested, accurate care instruction attached. A ribbon that cannot be washed, bleached or ironed without damage needs a care label saying so — and it needs one that is tested, not guessed.

The Practical Trigger: How to Decide What Your Ribbon Needs

Do not start with the regulation. Start with three questions about the end use.

Question 1: Is the ribbon separately marketed to the end consumer?

This single question determines most of the answer, and it is a question the supplier can only help with — because it depends on how you are going to supply it.

  • Separately marketed (labelling required). Retail-ready ribbon on a header card, ribbon sold in a gift bag or as a standalone seasonal item, ribbon sold as a trim pack for craft or florist retail, ribbon included as a visible accessory in a boxed gift set where the consumer can handle it independently.
  • Component supply (exemption usually available). Ribbon supplied on a spool or in bulk to a manufacturer who incorporates it into a larger product that carries its own fibre-content declaration for the finished article.

The distinction in practice is not whether the ribbon ends up on a retail shelf as part of something else. It is whether the ribbon is packaged, presented or sold as a distinct article in its own right. Bulk spools going into a factory are components. A carded ribbon hanging on a peg is a product.

Question 2: What is the fibre composition, and can it be declared accurately?

You cannot declare what you cannot verify. The composition declared on the label must match the actual fibre content by weight. This matters for ribbon because it is frequently a blend: polyester core with a metallic or glitter film, a cotton core with a polyester face, a paper or polypropylene core inside a braided construction, a bamboo viscose blend, or an RPET content that must be declared as such rather than as ordinary polyester.

Where the construction genuinely contains multiple fibres — a braided ribbon with a metallic film, a mesh ribbon with a film backing, a ribbon with a wired or paper core — the percentages must reflect the finished article by weight, and the core fibre must be included in the declaration.

Question 3: Will the end consumer wash, iron or dry-clean it?

A care label describes what happens when the consumer follows it. For ribbon this is more specific than it sounds, because ribbon behaves differently from the fabric it decorates.

  • Glitter and metallic film ribbon. Metallic pigment and glitter are abraded by friction and heat. This usually cannot be washed or ironed without loss of effect.
  • Heat-transfer and foil-printed ribbon. Foil and transfer-printed designs can distort, crack, discolour or lift under heat and agitation. The care instruction has to reflect the decoration, not just the ground fabric.
  • Velvet and flock ribbon. Pile and flock respond poorly to crushing and heat. Pile direction is also relevant, because reversing the ribbon changes the visual appearance.
  • Organza, tulle and sheer ribbon. Delicate constructions need a gentler instruction than a heavy satin, and in some cases the correct instruction is hand wash only.

What the Labels Look Like in Practice

The EU fibre-content label

The mandatory content is: the percentage of each fibre by weight in descending order, the fibre name, and for a single-fibre product, the word "pure" may be used if the product contains one fibre only. The label must be legible, indelible, and easily visible. Where the label is not visible at the point of sale, it must be attached to the product or be visible in the sales presentation.

There is no mandatory format, which gives buyers latitude. Most buyers use a printed satin or polyester tag sewn or stapled to the card, or a printed card back. What matters is that the information is present, legible and in the language required for the market of sale — and for multi-market distribution, that means producing per-language label versions rather than one universal label.

The US fibre-content and country-of-origin label

For the US, the label needs the fibre content, the country of origin of the finished product, and the name and address of the manufacturer, distributor or retailer. "Made in China" satisfies origin if the ribbon was woven and finished there. If ribbon is woven in China and decorated or finished in another country, the origin rule for the finished product depends on what constitutes substantial transformation — decoration alone is not usually sufficient, but this is a classification question worth getting right rather than assuming.

The care label

The care instruction must cover, where applicable, washing, bleaching, drying, ironing and dry cleaning, with the symbols or the full written instruction. The FTC requires that instructions be based on the actual performance of the product — a label that says "machine washable" when the ribbon curls, runs or loses its metallic effect is a liability, not a benefit.

The four-factor rule for a ribbon care instruction

Every ribbon care label has to survive four things at once: the fibre (does it stretch, shrink or bleed), the construction (is it sheer, wired, elastic or heavy), the decoration (foil, glitter, flock, heat transfer, embroidery) and the end use (does it sit on a gift that gets handled, or inside a garment that gets washed). A label that only accounts for the fibre will be wrong for most decorated ribbon.

Building a Labelling Specification That Survives a Change

The operational nightmare for a supplier is not one label. It is re-approving the label every time a buyer changes a colour, a width or a decoration. A specification built the following way removes most of that friction.

Step 1: Define the label variants, not the label

Agree up front which distinct label variants exist. In practice a programme of any size needs at most a small number: for example, "100% polyester, carded" and "100% polyester, bulk spool" and "polyester blend with metallic film". Each variant is a fixed printed item the factory stocks. Changing a colour or a width then needs no label change at all, because the label does not reference the colour — it references the fibre content and the presentation mode.

Step 2: Tie the fibre declaration to the construction, not the SKU

Fibre content is a property of the construction and the material, not of the colourway. If you build a matrix of constructions against declared compositions and freeze it, then every future colour added to an existing construction inherits a pre-approved declaration. This is the single most useful thing you can do to make labelling scale, and it turns a per-order artwork task into a per-construction task.

Step 3: Fix the presentation modes early

Decide, at programme level, how ribbon is presented: bulk on a spool for manufacturing, carded for retail, header-bagged for seasonal, cut to length for gift. Presentation mode determines whether a fibre-content label is required, and fixing it once prevents a stream of small decisions that each introduce a compliance question.

Step 4: Get the care instruction tested, not inferred

A care instruction should follow a defined test sequence — wash at the stated temperature with the stated detergent, dry as stated, iron as stated — and the label should state what was actually done. Where a construction cannot be tested meaningfully (metallic film that abrades, flock that crushes, wired edge that distorts), the conservative instruction is the correct one, and it should be documented as a deliberate choice rather than a default guess.

Step 5: Keep the evidence with the order

Keep the fibre composition verification, the test results and the label artwork version against the order number. When a retailer or a market surveillance authority asks in eighteen months what the ribbon was, who declared it and on what basis, a filed test report is the difference between an answer and an investigation.

Common Mistakes That Cause Detention and Recalls

  • Declaring 100% polyester when the construction contains a paper, polypropylene or metallic film core. The declaration must reflect the finished article by weight, including the core. This is one of the most frequently flagged issues in festive and gift-wrap ribbon.
  • Treating separately marketed ribbon as a component. If the ribbon is carded and sold on its own, the component exemption does not apply and a fibre-content label is required. Retro-labelling a retail-ready programme is expensive and slow.
  • Copying a care instruction from a garment fabric. A care label written for satin dress fabric is frequently wrong for glitter ribbon, foil-printed ribbon or flock ribbon, and it will be wrong in the direction of over-promising.
  • Using a single label for multiple EU markets. Mandatory information must be in the language of the Member State where the product is sold. A multi-country programme needs per-language label versions, which is a printing cost, not a design problem.
  • Losing the label at the packing station. The most common cause of a failed audit is not a wrong label but a missing one, because it was applied to the card before the ribbon was wound and the label was not carried through to the finished pack. Specify label application as a defined point in the packing sequence with a check.
  • Changing the fibre content without changing the label. A construction swap from polyester to RPET is a labelling event. RPET is not declared as ordinary polyester; it has its own designation, and the claim has to be substantiated rather than assumed from the supplier certificate.

Questions to Put in Your Ribbon Specification

  • What is the fibre composition of this construction by weight, including any core, film or metallic component?
  • Is this ribbon supplied carded, bagged or in bulk, and is it sold to the end consumer as a distinct item?
  • What care instruction has actually been tested on this construction and this decoration, and on what basis?
  • How many label variants exist in this programme, and which of them are held in stock versus printed per order?
  • Who signs off the label artwork, and what evidence is filed against it?
  • Can you supply a compliance dossier for the programme covering composition, care testing, material certifications and label artwork versions?

A supplier that answers all six of these clearly is one you can build a multi-market programme on. A supplier that answers only the first three will keep sending you artwork corrections every time the market changes.

A Note on "Decorative Trim" Arguments

You will occasionally hear a claim that decorative trim is exempt from textile labelling because it is not worn and not a household textile. Under the EU regime, the definition of textile product is broad and includes any product containing at least 80% textile fibres by weight. Ribbon qualifies, and the exemptions are narrow and conditional on separate marketing. The "decorative trim" argument does not hold as a general rule, and relying on it is how a programme ends up with a detention at the border rather than a conversation with the supplier.

Where a genuinely arguable case exists — for example, a technical ribbon with a plastic film construction that does not meet the 80% fibre threshold — it can be assessed case by case, with the composition documented. What cannot be done is to apply the argument across a range without checking it.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. Does ribbon sold to the EU need a fibre-content label?

It depends on whether it is separately marketed, not on the fact that it is ribbon. Under EU Regulation 1007/2011, a textile product is any product containing at least 80% textile fibres by weight, which ribbon almost always does. The exemption applies where a product is clearly identifiable as textile by touch and appearance, forms part of a composite product with at least two other materials, and is not separately marketed. Ribbon supplied carded on a peg, or in a header bag, or sold as a standalone trim pack is separately marketed and does need the label. Ribbon supplied in bulk on a spool for a manufacturer to incorporate into a larger product is generally within the exemption, provided it is not separately marketed.

2. What exactly must the EU fibre-content label state?

The fibre names and their percentages by weight, in descending order of weight, with the percentages totalling 100%. Where the product contains only one fibre, the word 'pure' may be used. The label must be legible, indelible and easily visible, or attached to the product where it is not visible at the point of sale. There is no mandatory label format, so buyers have latitude in design, but the mandatory information must be present and in the language of each market where the product is sold.

3. Does a ribbon care label need to be tested, or is an inferred instruction acceptable?

It should be tested, and in the US this is a legal requirement rather than good practice. The FTC Care Labeling Rule requires that instructions be based on the actual performance of the product, so a label stating 'machine washable' on a foil-printed or glitter ribbon that curls or loses its metallic effect is a liability. Because ribbon behaviour depends on fibre, construction, decoration and end use together, an instruction copied from a garment fabric is frequently wrong for ribbon and will over-promise.

4. How do I handle a ribbon range where each colour needs different labelling?

You should not need a different label per colour. Fibre content is a property of the construction and material, not of the colourway. Build a matrix of constructions against declared fibre compositions, freeze it, and every future colour added to an existing construction inherits a pre-approved declaration. This turns labelling from a per-order artwork task into a per-construction task, and it is the single biggest source of operational savings in a labelling programme.

5. What happens if the ribbon has a paper, polypropylene or metallic film core?

The declaration must reflect the finished article by weight and must include the core, film or metallic component. A metallic glitter or foil film ribbon that is declared as 100% polyester when a small percentage of the finished weight is a polyester or polypropylene film is a misdeclaration and a commonly flagged issue, particularly in festive and gift-wrap ribbon. This is one of the most frequent causes of a market-surveillance finding or a retailer audit observation, so it should be checked at construction sign-off rather than at label print.

6. Is one label acceptable for a programme selling into several EU markets?

Not if the language differs. Mandatory information must appear in the language of the Member State where the product is made available, so a multi-country European programme needs per-language label versions. This is a printing and stock-management cost rather than a design problem, and it is far cheaper to plan up front than to relabel finished stock that has already shipped to the wrong market.

7. What labelling documentation should I ask a ribbon supplier for?

Ask for a compliance dossier covering: the verified fibre composition by weight for each construction including any core or film component, the care test results supporting each care instruction, the material certifications such as OEKO-TEX or GRS where claimed, and the version history of the label artwork against the order or programme. The specific question to ask is whether the evidence is filed against the order number, because a supplier who can produce documentation for a two-year-old programme is a materially different supplier from one who can only produce a declaration for this month.

8. Does 'decorative trim' exempt ribbon from textile labelling rules?

Not as a general rule. The EU definition of textile product is based on fibre content by weight and is broad enough to cover ribbon, and the exemptions are narrow and conditional on the product not being separately marketed. There are genuine edge cases, such as a ribbon whose construction does not meet the 80% textile fibre threshold, which can be assessed case by case with the composition documented. What cannot be done is apply the decorative-trim argument across an entire range without checking each construction, because that is how a programme ends up facing a border detention rather than a conversation with the supplier.
