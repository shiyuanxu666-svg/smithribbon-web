Ribbon Payment Terms & Order Financing — Structuring T/T, Letter of Credit and Deposit Balances with an Overseas Ribbon Mill (2026)

Published: 2026-11-15  |  Updated: 2026-11-15  |  By Xiamen Smith Ribbon & Bow Co., Ltd.  |  Since 2004

Payment terms are not an accounting detail to be settled at the end of a negotiation. They determine who carries the working-capital cost of your ribbon programme, and for a brand buying continuously from an overseas mill, that cost compounds on every order. The right structure is the one that matches your order size, your cash cycle and the supplier's ability to absorb risk — and it is almost always negotiable, because most ribbon factories quote a standard term from a template rather than a term calculated for your business.

Most buyers negotiating with a ribbon factory spend all of their negotiating capital on price. That is understandable and usually worthwhile, but it can be a mistake, because payment terms carry a real and measurable cost that frequently exceeds the discount they fought for. A 3% discount on a single order is worth far less than a shift from 100% prepayment to 30% deposit and 70% against a shipping document, because the latter frees working capital on every order you will place for the next three years.

This guide is about how the standard instruments actually work with a ribbon mill, what each one costs, how the choice interacts with order size and risk, and how to negotiate a structure that both sides can live with. It is written for brand and procurement teams who are the buyer of record and who will carry the consequence of a payment structure that goes wrong.

Why Payment Terms Matter More Than the Price Discount

Consider a brand that buys ribbon continuously from a mill in Xiamen. Suppose the annual spend is 400,000 US dollars and the brand settles each order with 100% prepayment before production begins. That means the brand is funding the mill's entire production cycle for every order, on top of funding its own inventory.

Now suppose the same brand moves to 30% deposit on order and 70% against a bill of lading. The 30% funds the mill's raw material and dyehouse commitment, which is the point at which the mill's cash is genuinely exposed, and the 70% settles against a document that proves the goods have shipped. The brand's cash cycle shortens by roughly one production cycle on every order, and that working capital is redeployed into sales rather than sitting with the supplier.

The saving is not the sum of those two payments; it is the time value of the gap between the deposit and the final settlement. Buyers should also remember that payment terms are not purely financial. They are a risk allocation mechanism, and who bears the risk determines how much supplier assurance you need to get comfortable.

T/T in advance

Wire transfer against a proforma invoice, paid before the mill buys yarn. This is the right structure for a first order and for samples, and most experienced factories prefer it for a new customer because it removes any risk of non-payment. The problem is that it is structurally expensive for the buyer, because you are financing the supplier's entire production run and carrying all the risk of a quality dispute with the money already gone.

The sensible progression for most buyers is to start here and move to a deposit and balance structure once the relationship has a track record. A supplier who will not move to a deposit structure after several paid orders is telling you something useful about how they will behave when a dispute arises.

T/T deposit and balance

The most common structure in ribbon sourcing, and the one most buyers should be working towards. The deposit covers the mill's committed costs: yarn, dyehouse batches, setup materials and labour booking. The balance is settled against a shipping document, so the buyer has proof of shipment before the final payment leaves.

The two variables to negotiate are the deposit percentage and the point at which the balance falls due. A 30% deposit with the balance payable against the bill of lading is a standard and reasonable opening position. A 30% deposit with the balance payable before shipment gives the buyer more protection, because the money is not released until the goods are loaded, but it is less common for a buyer without an established relationship. Some mills will also accept a balance against inspection or against a bill of lading copy, which lets the buyer pay before the original documents arrive.

Letter of credit

A letter of credit is a bank undertaking. The buyer's bank promises to pay the mill's bank if the mill presents documents that comply with the terms written into the credit. Its real value is not the payment guarantee, which is what many buyers assume, but the documentary discipline it creates: both sides are working from a written, unambiguous specification of what must be produced, inspected, packed and shipped.

That is why L/C is often more valuable for a large first order or a new specification than for routine repeat business. It forces the commercial terms into a document that cannot drift during production. The costs are real and should be priced in: the buyer's bank charges for the credit, the mill's bank charges for the collection, the buyer must deposit a margin with the issuing bank, and every discrepancy between the documents and the credit terms can cause a refusal and a delay. A single missing document or a transposed number in a bill of lading can freeze the payment for weeks, so documentary precision matters more with an L/C than with any other instrument.

For most ribbon buyers, L/C is worth it on the first large order of a new specification, or where the order value is large enough that a failed delivery would be material. For ongoing repeat business in a stable specification, the cost is usually better spent on a well-drafted supply agreement.

D/P and D/A

Documents against payment means the bank releases the shipping documents, including the bill of lading, once the beneficiary's bank collects the payment. For the buyer this is useful because the bill of lading is a title document, so holding it means holding control of the goods. The problem is timing: the goods have already sailed. If the buyer inspects and rejects, recovering goods already at sea is expensive and slow.

Documents against acceptance is similar but the mill accepts a draft, so the buyer can defer payment to a maturity date, typically 30 to 90 days after shipment. That is genuine trade credit and it is the cheapest form of working-capital relief available in ribbon sourcing. The price is that the buyer is exposed to collection risk, and that risk is highest exactly where the risk is highest: with a supplier in a new relationship, in a jurisdiction with slow enforcement, or in a market where the currency is volatile.

Matching the Instrument to the Order

The instrument should follow the order size and the state of the relationship. A practical framework looks like this.

Two refinements are worth making at the top of this list. First, if the buyer can inspect before the goods leave the factory, the balance should be tied to inspection rather than to shipment, because inspection is the only point at which the buyer can still stop a problem costing freight. Second, where a buyer has a genuine, forecastable order history, the mill is often willing to discuss extended terms or D/A, because their risk on a customer with a clean payment record is low. The negotiating position for this is data: a twelve-month order history with no late payment is the strongest argument available.

What the Mill's Cash Cycle Actually Needs

Buyers sometimes negotiate payment terms as if they were simply asking the mill for free money. They are, in part, but the more productive framing is to understand where the mill's exposure sits, because a structure that covers the real exposure is easy to agree and a structure that ignores it is hard.

For a typical ribbon mill the cash commitments fall in this order. Yarn procurement and, in the case of custom-dyed colours, the dyehouse batch are committed first and are largely irrecoverable once incurred. Setup costs, including screens for printing or tooling for a specific construction, follow. Labour and weaving costs accrue through the production window and are partly variable. Packing materials, documentation and freight are relatively late and relatively small. Quality control and final inspection occur at the end, after most of the cost has already been committed.

A deposit that covers the first two items, which are the true out-of-pocket commitment, is convincing to a mill. A deposit that is much larger than that, asked for purely to improve the buyer's cash position, is harder to justify and usually attracts pushback. This is worth understanding because the most effective negotiation is one where the buyer can show that their proposed structure is tailored to the supplier's actual exposure rather than a generic template demand.

It also explains why the balance against a shipping document is such a widely accepted arrangement. By that point the mill has shipped the goods, the buyer has a bill of lading, and the mill's remaining exposure is small. The structure recognises where the risk actually is rather than pretending it is evenly distributed.

Negotiating Payment Terms: What Actually Moves

Buyers tend to negotiate price and then treat payment terms as a fait accompli. It is worth reversing that, because terms are often easier to move than price and the amount at stake can be larger.

  1. Lead with the order history, not the request. "We would like 30% deposit and 70% against documents" is a request. "We have ordered twelve times in the last year with payment on time every time, and we expect to double that volume next year" is a reason.
  2. Offer something in return. Terms are a two-sided negotiation. A longer commitment, a larger forward order, prompt payment on the balance, or accepting a shorter production window are all things the buyer can trade for better terms.
  3. Separate the deposit from the balance. Ask for a low deposit and a balance tied to documents. Many mills will accept 20% on order where they would resist 20% with the balance payable in advance.
  4. Ask for the balance against a bill of lading copy rather than the original. This lets the buyer release the balance earlier without giving up the right to inspect, and costs the mill nothing.
  5. Put the terms in the purchase order and the supply agreement, not in the email. Terms that live in a signed order and a supply agreement do not get reinterpreted when someone new takes over the account.
  6. Agree the currency and the FX treatment before the first order. A fixed currency for a quoted price and a defined window for validity removes an argument that otherwise appears at the worst possible moment.

Currency, Incoterms and the Hidden Cost

Payment terms and currency are usually negotiated separately and then interact. A quotation in CNY paid in USD exposes both sides to movement between quotation and settlement, and a buyer who does not address this will find the mill building in a currency cushion or refusing to hold a price for any period at all.

The cleaner approach is to agree the invoice currency, the payment currency and the validity window of the price as three explicit items. Where the buyer must pay in USD but the mill invoices in CNY, the conversion and its timing should be stated, not assumed. Some mills will quote in USD directly for export business, and this removes the question entirely.

The Incoterms rule determines what the quoted price already includes and therefore which costs remain the buyer's. Under EXW the buyer is responsible for nearly everything from the factory gate, including export clearance. Under FOB the mill handles export clearance and loading and the buyer takes it from the port of loading. Under CIF and DAP, additional cost elements are included. This matters for payment terms because the balance payment is usually triggered by a document that reflects one of these rules. A buyer asking for the balance against a bill of lading under an EXW quotation has asked for something the mill may not be able to produce in the form required, and the mismatch delays payment for everyone.

The Practical Checklist

  • Decide the instrument before the first order, because it sets the tone for everything that follows.
  • For a first order, expect to pay in advance and use it to build a track record rather than to optimise cash flow.
  • For repeat business, target 30% deposit with the balance against a bill of lading copy.
  • Where inspection before shipment matters, tie the balance to inspection rather than to shipment.
  • Use a letter of credit for the first large order of a new specification, and for any order where a failure would be material to your business.
  • Keep the documentation precise. Under L/C every discrepancy is a potential refusal and a delay.
  • Always use a proforma invoice, and check that it matches the purchase order, the contract and the agreed terms before you wire anything.
  • Verify the beneficiary account details through a separate channel. Payment redirection fraud is now common in manufacturing, and a supplier's real bank details can change without any warning.
  • Agree the invoice currency, the payment currency and the price validity window in writing.
  • Check that the Incoterms rule and the payment trigger are consistent, so the document the mill presents is the document your bank expects.
  • Record the agreed terms in both the purchase order and the supply agreement, so they survive a change of buyer or supplier contact.
  • Reconcile periodically rather than only when something goes wrong. Most payment disputes are administration failures, and a monthly reconciliation catches them early.

One point deserves emphasis because it is the most common avoidable loss in overseas sourcing. Never accept changed bank details communicated only by email, no matter how reasonable the explanation sounds and no matter how urgent the payment is. Send a request back to a contact number you already know, or to the contact details on the company registration, and confirm the change through a voice conversation. The cost of this check is minutes. The cost of skipping it is the entire order value.

Finally, treat payment terms as part of the relationship rather than as a tactical ask in each negotiation. A buyer who pays on time, communicates clearly and grows predictably will get better terms over time without ever making a difficult request, because the mill's perception of that buyer's risk falls steadily. Terms are one of the few advantages in this market that reward behaviour rather than negotiation skill, and they are available to any buyer willing to be reliable.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What are the standard payment terms when buying ribbon from a factory in China?

The most common structure is 30% deposit by T/T on order and 70% balance against a copy of the bill of lading or before shipment. Smaller trial orders and samples are usually paid 100% in advance. Larger or first-time orders are frequently handled by letter of credit at sight. A minority of mills will accept documents against payment or documents against acceptance, which provide deferred settlement, though this is less common for first orders because the supplier is carrying the risk. These are starting conventions rather than fixed rules, and experienced buyers negotiate improved terms once they have an established order history.

2. What is the difference between T/T, L/C, D/P and D/A?

T/T is a direct wire transfer between banks and is the simplest instrument, carrying no bank guarantee for either side. A letter of credit is a bank undertaking to pay on presentation of compliant documents, and its main benefit is that it forces both sides to work from an unambiguous written specification of what must be produced and shipped. D/P means documents are released against payment, so the buyer obtains control of the bill of lading but the goods have already sailed. D/A means documents are released against acceptance of a draft, which gives the buyer deferred payment but adds collection risk. For routine ribbon business, T/T with a deposit and balance is the normal choice; L/C earns its cost on the first large order of a new specification.

3. What deposit percentage is reasonable for a first ribbon order?

Thirty percent is the conventional opening position and most mills expect it on a new customer, though some will work with 20% and a small number will accept 50% for high-value or customised work. The economic logic is that the deposit should cover the mill's genuine out-of-pocket commitment, which is yarn procurement and dyehouse batches for custom colours, plus setup costs. Anything beyond that is negotiating position rather than risk cover. If a supplier insists on a higher deposit than your order justifies on a trial order, that is a question worth asking directly rather than accepting by default.

4. Should I use a letter of credit for a ribbon order?

Yes, for a first large order or for any order where a failure to deliver would be material to your business. The principal benefit is documentary discipline: the goods must conform to a written specification in the credit terms, which removes the ambiguity that otherwise appears during production, and both parties have a formal process for resolving discrepancies. The costs are bank charges on both sides, a margin you must deposit with your bank, and a real risk of delay if a document is inconsistent with the credit terms, so documentation must be precise. For ongoing repeat business on a stable specification, the cost is usually better spent on a well-drafted supply agreement instead.

5. How can I negotiate better payment terms with a ribbon supplier?

Lead with evidence rather than with the request. A twelve-month order history with no late payment, alongside a credible forward volume forecast, is the strongest argument available because it addresses the supplier's actual concern, which is whether they will be paid. Separate the deposit from the balance, and try to move the balance trigger from before shipment to against a bill of lading copy, which is cheap for the mill and costs the buyer almost nothing. Offer something in return, such as a longer commitment, prompt settlement of the balance, or acceptance of a shorter production window. And put the agreed terms in both the purchase order and the supply agreement so they are not reinterpreted later.

6. What should I check before making a payment to a ribbon supplier?

Check three things. First, that the proforma invoice matches your purchase order, the agreed specification and the agreed commercial terms, including currency and Incoterms rule. Second, that the beneficiary account details are correct, and verify any change of bank details through a separate channel using a contact number you already hold, because payment redirection fraud is now common in manufacturing and a genuine supplier can have its email compromised. Third, that the document which will trigger the balance payment is one the mill can actually issue under your chosen Incoterms and that your bank expects. Never accept changed bank details communicated only by email, however urgent the request appears.

7. Does the Incoterms rule affect how I pay for ribbon?

Yes, because the Incoterms rule determines which document the mill presents and when payment falls due. Under EXW the buyer is responsible for export clearance, so the mill may not be in a position to issue the bill of lading a buyer wants as a payment trigger. Under FOB the mill handles export clearance and loading, and the bill of lading is issued at the port of loading. Under CIF and DAP additional cost elements are included and the documentation set changes accordingly. Agreeing the Incoterms rule and the payment trigger together avoids a mismatch where the mill presents a document your bank does not accept, which delays payment and occasionally triggers bank charges on both sides.

8. Is it safe to pay a ribbon factory by bank transfer to a new account?

Not without independent verification. Changing beneficiary details by email is one of the most common fraud patterns in overseas manufacturing, and it is effective precisely because the request arrives inside an otherwise normal transaction, often with a plausible explanation and a genuine deadline attached. The correct response is to take the new details and verify them out of band, using a phone number from the supplier's website, their company registration, or a relationship contact you have used previously, and to confirm by voice before releasing funds. A supplier that objects to a brief verification call is telling you something you need to know before you transfer the money.

9. Should I ask for the balance to be paid before shipment or against documents?

Before shipment is more protective for the buyer, because the money does not leave until the goods are loaded and the mill has committed to shipping, and it is often achievable on a first order if the buyer is paying a meaningful deposit. Against a bill of lading copy is the common middle ground and is usually easy to agree, because the buyer still obtains proof of shipment and can settle before the original documents arrive. Where the buyer has the opportunity to inspect before the goods leave the factory, tying the balance to inspection is better still, since it is the only point at which a problem can still be stopped before freight and insurance costs have been incurred.
