Ribbon Colour Fastness Testing — How to Set a Colour Master Standard That Survives Washing, Rubbing and Sunlight 2026

Published: 2026-11-14  |  Updated: 2026-11-14  |  By Xiamen Smith Ribbon & Bow Co., Ltd.  |  Since 2004

The core principle: colour is the only ribbon attribute a customer notices before they know what the product is. A ribbon that holds its colour through a wash cycle, a retail display and a customer's hand is doing something a colour swatch on a sampling table never promised — and the difference is locked in by the fastness specification you write.

Why Colour Fastness Is a Specification, Not a Sample

Colour approval in ribbon sourcing usually happens in a meeting. A mill sends swatches, a buyer picks the closest one, a specification sheet records a colour name and a Pantone reference, and everyone moves on. The problem is that a swatch is a still image of a material in its best light, on the day it was made. It says nothing about what happens to that colour after it has been sewn onto a gift box, stacked under ten thousand other boxes in a warehouse, carried through an international supply chain in summer, unwrapped in a shop window under direct sunlight, and then handled by a customer whose hands are warm and slightly damp.

Colour fastness is the measurable property that covers those conditions. It describes how well the dye resists change under defined exposures — washing, rubbing, light, sweat, water, dry cleaning — and it is assessed against a numbered scale where a higher number means better resistance. For a brand buyer, the practical point is that fastness is a property of the dyed ribbon, not of the colour name. Two ribbons can share a colour reference and differ by three fastness grades, and only one of them will still look like the brand asset after six weeks on a shelf.

This guide covers which fastness tests actually matter for the applications ribbons end up in, how to build a colour master that works across factories and seasons, how to specify and test fastness in a way a mill can actually execute, and the tolerance discussions that prevent a dispute at goods-inward.

The Fastness Tests That Actually Matter, by Application

Ribbon is a broad product family, and a single fastness specification applied to all of it is wasteful in one direction and inadequate in another. What matters depends entirely on where the ribbon ends up and what it will be exposed to. The table below maps the common applications to the tests that govern them.

ApplicationGoverning Fastness TestsWhy It Matters
Garment trim, apparel facingWash fastness, rubbing fastness (dry and wet), sweat fastnessThe ribbon is laundered with the garment and sits against skin
Hair accessories, elastic, headbandsSweat fastness, wet rubbing, colour transfer to skinDirect, repeated skin contact with moisture present
Gift box decoration, ribbon bowsLight fastness, rubbing fastness (dry)Long shelf display in retail windows and seasonal point-of-sale
Florist and event trimLight fastness, wet rubbingExtended exposure to daylight and occasional moisture handling
Christmas and seasonal decorationsLight fastness, colour bleedStocked for months before a short sales window in lit retail
Scent bottle, cosmetics, fragrance trimColour transfer, alcohol and solvent resistanceContact with alcohol, perfume and oily cosmetic formulations
Pet accessories, toys, children's itemsWash fastness, saliva and sweat fastness, colour bleedContact with moisture and frequently washed
Home textile, cushion, curtain trimLight fastness, wash fastnessLong-term daylight exposure in the home environment

Two cross-cutting points. First, colour bleed — the transfer of dye onto adjacent material — is not strictly a fastness property but behaves like one, and it is the failure mode most likely to reach your customer as a complaint, because a dyed transfer mark on a white garment or a pale gift box reads as a manufacturing defect regardless of the underlying cause. Second, for printed or woven logos on a dyed ground, the fastness of the ground colour and the fastness of the print or weft-float are separate tests. A ribbon with a colour-fast ground and a fugitive printed logo is a common and avoidable specification mistake.

Building a Colour Master That Actually Governs Production

The single most common cause of colour drift across a brand's ribbon supply base is the absence of a physical colour master. Without one, the standard becomes "match the last delivery", and that standard drifts. Each new dye lot is adjusted against the previous one rather than against an absolute reference, so the error accumulates quietly in one direction until a shelf full of product is visibly off-brand and there is no record of when it happened.

A colour master is the approved physical reference against which every future lot is judged, independent of who dyes it, when, or on which machine. The process that holds up in practice has seven steps.

1. Agree the construction before the colour

Colour measured on a satin ribbon cannot be reliably compared with colour on a grosgrain or a velvet, because the surface geometry changes how light is reflected and how the eye integrates it. Fix the substrate, the weave, the width, the finish and the decoration method first. A colour master that does not specify the construction is a master for a colour in the abstract, which is precisely the thing you cannot buy.

2. Dye to a production standard, not a lab standard

The master swatch should come from a production-scale dye lot on production equipment, not from a small laboratory dip. Lab-dipped swatches are useful for screening recipes quickly, but they frequently shift in shade relative to production output, and a master built on a lab dip creates a false baseline that every production lot then appears to deviate from.

3. Evaluate under a defined light source

Colour matching performed under uncontrolled ambient light is a source of endless low-level disagreement. Specify a consistent light source — a daylight-equivalent viewing box, or a specified illuminant such as D65 — and hold the physical reference and the production sample at the same angle to it. This is also the condition under which your final instrument-based measurement should be taken, so that visual and numeric assessment describe the same thing.

4. Obtain written brand sign-off on the physical swatch

The approver must sign the physical reference, not a photograph of it and not a Pantone chip. Record who approved, on what date, and against which construction. This creates an unambiguous answer to the question that will eventually be asked: what was agreed?

5. Seal, label and split the master

Label the master with a unique identifier, the construction specification, the dye lot reference and the approval date. Seal it to prevent fading and contamination. Then keep two copies in two places — the buyer's office and the mill's quality department. A master held in only one location is a single point of failure, and the most likely place for it to be lost is the one that causes the dispute to become unresolvable.

6. Use it as the reference for every lot approval

Every incoming lot is compared against the master, not against the last delivery. If your own specification permits a tolerance, the tolerance band is measured from the master. This single change eliminates the accumulation problem entirely.

7. Retain, do not discard

Archived masters should be retained for the life of the programme plus the stated shelf life of the product, because a complaint raised two years after a seasonal programme shipped can only be defended if a physical reference still exists. Ribbon fades; so do archived swatches. Store them protected from light and re-verify them periodically against a retained reference.

Specifying Fastness So a Mill Can Execute It

A fastness specification that names a rating but not a method is not a specification. The same ribbon can produce different grades under different methods, different load conditions, different abradants and different cycle counts. To make the requirement actionable, state four things together: the test method, the rating scale, the pass threshold and the conditioning and sampling regime.

Specification ElementWhat It FixesExample of Acceptable Wording
Test methodWhich prescribed procedure is used, including abradant, cycle count and loadRubbing fastness per ISO 105-X12, dry and wet, specified load
Rating scaleWhich scale the result is expressed on, and which direction is betterISO 105-A02 grey scale; rating 4 or better acceptable
Pass thresholdThe numeric level the mill must meetMinimum rating 4 on the specified grey scale
Sampling and conditioningHow many samples, from where, tested under what conditionsThree samples per lot, conditioned 24 hours at standard temperature and humidity

Alongside these, the specification should require the mill to state the dye system in use and the rinse, soaping and heat-setting parameters for the construction. Fastness is largely determined at the dyeing and finishing stage, long before the ribbon reaches the decoration line, so those parameters belong in the technical agreement rather than being left to production judgement.

Running the tests in-house versus using a laboratory

Both are legitimate and they answer different questions. Mill-side testing is essential for in-process control: it catches a drifting dye bath before a full production lot is committed, and it lets the mill tune a recipe against the master quickly. For this to be meaningful, ask to see the method, the grey scale or instrument, the operator training records, and the calibration status of any measuring equipment.

Independent laboratory testing is the right instrument for a first approval on a new construction or dye system, for a dispute or a formal claim, and for anything you intend to show to your own customer as evidence. When commissioning a laboratory, specify the method reference, the sample quantity, the conditioning requirement and the reporting format, and require that the report states the method used rather than only the result. A report that gives a rating without naming the method is not usable as evidence.

A low-cost screening routine worth adding

You do not need a laboratory to catch most colour problems early. A simple screening set — a wet swatch rubbed against white cotton, a wet crocking check, and an inspection under daylight-equivalent light against the master — will detect the overwhelming majority of dye transfer and shade issues at the point of goods-inward, at essentially no cost, provided the inspector is trained and the procedure is written down. The screening test does not replace the formal test; it stops obvious problems from travelling further into your supply chain.

Why Lots Drift Even With a Fixed Specification

Buyers are sometimes surprised that two lots produced to an identical specification can differ visibly. A specification fixes inputs and process parameters, but several variables remain in the mill's control and not in the buyer's.

  • Dye lot variation. Different dye batches differ in strength and hue, and the dyer compensates by weight or volume. Small compensation errors produce visible shifts, particularly in saturated and dark shades.
  • Bath exhaustion across a long run. A dye bath weakens as it progresses. A mill running a single bath across a large order will produce a slight gradient across the run, which is why a large order sometimes shows visible change between the first and last rolls.
  • Water quality and hardness. Minerals and alkalinity in process water affect dye uptake and are a common cause of shade shift between two plants that both believe they are running the same recipe.
  • Drying, finishing and ironing conditions. Ambient humidity and temperature during drying, and the temperature and dwell of any heat-setting or finishing step, pull the colour to its final shade. Variation here is a frequent and underappreciated cause.
  • Substrate variation between runs. Yarn from a different batch or a different spinning run can shift the final shade, because the same dye behaves differently on a slightly different substrate.

The countermeasures are well understood: sequential dyeing against the master so that every production run is corrected toward an absolute reference rather than a relative one, controlled water quality, documented dye recipes, and stable finishing parameters. Accepting that a small residual drift exists is also part of the answer, which is why a tolerance band agreed in advance is more useful than an expectation of absolute perfection.

Agreeing the Tolerance Before the First Delivery

The tolerance conversation is much easier before a shipment has arrived than after it has been rejected. The practical approach is to agree a colour difference limit expressed as a delta E value, measured instrumentally under a stated illuminant, and to tie it to a visible assessment against the master. Widely used tolerances for saturated ribbon colours fall around a delta E of 2 to 3, with tighter expectations where multiple colours sit adjacent in a single set or where printed artwork sits on the dyed ground. For colours that will never sit next to one another and are consumed within weeks, a looser band has no commercial cost.

Two disciplines matter here. First, the tolerance should be agreed with the brand's design and quality functions, not imposed by procurement alone, because they are the ones who decide whether a delta E of 3 is visible on a finished pack. Second, the tolerance must be written into the purchase order or technical agreement, with a stated measurement condition, so that a claim at goods-inward is settled by a number rather than by argument.

Handling the borderline case

Occasionally a lot lands just outside the agreed band. The right process is a defined disposition path rather than an ad-hoc decision: measure, document, photograph under the standard viewing condition, and escalate to a pre-agreed decision-maker. If the deviation is accepted for a specific order, record that acceptance in writing with the reason. Otherwise a pattern develops in which every lot is negotiated, which erodes the value of having a tolerance at all.

Documentation That Lets You Answer Your Own Customer

Your customer will, sooner or later, ask for evidence of how the ribbon in their product was controlled. Being able to answer in minutes rather than weeks is a commercial advantage, and it comes from requiring a small, consistent document set with every delivery.

  • The colour master identifier and batch reference used for the lot.
  • The dye lot reference and the dyeing recipe revision.
  • A test report covering the agreed fastness methods, stating method, instrument or grey scale, result and pass or fail against the threshold.
  • A signed visual approval against the master, with the inspector name and date.
  • For OEKO-TEX certified programmes, the certificate reference and scope confirmation covering the actual material and the actual mill. A certificate that does not cover the site or the material being supplied provides no coverage at all.
  • The lot identity and traceability record linking the delivered quantity to the production run.

Note that these documents are far cheaper to collect at the first delivery of a programme than to reconstruct eighteen months later. The mill that has supplied you for three years will still hold the dye lot and test records for that period if the requirement was written into the agreement. The mill that has never been asked will not, and there is no retroactive version of the question.

A Practical Programme-Level Checklist

  • The construction is fixed and written before the colour master is created.
  • The master is a production-standard physical swatch, evaluated under a defined light source, with written brand sign-off.
  • Two sealed master copies exist, one at the buyer and one at the mill, both labelled with batch identifier and approval date.
  • Every incoming lot is compared against the master, never against the previous delivery.
  • Each fastness requirement states the method, the scale, the pass threshold and the sampling regime.
  • The mill documents dye system, rinse, soap and heat-setting parameters for the construction.
  • A written tolerance band in delta E exists, agreed with design and quality, with a stated measurement condition.
  • A written screening procedure runs at goods-inward, ahead of the formal test.
  • The full document set arrives with every delivery, and traceability links quantity to production run.
  • Archived masters are retained for the life of the programme plus the stated product shelf life.

Colour fastness is one of the few ribbon attributes where a small amount of specification discipline at the start eliminates a disproportionate amount of argument later. Write the method and the threshold down, hold a physical master in two places, and the conversation about shade stops being a matter of memory and becomes a matter of measurement.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is ribbon colour fastness and why does it matter for branded packaging?

Colour fastness describes how well a dyed ribbon holds its colour under exposure to washing, rubbing, light, sweat, water and dry cleaning. For branded packaging it matters because the colour is part of the brand promise: a ribbon that fades in sunlight on a retail display, transfers dye to a white garment in a wash cycle, or marks on a customer's hand represents a defect the end consumer experiences as a product fault. A ribbon that looks correct on the sampling table can behave completely differently after three weeks on a shop floor or one laundering cycle, which is why fastness has to be an agreed specification with a stated test method rather than a visual judgement.

2. Which colour fastness tests matter most for ribbon used in gift packaging?

Three usually dominate. Wash fastness matters wherever the packaged item or the ribbon itself may be laundered, or where ribbon is used on textiles such as apparel trim. Rubbing fastness, tested dry and wet, matters wherever the ribbon is handled, rubbed against skin or packed against a surface. Light fastness matters for any ribbon on display in daylight, including retail windows, seasonal point-of-sale and gift sets that stay unpacked on shelves. For hair accessories, sweat fastness and colour transfer to skin become the priority because the ribbon is in direct and repeated contact with skin and with wet hair.

3. What is a colour master and why does a brand buyer need one?

A colour master is the single approved physical reference that defines a colour for all future production, regardless of which factory, dye lot or season produces the ribbon. Without one, every order becomes a fresh negotiation, and 'match the last delivery' becomes the standard, which drifts in small increments each time until the difference is commercially significant. A colour master held centrally by the buyer, with signed approval before first production, turns an open-ended judgement into a measurable comparison against a physical reference.

4. How should a colour master be created and approved?

It should be a physical ribbon swatch from an agreed reference construction, not a print file or a screen colour. The process that works: agree the construction and finish first, dye a production-standard sample, evaluate it under a defined light source against the brand's colour reference, have the brand sign the physical swatch, then seal and label it with a batch identifier and keep it at both the buyer's and the mill's offices. Two copies in two places matters, because the point of the master is to survive a change of factory, a change of machine or a change of personnel.

5. How do you test colour fastness on ribbon practically, and what do the ratings mean?

The practical approach is to define a method and a pass threshold in the specification, then have it executed by a laboratory or by a trained inspector using standard textile test methods. Common methods include wash fastness by prescribed washing cycles with grey scale or colour transference assessment, rubbing fastness by dry and wet crocking with a specified abradant and load, and light fastness by exposure to defined light sources compared against a standard scale. Ratings are expressed on numbered scales where a higher number indicates better resistance to change. The key contractual point is that the method and the rating threshold must both be written down, because the same colour can produce different ratings under different methods.

6. What causes colour drift between production lots even with the same specification?

The specification controls inputs but not every variable in the process. Dye lot variation, dyeing bath exhaustion across a long run, water quality and hardness, ambient humidity and temperature during drying, the finishing and ironing step pulling colour into a slightly different shade, and differences in substrate between production runs all contribute. Sequential dyeing against a master, controlled water, and matching dye recipes are the practical countermeasures. Some drift is unavoidable, which is why a tolerance band agreed in advance is more useful than a demand for absolute perfection.

7. How much colour variation should a brand buyer accept between lots?

A reasonable commercial tolerance for a saturated colour on a woven or printed ribbon is usually expressed as a small delta E value, commonly around 2 to 3 measured under a standardised light source, with tighter expectations for colours that will sit adjacent to each other in a set or carry printed artwork on top. The tolerance should be agreed with the brand's design or quality function rather than imposed unilaterally, because for some colour and packaging combinations a tighter band has no commercial value and for others, such as multi-colour gift sets, it is essential. The important discipline is that tolerance is a negotiated, written number rather than a post-hoc judgement after a delivery has been rejected.

8. Should brand buyers use a third-party laboratory or the mill's own testing?

Both have a place, and they answer different questions. The mill's own testing is useful for in-process control and for fast screening of a new dye recipe, provided the mill can show method, equipment and operator competence. An independent laboratory is the right choice for a first approval on a new construction, for a dispute, and for any claim you intend to make to a customer of your own. Ask specifically for the test method reference, the grey scale or instrument used, the sample conditioning, and the pass or fail threshold agreed against it.

9. How can colour bleed or dye transfer problems be prevented on ribbon?

Dye transfer usually points to insufficient rinsing after dyeing, an unsuitable disperse or reactive dye system for the substrate, or a finish that has not been heat-set. On the specification side, ask your supplier for the rinse and soaping steps, the heat-setting temperature, and the substantivity of the dye system against the specific fibre. A simple white-cotton bleed test on a wet swatch, followed by a wet crocking test, catches most problems early and cheaply before they reach a packaged product.

10. What documentation should a brand buyer require with each ribbon delivery?

At minimum: the colour master reference and batch identifier used, the dye lot reference, a test report covering fastness on the agreed methods, and a visual approval against the master signed by an authorised inspector. For OEKO-TEX certified programmes, the transaction certificate or scope certificate reference should be present and should cover the actual material and the actual mill. These documents are what allow your own customer to audit you, and they are far cheaper to require at the first delivery than to reconstruct after a recall.
