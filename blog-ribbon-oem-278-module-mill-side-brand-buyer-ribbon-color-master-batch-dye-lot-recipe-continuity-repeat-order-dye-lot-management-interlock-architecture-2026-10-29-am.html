Mill-Side Brand-Buyer Ribbon Colour Master Batch, Dye Lot Recipe Continuity & Repeat-Order Dye Lot Management Interlock Architecture (2026)

B2B Sourcing · Module 278 · 2026-10-29

A mill-side brand-buyer guide to ribbon colour master batch, dye lot recipe continuity and repeat-order dye lot management. Covers why a colour approved at first sample drifts by the fourth reorder, how dye lot recipe locking works across a programme, what a colour master batch actually is and why it must be retained, how to set an acceptable delta E tolerance that survives lighting, how to handle a change in raw material, dye house or finishing route without losing the approved colour, and how to keep lot traceability from roll to finished component.

The gap between a roll and a colour that holds

Any brand buyer who has run a ribbon programme for more than one reorder cycle already knows the pattern. The first sample matches. The first production lot matches. The colour is approved, the programme is launched, the brand orders again in eight months, and the new lot is a shade that is technically the same colour code and visibly not the same colour.

This is not a mill quality failure and it is not usually a brand team's failure either. It is the predictable consequence of specifying colour as a reference rather than as a recipe. A Pantone number, a physical swatch or a hex value describes what the colour should look like. It does not describe the combination of dye, substrate, bath chemistry, temperature, dwell time, finishing route and machine that produced it. When any one of those variables moves, the output moves with it — and the output moves by an amount that is invisible on a paper reference and obvious on two rolls side by side.

The commercial consequence is larger than the aesthetic one. A colour that drifts between reorder cycles means the brand's packaging changes across its own product line, which is exactly the kind of thing a brand buyer cannot explain to a retail customer. It also means that the approved sample, which is the legal and commercial reference for the whole programme, stops being representative of what is actually shipping.

The fix is not to demand a more accurate colourimeter reading on every roll. It is to change what is being locked, so that the next lot is produced by the same recipe rather than by a fresh attempt at the same target.

What a colour master batch actually is

A colour master batch is the retained, fully compounded portion of dye — pigment, carrier and the additives that govern performance — that was used to produce the approved colour. Master batches are typically compounded by a master batch producer or a specialist compounder, then supplied to the ribbon dye house in a concentrated form that is dosed into the dye bath at a defined percentage.

Three properties make a master batch the linchpin of colour continuity.

1. It removes the compounding variability from the dye house. When a mill mixes its own dye bath from loose pigment, the bath is subject to weighing error, dispersion variability and the condition of the mixing equipment. Two batches mixed to the same nominal formula from the same nominal pigment can differ measurably. A dosed master batch removes most of that variation, because the dispersion is controlled once, upstream, and is delivered in a pre-dispersed form.

2. It can be retained and re-dosed indefinitely. This is the part that matters most for a repeat-order programme. A master batch that was used to produce the approved lot can be stored and used to produce the lot twenty-four months later. If the programme is specified with master batch retention as a requirement, the dye house is not re-creating the colour at reorder — it is re-running a chemistry that has already been validated.

3. It carries the performance properties with the colour. Rub fastness, light fastness, wash fastness and the behaviour of the colour under a particular finishing route are properties of the dye system, not of the colour appearance alone. A master batch locks those properties alongside the shade, so a reorder that matches on colour also matches on fastness.

When a brand buyer asks a mill whether the programme will be re-ordered on the same colour, the useful question is more specific: will the same master batch be used, and can you confirm the master batch reference on each production lot? A mill that cannot answer that is re-matching the colour by eye and recipe each time, which is precisely the failure mode described above.

Why recipe continuity is the actual requirement

Continuity is not a single property. It is the outcome of a set of upstream variables that must be held together as a set. A buyer who understands which variables actually move the colour will write a better specification than one who simply asks for "the same colour".

  • Master batch reference and lot number. The identity of the compounded dye and the lot within it. This is the primary key and it should appear on the lot documentation for every reorder.
  • Substrate specification. The fibre, the yarn count, the weave construction and any finish applied before dyeing. A colour matched on a different substrate or a different yarn count is a different colour, regardless of the dye used.
  • Substrate supplier and yarn lot. Where the fibre source can vary — and in a recycled or blended programme it usually can — the acceptable variation and the reconfirmation trigger should be defined in advance rather than discovered after the dye lot has been produced.
  • Dye house and machine. Different machines run different baths, different heating profiles and different liquor ratios. A colour that has been approved on one machine should be re-qualified if the programme moves to another, and the re-qualification should be a documented step rather than an assumption.
  • Bath chemistry and additive pack. The pH, the levelling and migration agents, the shading component, the dye-exhaustion profile. These are part of the recipe even though they are rarely written down in a buyer specification.
  • Temperature, dwell time and liquor ratio. The three variables with the largest direct effect on the final shade and the most practical variation between production runs.
  • Finishing route. Washing, heat-setting, calendaring, coating and finishing all shift the final appearance. A colour approved on an unfinished ribbon and a colour approved on a finished ribbon are two different approvals, and this is the most common source of a dispute at the final inspection stage.

The practical specification for a repeat-order programme is therefore a colour recipe record: a document that names all seven of the above and is version-controlled. Each production lot is produced against a version of that record, and any proposed deviation is treated as a change requiring re-approval rather than as a production decision.

Setting a delta E tolerance that survives the lighting

Delta E is the standard measure of colour difference, and it is the right instrument, but a raw delta E number on a spec sheet is meaningless without a measurement condition and a viewing condition. This is where most ribbon specifications go wrong, because they quote a number without specifying how it was obtained.

The measurement conditions. A colorimeter reading depends on the illuminant used, the aperture geometry and the observer. Two people reading the same ribbon with two different instruments can produce different delta E values for an identical pair. A specification that says "delta E ≤ 1.5" without stating "under D65, 10° standard observer, 8 mm aperture" is not a specification.

The viewing conditions. This is the part that matters commercially. A ribbon that measures delta E 1.2 against the approved swatch can still be obviously wrong to a customer holding it in a shop under 3000K light, because the two product colours are being viewed under a different illuminant than the one used in the laboratory. Metamerism — the phenomenon where two samples match under one light source and not under another — is entirely normal in textiles and cannot be eliminated, only managed.

The recommendation is a three-condition acceptance protocol rather than a single number.

  • Instrumental gate. A delta E limit against the retained master swatch, measured under a stated illuminant, aperture and observer. This catches drift early and cheaply, and it is the condition under which most lots should be accepted.
  • Daylight visual gate. A visual comparison under a 5600K daylight-balanced source, which is the reference condition for product photography and for most e-commerce rendering.
  • Retail visual gate. A visual comparison under the actual fixture lighting of the market the product will sell into. This is the condition that catches metameric failures and deep-tone saturation loss, and it is the one most often skipped.

It is also worth specifying a visual pass/fail band for the retail gate rather than a delta E number, because at retail the judgement is categorical: does this lot look like the approved one, yes or no. A buyer who trains a shop-floor or QA team to recognise "not the same shade” catches more drift than a delta E limit alone.

Handling a change in raw material, dye house or finishing route

Programmes change. A yarn supplier changes, a dye house closes a machine, a finishing route is upgraded, a customer moves the programme to a second mill for continuity. Each of these is legitimate and each of them can silently break the colour.

The principle is simple: no change is a production decision; every change is a re-approval.

Four change categories, in ascending order of risk:

1. Administrative change. A change in packaging format, labelling, shipping configuration or documentation. No colour impact. A records update is sufficient, but the lot must still carry the master batch reference and the recipe record version.

2. Process-equivalent change. A change that is known to be colour-neutral by prior qualification, such as a machine of the same type running the same recipe in a different dye house that has been qualified against the same master batch. A written confirmation of equivalence and a first-lot visual gate is sufficient.

3. Qualified deviation. A change that is expected to be small but not zero — a different yarn supplier within a defined tolerance, a minor bath chemistry adjustment, a different finishing temperature inside the qualified band. This requires a laboratory dip against the retained swatch, followed by instrumental measurement across all three acceptance conditions, and then a first production lot released only after the retail visual gate is passed.

4. Unqualified change. A new dye house, a new master batch, a new substrate, a new finishing route. This is effectively a new colour approval, and it should be run as one: new lab dip, new instrumental measurement, new three-condition visual gate, and a written colour approval before the programme continues to ship against it.

The most valuable asset in all of this is the retained master swatch set. At minimum, a buyer should hold a production swatch from the approved lot, physically cut from the shipped ribbon, not from a sample book. A swatch from a sample book is dyed in different conditions and will not be the reference the mill is matching to. Two or three swatches from different production lots give a picture of the natural spread of the programme, which is more useful than a single reference because it tells the mill what the colour actually looks like across time.

Lot traceability from roll to finished component

A colour programme and a traceability programme are the same programme. The question a brand buyer will eventually be asked — by a consumer, a retailer, an auditor or a regulator — is not "what colour was this" but "which lot was this, and what was in it".

The traceability chain for ribbon has five joins, and each join is a place where the chain breaks.

  • Master batch to dye lot. The compounded dye lot that went into the bath. Every dye lot is logged against a master batch reference.
  • Dye lot to ribbon lot. The roll or lot number produced from that bath, with the master batch reference, the recipe record version, the machine and the date carried forward onto the lot documentation.
  • Substrate to dye lot. The yarn or substrate lot that was dyed, with the substrate supplier and the yarn lot recorded, so that a colour complaint can be traced back to the fibre as well as the dye.
  • Ribbon lot to converted component. Slitting, cross-cutting, folding and tipping. If the programme is converted downstream, the conversion lot must inherit the ribbon lot reference. This is the join most often lost, and it is the join that makes it impossible to answer a complaint about a finished bow.
  • Component to finished good. The finished product lot and, where the customer requires it, the shipment reference.

The failure mode is always the same: the chain exists on paper at every stage except one, and that one is where the complaint lands. It is worth asking each supplier in the chain, in writing, what fields does your lot documentation carry, and can a lot reference be traced backwards through your operation to the master batch? A supplier who returns the lot number of the input and nothing else has broken the chain, regardless of how good their own records are.

For programmes with an EU regulatory dimension — textile labelling, packaging waste rules, or scope 3 reporting on materials — this traceability is not optional. A scope 3 figure for the ribbon in a brand's product cannot be substantiated without the chain running from master batch to finished component.

What to specify, and what to ask a mill

A workable colour-continuity specification for a repeat-order ribbon programme contains the following fields.

  • Colour reference. The approved colour identified by master batch reference, master batch lot, and the retained production swatch, with the swatch identified by the production lot it was cut from.
  • Colour recipe record. A version-controlled document naming substrate specification, yarn or substrate supplier, dye house and machine, bath chemistry and additive pack, temperature, dwell time, liquor ratio and finishing route. The version number appears on every lot documentation.
  • Instrumental tolerance. Delta E limit against the retained swatch, stated together with illuminant, observer, aperture and measurement geometry, plus the position on the ribbon where the reading is taken.
  • Three-condition visual acceptance. Daylight, retail-fixture and — where the product is handled in a domestic setting — domestic visual gates, each with a defined pass criterion.
  • Master batch retention. The required retention period for the master batch and the retained production swatch, and the notice period the mill must give before any material or process change.
  • Change control. The four change categories above, each with its required approval path, and the rule that no unapproved change ships against the programme.
  • Traceability fields. The minimum set every lot document must carry: master batch reference and lot, substrate lot, dye lot, ribbon lot, recipe record version, machine, date, and the input lot reference for any converted operation.
  • Re-approval trigger. The specific conditions that oblige the mill to run a new lab dip and seek re-approval, stated as objective triggers rather than as a general obligation to inform.

And six questions worth putting to any mill before the programme is awarded.

  1. Can you supply the master batch reference and lot on the documentation for every production lot?
  2. What is your master batch retention period, and will you commit to retaining the batch for the life of the programme?
  3. Will you run a lab dip against our retained production swatch before any change to yarn supplier, dye house or finishing route?
  4. Under what illuminant, observer and aperture do you quote delta E, and what is your standard measurement position on the ribbon?
  5. Can a finished converted component be traced backwards to the master batch through your operation?
  6. How much notice will we receive before any change that could affect the colour, and what is the re-approval route?

A mill that answers all six precisely is running a colour management system. A mill that answers two is re-matching the colour by eye on every reorder, and the brand will find out about it in a colour-continuity complaint from a retail customer rather than in a specification conversation.

Why colour continuity deserves its own module: every other ribbon variable — width, construction, finish — is either stable by design or corrected mechanically at the next inspection. Colour is not. It drifts silently, in small increments, between production lots, and it drifts in the direction the programme can least afford: visible, on shelf, on a product the brand has already committed to. By the time a reorder is visibly off, the fix is not a colour correction. It is a new approval, a new production run, and a conversation with a retail customer. This is why the colour recipe, not the colour reference, is the thing worth locking.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a colour master batch in ribbon manufacturing?

A colour master batch is the retained, fully compounded portion of dye — pigment, carrier and performance additives — used to produce the approved colour, normally dosed into the dye bath at a defined percentage. It matters because it moves dispersion variability upstream to the compounder, it can be retained and re-dosed for the life of a programme, and it carries the fastness properties along with the shade. A programme specified with master batch retention is re-run against a validated chemistry rather than re-matched by eye.

Why does ribbon colour drift between repeat orders?

Usually because colour was specified as a reference rather than as a recipe. A Pantone number or a swatch describes the target but not the combination of master batch, substrate, bath chemistry, temperature, dwell time, liquor ratio and finishing route that produced it. When any one of those variables moves between the first and fourth reorder, the output moves with it — usually by an amount that is invisible against a paper reference and obvious between two rolls. The fix is a version-controlled colour recipe record plus retained master batch and retained production swatch.

What delta E tolerance should we specify for ribbon colour?

A delta E limit is the right instrument but is meaningless without the measurement and viewing conditions stated. Specify the limit together with illuminant (D65), observer, aperture and measurement position on the ribbon, and add two visual gates that the instrument cannot replace: a daylight visual comparison at approximately 5600K, and a visual comparison under the actual retail fixture lighting of the target market. The retail gate is what catches metameric failures, where a lot matches in the laboratory but not in the shop.

Should we keep a physical swatch from the production run?

Yes, and it must be cut from the shipped ribbon rather than taken from a sample book. A sample book swatch is dyed in different conditions and will not be the reference the mill is matching against. Retain two or three production swatches from different production lots so you hold a picture of the programme's natural spread rather than a single point. Retention of both the production swatch and the master batch should be a contractual requirement for the life of the programme.

What happens if our yarn supplier changes mid-programme?

It depends on whether the change is colour-neutral. A different yarn supplier within a defined tolerance is a qualified deviation requiring a laboratory dip against the retained swatch, instrumental measurement, and a first production lot released only after the retail visual gate passes. A new substrate or a new dye house is an unqualified change and is effectively a new colour approval: new dip, new measurement, new three-condition visual gate, and written re-approval before the programme continues shipping.

How do we keep ribbon colour traceable from roll to finished bow?

Through five joins: master batch to dye lot, dye lot to ribbon lot, substrate to dye lot, ribbon lot to converted component, and component to finished good. The join most often lost is ribbon lot to converted component, because the slitting, cross-cutting and tipping operation does not always inherit the input lot reference. Ask each supplier in the chain what fields their lot documentation carries and whether a lot reference can be traced backwards to the master batch. Without that chain, scope 3 reporting on the ribbon in a brand's product cannot be substantiated.
