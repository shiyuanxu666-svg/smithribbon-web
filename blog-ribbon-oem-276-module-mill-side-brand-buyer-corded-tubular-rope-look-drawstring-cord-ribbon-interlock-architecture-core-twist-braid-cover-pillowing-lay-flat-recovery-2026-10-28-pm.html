Ribbon OEM Published October 28, 2026 · Xiamen Smith Ribbon & Bow Co., Ltd. · 16 min read

A corded ribbon is the trim that looks like a rope and behaves like a spring. It is the only ribbon construction in which the finished product contains a component that is designed to be pulled and to return - the elastic or recoverable core inside the braid or the flat cover. Because it contains a designed mechanical behaviour, it cannot be specified like a flat ribbon by material and width. It has to be specified as a mechanism: a core, a cover, a construction method and a recovery requirement.

The cord is a mechanism, not a material

When a buyer specifies a flat ribbon, the specification constrains what the ribbon looks like and how it drapes. When a buyer specifies a corded or tubular ribbon, the specification has to constrain a mechanism that is under load in service: it is pulled open by a finger or a cord lock, it is supposed to spring closed again, and it is supposed to do that thousands of times without the braid loosening, the core taking a permanent set, or the cover unravelling at the end.

Three constructions cover almost all of the trade, and they are genuinely different products rather than variations of one thing:

  • Corded flat ribbon - a flat woven or printed ribbon with fine cords or a single cord threaded through it, producing a ribbed, rope-like surface while remaining flat and drapable.
  • Tubular or round cord - a hollow or semi-hollow tube woven in the round, or a flat tape rolled and closed. Rigid, holds a curve, sits proudly on the surface.
  • Covered elastic core - an elastic core inside a braid or a knitted cover. This is the drawstring, and it is the only one of the three with an engineered recovery behaviour.

Collapsing these three into a single specification line is the most common purchasing error in the category, and it produces a mismatch that only appears when the customer pulls the cord.

The 4-layer cord build ladder

A corded product, read from the centre outwards, is a four-layer structure. Every layer is a decision, and each one changes the behaviour:

LayerWhat it isWhat it controls
1. Core Elastane filament, rubber latex thread, or a thermoplastic monofilament; or nothing, in a hollow tube Stretch, recovery, elasticity, and the maximum extension
2. Carrier or braid The woven or braided structure that holds the core in place Strength, the geometry of the tube, and how the cover distributes load
3. Cover or wrap Woven tape, knitted sleeve, braided sleeve, or fusible yarn Surface, hand, printability, abrasion, and the visibility of the core
4. Finish Cutting, tipping, agletting, and any secondary treatment How the cord terminates and what happens at the end of the cable

Why the core matters more than the cover: a buyer choosing a drawstring usually chooses the cover, because the cover is what they can see and what carries the print. But the core determines the recovery, the maximum extension, the durability of the product and the feel in the customer's hand. A polyester cover over an elastane core and a cotton cover over a rubber core are two entirely different products, and specifying "cotton drawstring" or "polyester drawstring" tells the mill almost nothing about either. Specify the core, then the cover.

The 3-route core and cover decision tree

Route 1 - Spun elastic core with woven tape cover

The most common drawstring construction. A bundle of spandex or elastane core yarns is wrapped in a woven polyester or cotton tape, usually in a tubular braid. It is soft, it has good recovery, it takes a print well and it is comfortable against skin, which makes it the default for apparel, bags and gift packaging with a drawstring pouch. Its weakness is that the core yarn count and the cover tension determine the stretch, and both are frequently left unstated - so the same specification yields a stiff cord on one lot and a loose one on the next.

Route 2 - Braided round sleeve over a core

A hollow braided sleeve drawn over an elastic core and stitched closed, or a round braid knitted with the elastic in the same yarn. This gives a genuinely round cord with a consistent diameter and a clean, even surface, and it is the construction used where the cord is visible as a decorative element. The mechanical issue is the relationship between the braid's tightness and the core: too tight and the core cannot recover, too loose and the cord looks slack on the shelf and develops a permanent kink.

Route 3 - Flat corded ribbon with a woven or laminated cord

A flat ribbon with a cord running through or alongside it, giving a ribbed rope-look surface while staying flat. This is widely used in gift wrap, decorative trim, and retail presentation where a round cord is too stiff. Here the failure mode is different: the cord and the ribbon can work loose relative to each other, and the profile changes with use.

The 5-defect pillowing and lay-flat atlas

Cord defects are hard to diagnose from the outside because they present as a symptom the customer describes loosely - "it feels loose", "it does not pull", "it goes slack" - rather than as a named failure. These five are the ones that account for the majority of complaints, and each has a distinct signature.

DefectHow it presentsRoot causeHow to confirm
Pillowing The cord bulges between the anchor points and does not lie flat Core is longer than the cover, so the excess core has nowhere to go Measure core length against cover length on a cut specimen
Lay-flat failure The cord does not sit flush on the pack surface and rolls or twists Cover too stiff or too thick for the channel, or the core has relaxed over time Compression test between two flat plates
Low recovery / permanent set The cord is pulled open and does not return; it stays extended Core has taken a permanent set, or the core specification was never stated Extend, hold, release, measure residual extension
Cover slippage The braid slides along the core and the cord necks or bulges Braid tension inadequate relative to the core, or a shrinkage mismatch Mark-and-measure length change after a handling cycle
Unravelling at the cut end The cord frays at the terminal and the braid opens up End not tied, tipped or heat-set; often a trimming issue rather than a weave issue Inspect terminals on a cut specimen before finishing

Pillowing in one sentence: the core and the cover must be the same length, and the only way to guarantee that is to specify the core and the cover separately and require the mill to confirm the relationship in the construction drawing. When a buyer specifies "elastic drawstring, 4 mm", the mill has been given the diameter and nothing else, and will build to whatever core and cover combination its standard product uses that week. This is the mechanism behind most of the "the cord is fine but it does not match the last batch" complaints received in this trade.

The recovery test, and why the standard matters

A corded product has a performance figure that no flat ribbon has, and it should be written down the same way any other performance figure would be.

Three things have to be specified together or the number is meaningless:

  1. The extension - how far the cord is stretched during the test. Recovery is measured at a stated extension, and a cord tested at 20 percent extension is not comparable with one tested at 60 percent.
  2. The dwell - how long the cord is held extended. A cord held for one minute recovers almost completely; the same cord held for a day in a display does not.
  3. The measurement point - the residual extension after release and after a stated conditioning period. Standard test methods for this are published and should be cited by number.

Specify it as a percentage residual extension at a stated extension, stated dwell and stated conditioning. A cord that is specified as "high recovery" without those three parameters cannot be compared between suppliers and cannot be adjudicated when a batch fails.

Flat versus tubular: a decision with commercial consequences

Flat cord and round cord are not interchangeable and the choice is usually driven by a distribution or retail decision rather than a technical one. It is worth understanding the trade explicitly because it determines the pack volume, which is often a significant cost in an e-commerce or direct-to-consumer programme.

A round cord occupies the volume of a cylinder of its own diameter. A flat corded ribbon of comparable visual bulk occupies far less. On a bag with forty drawstring ends, on a gift pack with a dozen, on a garment hangtag with a cord, the difference in shippers and in cartons is real and recurring. A buyer who has specified round cord for visual reasons and then notices the freight cost has usually found the expensive end of the trade-off late. Where the cord is functional rather than visible - a bag closure, a gift pouch - flat corded ribbon is usually the better commercial answer, and where the cord is a visible decorative feature, round cord earns its place.

Terminals and finishing: where the product is actually born or lost

The cord itself is the visible part. The terminal is what the customer touches first, and it is where a programme goes wrong most often.

Three standard treatments exist:

  • Heat-cut with a melt seal - the end is heat-sealed so the braid cannot open. Suitable for polypropylene and some polyamides. The seal is a visible flat tab and it is quite hard.
  • Tipped or crimped with a metal or plastic aglet - the classic look on premium drawcords. It adds a component, a cost and a failure mode, because the aglet can detach or can be applied without enough crimp to hold it.
  • Tied-off or overlocked end - the traditional textile finish, cheapest, but it has a visible knot or a bulky thread loop.

The specification should say which, and for the aglet case it should also specify the aglet material, the length and the grip, and should require a pull test on the finished terminal. A cord that is correct and an aglet that is not crimped properly will still present to the customer as a bad product, and it will present as a bad product at the moment of use, which is the worst moment.

Shrinkage, and the conditioning step nobody writes down

Every textile cord has a shrinkage behaviour, and it is not the same for the core and the cover. A polyester core will have a very low shrinkage; a cotton cover may shrink noticeably on first wetting or on exposure to heat; a rubber latex core can change dimension with age and temperature.

Where the core and the cover have different shrinkage behaviour, the cord will either tighten or slacken over time, and this shows up as a change in the extension and the recovery rather than as a visible fault. This is the mechanism behind the slow drift that makes a drawstring product fail its specification at month eight even though it passed at month zero.

The fix is procedural and inexpensive: specify a conditioning and pre-shrink step before assembly, state the test conditioning standard, and require the residual extension and the length to be verified on the finished product after conditioning rather than on the component before it is cut and tipped.

Working with a mill that braids as well as weaves

Corded and tubular products are not made on a ribbon loom in the ordinary sense. They are braided, or knitted, or woven in the round, or made by wrapping and closing a flat tape - and the equipment and the skill set for those operations is a separate capability from weaving flat ribbon. Many mills that are excellent at flat satin and grosgrain do not braid at all, and will buy the cord in and convert it.

Where the cord is the hero of the product - visible drawstring, rope-look trim, rope decorative - the braid capability should be at the mill you are buying from, because the decisions about core length, braid tension and cover shrinkage all happen in the braiding operation. A converter who braids somebody else's tape adds a hand-off between the people who decided the construction and the people who make it.

At Smith Ribbon, weaving, braiding and finishing sit under one roof, so a corded product can be specified from the core outwards and verified on the finished assembly, with the same team accountable for the whole chain. For a brand buyer building a drawstring programme across multiple bag or pouch lines, that continuity is usually worth more than the unit price difference between a mill and a converter.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a corded ribbon and a tubular ribbon?

A corded ribbon is a flat tape that has fine cords woven into or alongside it, so the finished product stays flat and drapes like a ribbon while carrying a rope-like ribbed surface. A tubular ribbon is made in the round, or a flat tape is rolled and closed, so it holds a genuinely round cross-section and stands proud of the surface. The practical differences are substantial: a flat corded ribbon uses far less pack volume for comparable visual bulk, which matters when a product carries many ends; a tubular cord has a defined diameter, holds a curve, and cannot be flattened without creasing. They also fail differently - a flat corded ribbon can work loose between the cord and the tape, while a tubular cord fails by cover slippage along the core. Specify which construction you want rather than describing the appearance, because both can be made to look similar in a sample.

Why does my drawstring cord go slack and not spring back?

Four causes, in the order they usually appear. The first is that the core was never specified - if the specification says only the diameter and the cover material, the mill has used whatever core its standard product uses, and that core may have a lower recovery than the product needs. The second is permanent set, where the elastic core has been held extended for a long time, commonly on a display or a retail floor, and has taken a set it cannot recover from. The third is a shrinkage mismatch between the core and the cover, so the cord changes length after conditioning. The fourth is cover slippage, where the braid slides along the core and the effective extension changes. Specify the core material, the extension at which recovery is measured, the dwell, and the conditioning protocol, and require residual extension to be verified on the finished product rather than on the component.

How do I stop a corded ribbon from unravelling at the cut end?

This is a finishing decision, not a weaving one, and it should be specified at the drawing stage rather than left to the trimming department. The three standard treatments are a heat-cut melt seal, which suits polypropylene and some polyamide cords and leaves a visible flat tab; a tipped or crimped aglet in metal or plastic, which gives the premium appearance but introduces a second component that can detach if the crimp is insufficient; and a tied-off or overlocked end, which is the traditional textile finish and the cheapest but leaves a visible knot or thread loop. For a corded flat ribbon, the more common cause of unravelling is that the trimming operation cuts the cord and the ribbon separately, so the cord ends up fractionally longer or shorter than the channel and works loose. Specify the end treatment, require the aglet pull test where an aglet is used, and inspect terminals on a cut specimen before the finishing operation rather than after it.

Is a flat corded ribbon cheaper than a round drawstring?

Not always, and the comparison is worth doing properly rather than assuming. A flat corded ribbon usually wins on pack volume and freight, because a round cord occupies the volume of a cylinder of its own diameter while a flat tape of comparable visual bulk occupies much less, and a product with many cord ends multiplies that difference. On the conversion side a flat corded ribbon is a conventional ribbon operation, whereas a round drawstring needs braiding or tubular weaving, so at low volume the round cord can be cheaper because it is a single specialised process rather than a weave plus a cord insertion step. At volume the volume advantage usually becomes decisive. The right way to decide is on delivered cost per shipped unit rather than on the per-metre price of the trim, and to include the carton and freight effect explicitly rather than leaving it in the margin.

How much stretch should a drawstring cord have, and how do I specify it?

Specify it as three parameters, not as a word. The extension - the percentage the cord is stretched during the test, and recovery figures at 20 percent and at 60 percent extension are not comparable and will produce a different answer. The dwell - how long the cord is held extended, because a cord held for one minute and a cord held overnight recover very differently, and a product displayed on a shop rail for a week is the overnight case. And the residual extension - the percentage of stretch remaining after release and after a stated conditioning period, measured against the original free length. State a maximum residual extension, cite the test method, and require the measurement on the finished tipped and cut cord rather than on the cord before assembly. A product that is specified as good recovery without these parameters cannot be compared between suppliers and cannot be adjudicated when a batch fails.
