Mill-Side Brand-Buyer Elasticated Ribbon & Power-Stretch Recovery Interlock Architecture — Elastic Core Type, Force at Extension, Permanent Set and Face-Yarn Relaxation 2026

Ribbon OEM Published October 26, 2026 · Xiamen Smith Ribbon & Bow Co., Ltd. · 17 min read

An elasticated ribbon is a ribbon and a spring in the same object, and it is the only trim in this range where the product is defined by a behaviour rather than an appearance. A velvet ribbon is judged by how it looks under light. A printed grosgrain is judged by how the print holds. An elasticated ribbon is judged by how much it stretches, how much force it takes to stretch it, how completely it comes back, and — critically — how much it does not come back after it has been stretched and released.

That last parameter is the one that catches almost everybody out. Buyers specify a stretch percentage, receive a sample that stretches correctly, approve it, and then discover in month six that the ribbon no longer returns to its original length after a week of being stretched on a gift box, so the bow no longer sits square on the pack. The elastic core recovered. The face yarn did not. Nothing was inspected for it, because nothing was specified for it.

"Elasticated ribbon specification: 12 mm wide, plain woven elasticated ribbon, 100 % polyester face yarn with a 4 mm natural rubber core (unguarded, wrapped). Elastic core tension at 100 % extension: 0.45 N/cm nominal. Elastic core tension at 200 % extension: 0.85 N/cm nominal. Recovery: at least 95 % of original length after 30 minutes rest following a 100 % extension held for 1 hour. Permanent set: no more than 2 % length gain after 10 cycles of 50 % extension with 30 minutes rest between cycles. Face yarn must not be visibly bagged, slack or skewed after the permanent-set test. Sweat resistance: no loss of core tension exceeding 10 % after 24 hours immersion in artificial sweat at 40 °C, pH 4.5. Light exposure: core tension retention of at least 90 % after 40 hours continuous exposure to 500 lx."

That specification contains nine requirements, and three of them — the permanent set limit, the sweat resistance figure and the light exposure retention — are almost never present in a buyer brief, because they are properties of the elastic core rather than properties of the ribbon. They are also the three that determine whether the trim still works after six months on a shelf in a warm retail environment.

Commercial significance: an elasticated ribbon that loses 8 % of its length instead of 2 % is not a 6 % price difference. It is a trim that no longer fits the packaging it was engineered for, a shelf that will not present straight, and a re-order that arrives as a different product from the first one. In a gift-gifting programme with a season-long order cycle, that gap between the approved sample and the re-order is where the real cost of an elastic programme hides.

1. Why Elastic Ribbon Is a Two-Material System

Every elasticated ribbon contains at least two materials doing two different jobs, and almost every elastic trim failure is a failure of the interface between them. The core provides the force. The face yarn provides the appearance, the abrasion resistance, the width, the print surface and the hand-feel. They are held together in one of two ways.

In a woven elasticated ribbon, the elastic core is inserted into the loom as a warp and is covered by face wefts, so the core is effectively enclosed and the ribbon has a smooth, printable face. This gives the best surface quality and the tightest dimensional control, and it is the right construction for gift wrapping, packaging bands and apparel trim where the ribbon is printed or needs a clean face.

In a knitted elastic, the elastic core is looped into a knitted structure, which gives outstanding stretch in one direction and very high recovery, but produces a different surface. It cannot be printed in the conventional sense, its width is controlled by the knitting gauge rather than by a loom reed, and its appearance is a technical mesh rather than a clean ribbon face. This is the construction used in medical, sportswear and comfort-oriented applications where function outranks appearance.

A third construction, laminated elastic, bonds a film or foam between the face layers. It gives excellent hold and a specific soft or cushioned hand, and it introduces a further failure mode: the delamination between the elastic core and the facing layer, which is a bond failure rather than a stretch failure.

2. The 5-Layer Elastic-Core Authority Model

Five layers govern an elasticated trim, ordered from most frozen to most operational, each with a named owner and an explicit change threshold. The model exists because the most common elastic failure is not a bad core. It is a core that was correct for a different environment, a different extension ratio, or a different face yarn.

LayerParameterOwnerChange threshold
L1 End-use and extension demandRequired extension %, holding time, cycle frequency, skin or food contactBrand technical functionAny change of application, packaging geometry or wear cycle
L2 Regulatory and chemical statusRestricted substances, latex allergen status, REACH and CPSIA fit, skin-contact classBrand complianceAny market change or new body-contact application
L3 Core specificationCore material, core gauge, tension at extension, recovery, permanent setSupplier engineeringAny change of core supplier, gauge or wrapping yarn
L4 Construction and interfaceWoven or knitted route, pick density, face yarn tension, core coverSupplier productionAny change of loom, gauge, face yarn lot or construction
L5 PresentationFace wrinkling, bagging, skew, width stability, surface fuzzSupplier qualityAny new colour, width or face yarn

Notice that L2 carries a requirement that is unique to elastic and to nothing else in the trim library: whether the core is natural rubber, and therefore whether it is a latex allergen. A brand putting elasticated ribbon near skin or into a baby or medical-adjacent product needs to know this, and the answer determines whether the programme is built on a natural rubber core, a polyurethane core or a spandex core. It is a question that is expensive to answer after the tooling has been set up.

3. The Four Parameters That Govern Elastic Performance

3.1 Force at extension

The single most useful number in an elastic specification is the force the ribbon exerts at a stated percentage of extension, quoted in N/cm. It is the number that turns “stretchy” into an engineering value, and it is what allows a bow closure, a gift band and a sportswear strap to be specified independently of each other. A gift band that needs to hold a heavy box shut and a hair-tie that needs to be comfortable against the scalp want very different force-at-extension profiles from the same nominal stretch percentage, and the difference shows up in the force curve rather than the stretch figure.

3.2 Recovery

Recovery is the percentage of the original length returned after a defined rest period following a defined extension. A ribbon that stretches to 200 % and returns to 100 % of its original length after 30 minutes has recovered fully. A ribbon that returns to 103 % has permanent set, and in a fitted application such as a closure band that has to stay tensioned, 3 % is enough to leave the pack visibly slack after a week of use. Recovery and permanent set are the same phenomenon measured on different timescales, and both belong in the specification.

3.3 Permanent set

Permanent set is the length gain that remains after repeated extension-and-release cycles with rest periods allowed. This is the parameter that decides whether a re-order matches the first order, and it is the parameter that reveals an interface problem between the core and the face yarn. The mechanism is straightforward: the elastic core recovers elastically, but the face yarn does not. Every cycle leaves a small amount of residual elongation in the face yarns, and once the accumulated face elongation exceeds the core's own recovery, the ribbon has visibly bagged or slackened. The higher the extension ratio and the shorter the rest period, the faster this accumulates.

3.4 Environmental resistance

Two environmental effects deserve specific limits on an elastic specification. The first is sweat and moisture, which attack natural rubber cores and cause a loss of tension and eventually a loss of elasticity. The second is light, which degrades rubber over time; this is the reason elastic stored in a bright warehouse or displayed in a sunlit window degrades faster than elastic in a carton. A retention figure after a defined immersion or exposure period is a more meaningful specification than a generic claim of “good UV resistance”.

"Note on core choice for the buyer's own compliance file: natural rubber core — good elasticity and low force drift, contains latex, unsuitable for skin-contact or baby-adjacent applications without disclosure, and requires a sweat-resistance retention figure. Polyurethane core — latex-free, good for skin contact, lower temperature resistance. Spandex core — latex-free, high recovery and low permanent set, more expensive, lower heat resistance."

4. The Four-Stage Stretch Approval Gate

Elasticated ribbon approval proceeds in four stages. Each stage exists to catch a failure class that the previous stage cannot see, and the sequence matters because a sample that passes only stage 1 tells you almost nothing about whether it will still work in month six.

StageWhat is submittedHow it is judgedWhat it catches
1. Construction reviewMill construction proposal: core type and gauge, construction route, face yarnAgainst the end-use extension demand and the regulatory classWrong core family or wrong construction for the use
2. Force curveForce at 50, 100, 150 and 200 % extension on production yarnPlot against the required curve, not against a single pointCorrect stretch figure, wrong force, wrong hand-feel
3. Recovery and permanent setProduction-length samples, 10 cycles, rest intervals, re-measurementRecovery percentage and residual length gain after the set cycleFace yarn that does not return with the core
4. Environmental retentionSpecimens after sweat immersion and after light exposureCore tension retention against the pre-exposure baselineCore degradation that only appears in a warm, bright environment

Stage 2 is the one buyers most often compress to a single tensile test at a single extension, and it is the stage that produces the most rework later. A ribbon that meets the required force at 100 % extension can be useless at 150 %, because the curve is not linear and a core that feels soft at low extension can climb steeply just before it reaches its working range. For a closure band that has to be pulled over a box, that behaviour matters more than the headline stretch figure.

What this costs a buyer: a force-curve mismatch found at stage 2 costs a sample round and a week. The same mismatch found at launch, when the packaging is already printed with the band size specified, costs a packaging reprint. This is why the curve stage is worth insisting on even when the trim itself is inexpensive.

5. The Three-Tier Elastic-Defect Reconciliation Ladder

Elastic complaints arrive in three distinguishable families, and the triage below resolves most of them without a laboratory.

TierSignatureLikely causeResolution
T1 Slack or bagging after useRibbon no longer tensioned; face visibly wrinkled or bagged; length increasedFace yarn permanent set, or a rest period too shortMeasure residual length. Above the set limit it is a face yarn and construction issue; within it, extend the rest period.
T2 Loss of tension in a warm environmentClosure no longer holds; core feels soft and lifelessNatural rubber core attacked by sweat, heat or lightRe-measure core tension. A fall beyond the retention limit points to core degradation, not to the buyer’s handling.
T3 Failure at the core interfaceCore exposed or separating; surface pilling; width growthInsufficient core cover, wrong pick density, or tension imbalance in weavingCross-section the sample. A visible core or a gap means the cover is inadequate at the specified construction.

The most common dispute is T1, because it is the one a customer reports and a supplier can plausibly attribute to customer handling. The reason it can be settled quickly is that permanent set is measurable. A ribbon that has been stretched and released has a measurable residual length, and if that residual length exceeds the specified limit, the cause is in the material, not in the way it was used. If it is within the limit, the complaint is about the rest period or the cycle count, which is a usage issue and is properly discussed as one.

6. Writing an Elastic Ribbon Specification That Works

  • Core material and gauge, stated explicitly, with the latex status declared where relevant. Never leave the core material implicit — it is the one attribute that cannot be inferred from a sample.
  • Force at extension at four or more extension points, not a single tensile figure.
  • Recovery after a defined extension, hold and rest, with all three numbers written down, because recovery without a stated hold time is meaningless.
  • A permanent set limit with the cycle count, the extension ratio, the rest interval and the tolerance band, which is what makes a re-order comparable to the original.
  • Sweat immersion and light exposure retention figures for any core that will be handled by, worn next to, or displayed in a warm environment.
  • Face yarn specification, including the fibre and the tension, because the face yarn is what fails in permanent set and it is the part the buyer actually sees.
  • Width and thickness tolerance, since an elastic ribbon that varies in width will not sit correctly in a pre-printed band or slot.
  • Face surface requirement, specifying no core exposure, no pilling and no visible skew, assessed after the permanent set cycle rather than on arrival.
  • Applicable restricted substance status, aligned to the markets in which the finished product will be sold.

7. Working With an Elastic Trim Supplier

The relationship with an elasticated ribbon mill is a technical one, and the buyer has to bring the end-use requirement into the conversation rather than a colour and a width. The sequence that works is: agree the core family and the construction route against the extension demand and the regulatory class; agree the force curve as a curve; approve the face yarn and the appearance on a physical sample; then run recovery, permanent set and environmental retention on production-length samples before the first order.

Two commercial points are worth raising early. First, the binding constraint on an elastic programme is normally the number of distinct core gauges and extension ratios, not the face design, because each combination needs its own force curve and its own set testing. Second, the face yarn and the core should be locked together at the same time, because a face yarn substitution later will change the permanent set behaviour of an otherwise identical product and will invalidate the qualification.

8. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a natural rubber core and a spandex core in elastic ribbon?

Natural rubber gives excellent elasticity at a relatively low force, which makes it easy to stretch and comfortable to wear, but it contains latex, which must be disclosed and makes it unsuitable for skin-contact or baby-adjacent applications without a clear regulatory position, and it degrades faster under heat, sweat and light. Spandex gives higher recovery and much lower permanent set, is latex-free, and is the better choice where the trim is stretched repeatedly or sits against skin, at a higher material cost and with lower heat resistance. Polyurethane sits between the two and is also latex-free. The choice should be driven by the extension demand, the environment and the regulatory class rather than by the unit price, and it should be declared explicitly in the specification because it cannot be inferred from a sample.

How do I test the recovery and permanent set of an elasticated ribbon?

Stretch a specimen to a stated percentage of its relaxed length, hold it for a stated time, release it, allow a stated rest period, and measure the recovered length. Recovery is the recovered length as a percentage of the original. Permanent set is assessed by repeating that cycle ten times with rest intervals between cycles and then measuring the residual length gain, which for most packaging and apparel applications should be no more than about two per cent. Two details make the test meaningful. First, the rest interval must be stated, because recovery is strongly time-dependent and a ribbon measured immediately after release will look far worse than the same ribbon measured after thirty minutes. Second, the specimen must be production-length and produced on the production loom, because face yarn tension is the variable that governs permanent set and a short lab swatch will not carry the same tension.

Why does my elasticated ribbon look wrinkled and slack after a few weeks of use?

This is permanent set in the face yarn, and it is the most common elastic ribbon failure. The elastic core recovers to its original length, but the face yarns that wrap around it do not fully recover, so after enough cycles the face sits in slack wrinkles and the ribbon no longer holds tension. It is aggravated by high extension ratios, short rest periods and high cycle frequency. It can be confirmed by measuring the residual length of a stretched and released specimen: if it exceeds the specified permanent set limit, it is a material and construction issue rather than a handling issue. It is best prevented at specification stage by quoting a permanent set limit with its cycle count, and by locking the face yarn specification so it is not substituted later in the programme.

Can elasticated ribbon be printed or dyed like a conventional ribbon?

The face yarn can be dyed and, on a woven elasticated ribbon, printed on the face in the conventional way, since the face presents a dense, flat surface to the printing equipment. Knitted elastic cannot be printed in that sense because there is no continuous film or surface to take the ink, and it is usually supplied in greige or with a solution dye. Where printing is required, the correct specification practice is to state that the elastic core is inserted before printing so that the print is applied to the finished face without stretching it, and to approve the colour on a printed production sample rather than on a dyed swatch, because the face yarn tension in the elastic construction affects how the print lays down. Hot stamping and heat-transfer decoration are also used on elastic ribbon, but the heat tolerance of the core should be confirmed against the core specification before committing to either.

What is the minimum order quantity for custom elasticated ribbon?

At Smith Ribbon, custom elasticated ribbon typically starts around 1,000 m per width and colour on an existing core and construction, with a new core gauge, a new extension ratio, a new face yarn or a new width adding normally ten to fifteen days on top of the standard 30 to 45 days to production readiness. The binding constraint is the number of distinct core and extension combinations, because each combination requires its own force curve, its own recovery test and its own permanent-set validation. It is worth rationalising early: consolidating four extension ratios onto one core gauge usually saves more in qualification and re-order risk than it costs in unit price, and it makes a re-order far more likely to match the approved sample.

How should elasticated ribbon be stored to protect the core?

Store it cool, dark and relaxed. Heat accelerates the degradation of a natural rubber core, so an elastic ribbon stored in a hot warehouse or a shipping container through a summer will lose tension faster than the same ribbon stored in a climate-controlled space, and the loss is not reversible. Light also degrades rubber over time, so the carton should be closed and the ribbon should not be displayed on an open shelf in a sunlit window for long periods. The ribbon should be stored on the roll and not in a tight fold, and heavy cartons should not be stacked on top of it, since compression of a relaxed elastic ribbon over time is a form of permanent set. Where the programme includes a sweat-exposed or skin-contact application, the sweat immersion retention test should be run at the qualification stage rather than assumed, because the core behaves very differently after a hot bath than it does on a dry sample.
