Ribbon Sample Fees, Payment Terms and Shipping Policy — How to Buy Samples Without the Small Disputes That Delay a Programme (2026)

Published: 2026-11-18  |  Updated: 2026-11-18  |  By Xiamen Smith Ribbon & Bow Co., Ltd.  |  Since 2004

Sample requests are where most supplier relationships start, and they are where the commercial terms are most often left vague. A buyer who assumes samples are free, and a supplier who assumed the same, will have a short and unpleasant conversation when a courier invoice arrives. The fix is not argumentative. It is a written sample policy, agreed before the first sample is made, that states what each sample costs, who pays the shipping, and what happens to the money if the programme goes ahead.

Ask five sourcing managers what a ribbon sample costs and you will get five different answers, all of them technically defensible. One will tell you samples are free because the supplier wants the business. Another will tell you every sample is paid for because free samples attract browsers. A third will describe a sample fee that is refundable against the first bulk order. A fourth will say the ribbon is free and you pay the courier. The fifth will mention a development charge for a custom colour that nobody else mentioned.

Each of those is a workable commercial model. The problem is not that any of them is wrong. The problem is that the sample terms are almost never written down, so the buyer and the supplier each carry a different version of the agreement, and the disagreement surfaces at the worst possible moment — usually when a courier invoice arrives for USD 180, or when a buyer has approved four sample rounds and is told that the fifth requires payment before it will be made.

This guide covers the commercial side of sampling with an overseas ribbon supplier: what a sample actually costs, how sample charges are usually structured, who should pay the shipping, how to handle lab dips and custom development fees, how a refundable sample fee works and how to claim it, and how to write a sample request that gets a usable answer instead of a clarification email.

What a Ribbon Sample Actually Costs

The word "sample" covers several quite different things, and they have very different costs. Conflating them is the most common source of friction, because the buyer expects a catalogue swatch to be free and then objects when it is quoted like a production sample.

Sample typeWhat it isTypical cost treatmentLead time impact
Catalogue swatchA 30-50 cm length of existing stock ribbonUsually free; shipping is the real costSame day to a few days
Stock colour swatchAn existing colour in a specific width or construction not normally heldOften free or nominal, unless a new reel must be setSeveral days
Lab dip / lab dip setA short ribbon dyed to a target shade in a development rangeCharged per shade, per development round5-10 days per round
Custom construction sampleA new weave, width, finish, edge treatment or print methodCharged for the development setup, sometimes waived7-15 days
Production-representative sampleA length made on production equipment and processUsually charged at the quoted unit rateAdds to the production schedule
Bow or component sampleA tied bow, a spool, a decorated trim assemblyCharged per piece, reflecting labour3-7 days

The useful takeaway for a buyer is that the cost of a sample is driven by three things that have nothing to do with how much ribbon it is: the number of distinct set-ups it requires (a new dye lot, a new width, a new screen), the development effort in labour and machine time, and the shipping. A 30 cm lab dip that requires a new dye recipe costs far more than three metres of stock ribbon, because the meterage is irrelevant and the set-up is not.

Second takeaway, from the other direction: sample cost is a poor proxy for programme value. A supplier who charges properly for samples is not necessarily expensive on bulk. The samples that cost money are the ones where real work happened. A supplier who gives away everything may be doing so because a production sample costs them almost nothing in materials and they are pricing the margin into the bulk order instead.

How Sample Fees Are Usually Structured

Sample charges in the ribbon industry tend to fall into four structures. Understanding which one you are in prevents most of the arguments.

The free catalogue swatch. The supplier ships an existing ribbon at no charge, and the buyer pays the courier. This is the norm for stock items and is entirely reasonable, because the cost to the mill is genuinely trivial. The commercial reality is that the buyer pays for shipping even though the ribbon is free, which is worth stating explicitly in a sample policy so nobody feels misled.

The nominal sample fee. A fixed charge — often expressed as a set amount per sample order or per development round — covering material, set-up and labour, with shipping billed separately. This is the most common structure for custom development and the easiest to administer, provided the amount and what it covers are both stated.

The development charge. A separate, larger fee covering the engineering work of a new construction, a new print method or a new colour system. Sometimes it is credited against the first bulk order. It is common in the printing and embossing side of the business, where a plate, screen or die has a real cost that is specific to your artwork and your programme.

The refundable sample fee. A fee that is credited back against the first production order, usually capped and usually conditional on the order being placed within a stated window. This structure exists for a good reason: it aligns the interests of both parties. The supplier recovers the development cost, the buyer recovers the fee if the programme proceeds, and the supplier has a genuine commercial reason to want the programme to go ahead rather than to treat the sample as free advertising.

What causes friction is not the structure. It is that the buyer assumed one structure and the supplier was operating another, and neither had written it down. The single most useful step is to ask, in the first exchange, what the sample policy is: fee, shipping, credit terms, and whether anything is non-refundable.

Who Should Pay the Shipping

Almost always the buyer, and this should be stated without ambiguity. The reasons are worth understanding because they come up in negotiations:

  • Courier cost is not a sample cost. A ribbon swatch may weigh almost nothing, but an express courier shipment to Europe or North America carries a minimum charge that dwarfs the material. The mill incurs that cost on every single request.
  • Free shipping destroys the supplier's ability to filter. A supplier that ships free to anyone attracts a large volume of requests from buyers with no intention of ordering, and eventually limits free shipping to established accounts or starts charging.
  • The shipping cost is the buyer's real decision variable. When the buyer pays, they naturally consolidate — one request covering several colours and constructions from one supplier, rather than five separate one-colour requests. That consolidation is good for the supplier too, because it reduces the number of set-ups.

The exception is worth noting: a supplier with an established account relationship may include shipping in a development fee, or absorb it on a fee that is genuinely refunded if the programme proceeds. That is a negotiation, not a default, and it should be documented rather than assumed.

For the buyer, the practical protocol is to batch. One sample request should carry a target colour, a target width, a construction, a finish and any specific test requirement. Requesting three shades as three separate courier deliveries, when one courier delivery could carry all three, triples the freight for the same information. If you need six colour options, ask for them as a lab dip set in one dispatch rather than six swatches by post.

Lab Dips and Custom Development Fees

Lab dips are where sample cost becomes genuinely significant, and where the rules should be agreed most explicitly, because each development round can be a round of money.

The key commercial point is that the charge is usually per round, per shade, not per sample. A lab dip range of six shades delivered in a single round is one charge structure. The same six shades developed across three separate rounds, because each round introduced a correction, is three times the development cost. This makes the review process part of the cost model: a buyer who gives clear, consolidated feedback on the first range avoids paying for the second and third rounds.

Three questions belong in the initial request for any custom colour work:

  • What is the fee per development round, and is it per shade or per range?
  • How many rounds are typically needed for a shade to be approved?
  • Is the development fee credited against the first bulk order, and is it capped?

For custom construction and printing work, the fee usually covers a genuine one-off asset — a screen, a plate, a die, or a set-up on a specific machine. That asset belongs to the artwork, which raises a question that should be settled at the same time as the fee: who owns it if the programme never goes ahead, and can the buyer use it for a different supplier later? Ownership of artwork and tooling is a broader question that belongs in your IP and trademark documentation, but it is cheapest to settle alongside the sample fee rather than after a development investment has been made.

Refundable Sample Fees: How the Credit Actually Works

The refundable sample fee is the structure that most rewards clarity, and also the one where buyers most often lose money, usually not through a dispute but through a bookkeeping omission. A credit that is not claimed is worth nothing to the buyer, and a supplier who has not recorded it as a liability will not chase you for it.

A workable refundable fee policy has five components, and all five need to be written down:

  1. The fee amount and what it covers — material, set-up, labour, or everything including courier.
  2. The credit value — whether the credit is the full fee, a percentage of it, or a capped fixed amount.
  3. The qualifying condition — what counts as a qualifying order: a first production order of a stated minimum quantity, an order within a stated window, an order for the same colour and construction that was sampled.
  4. The time limit — how long the credit remains claimable, commonly three to six months from the sample invoice date.
  5. The mechanism — whether the credit is applied automatically as a reduction on the first order invoice, or whether the buyer must claim it in writing and the supplier must acknowledge it.

The mechanism is the one most often left vague, and it is where the money actually goes. The safest arrangement for the buyer is a credit that is applied automatically to the first qualifying invoice, with the credit shown as a separate line. A credit that requires a claim is a credit that requires a purchase-order amendment, a new approval chain, and somebody to remember. On a mid-size programme with several sample rounds, a forgotten credit on three or four sample fees is a real amount of money, and it is money that leaves without any dispute ever happening.

For the supplier's side, the corresponding discipline is to record the credit as an outstanding commitment from the moment the sample is invoiced, so that when the first production order arrives, the deduction is applied without anyone needing to remember. Buyers who set a clear lead time and confirm qualifying criteria up front tend to get a more willing supplier, because the supplier can see the programme is real.

Writing a Sample Request That Gets a Usable Answer

Most sample requests that generate clarification emails are missing the same five or six items. A request that contains them usually gets a quotation and a date back on the first exchange, and it costs both sides less time.

  • Target specification — construction, material family, width in millimetres, finish, and edge treatment.
  • Target colour — a Pantone reference or a physical swatch, and whether you need a lab dip or are checking an existing stock colour.
  • Quantity and format — how much, and in what form: a swatch length, a lab dip set, a spool, or a specific cut length.
  • Application — what the ribbon is for, because it changes the advice a supplier gives and often determines whether an additional test is needed.
  • Testing or certification — whether the sample must carry a specific test report, and for which standard. A sample destined for a retailer compliance review needs different paperwork from one destined for a mood board.
  • Programme context — indicative annual volume and target timing. This is what allows the supplier to tell you whether the sample is worth a development fee and whether the programme should proceed.

The programme context item is worth emphasising. A supplier asked for a sample with no volume information has to guess, and will quote conservatively. A supplier told that the programme is 40,000 metres annually can tell you whether a development fee is worth waiving, whether the construction is right, and whether your target price is achievable. It also makes it easier for the supplier to approve a refundable fee.

Common Sample Disputes and How to Avoid Them

Most disputes in this area are small in value and large in relationship cost. They cluster into a handful of predictable types, and every one of them is preventable with a sentence written at the start.

DisputeWhat happenedThe clause that prevents it
Sample fee charged after the buyer assumed freeNo fee policy was communicated before the sample was madeState the sample fee and credit terms in the quotation, in writing, before the sample is dispatched
Courier billed at cost after the buyer assumed the mill paidShipping responsibility was never specifiedState that samples are supplied free of charge and the buyer bears courier cost, or state the delivered cost explicitly
Development fee not credited on the first orderThe credit was never claimed, or the supplier did not record itApply the credit automatically as a separate line on the first qualifying invoice, with a stated validity period
Colour round count exceeded the agreed scopeThe number of rounds was never fixed, only the fee per roundState the included number of development rounds and the fee for each additional round
Sample did not match the bulk productionThe sample was a stock length and the bulk was a different production lotSpecify that the sample must be production-representative, with the construction and process stated
Sample order changed after despatchCustoms and courier were billed on the original specificationCharge changes to the order before despatch, or explain that a sample already in transit cannot be amended

The fifth row is worth expanding slightly, because it is the one with the longest tail. A buyer who approves a shade from a swatch cut from a stock reel and then receives bulk production in a visibly different shade has usually been told nothing misleading — the buyer simply approved the wrong thing. That is why the request should specify whether the sample needs to be production-representative, and why a programme should have a stated process for approving from production rather than from stock. This connects to colour master control and dye-lot variation, and it is far cheaper to solve at the sample stage than at the first bulk delivery.

A Short Sample Policy You Can Actually Use

Below is a compact policy that covers most of the points above. It is short enough to send with a first enquiry and detailed enough to prevent the disputes that normally arise in the second month of a programme.

  • Catalogue and stock swatches are supplied free of charge. The buyer covers courier cost, and we will consolidate multiple swatches into one dispatch where requested.
  • Custom colour development is charged at a stated fee per development round, covering material, set-up and labour. The fee for each additional round is quoted in advance and only incurred if the round is requested.
  • Development fees are credited against the first production order of the sampled colour and construction, up to the stated cap, within the stated validity period, and applied automatically as a credit line on that invoice.
  • Development assets such as screens, plates and dies belong to the buyer and remain the buyer's property once paid for, for use across the programme.
  • Production-representative samples are quoted at the production unit rate and are recommended where the programme is intended to move to bulk production.
  • Sample turnaround is confirmed in the quotation for each sample type, with the number of development rounds assumed for custom colours stated in advance.

Publish it once, attach it to the quotation, and refer to it in the purchase order. A supplier that does this looks more professional than one that negotiates each sample individually, and a buyer that asks for it is asking for the same thing every time rather than testing the waters with a smaller supplier first.

What to Ask Before You Order Your First Sample

Whichever supplier you approach, and whichever commercial model they operate, these questions establish the terms before any money changes hands:

  1. What is the fee for this sample type, and what does it cover — material, set-up, labour, or all three?
  2. Who pays the courier, and what is the expected cost to our destination?
  3. Is the fee refundable or creditable against a first bulk order? If so, what is the credit value, the qualifying condition and the expiry date?
  4. How many development rounds are assumed for this colour, and what is the fee for each additional round?
  5. Who owns any screens, plates or dies created for this programme?
  6. Can the sample be produced to be representative of the bulk process, and what does that change to the price and the lead time?
  7. What is the confirmed turnaround for this sample, and does it hold during our peak season?
  8. Are all samples and their documentation available for audit, including the test reports our retailer will require?

Ask them at the start, in one exchange, and write the answers into the quotation. A programme that begins with clear sample terms tends to run without a sample-related dispute for its entire life. One that begins with vague terms will have a fee argument before the first bulk order arrives, and it will usually arrive exactly when both sides are busiest.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. Should ribbon samples be free?

For existing stock and catalogue items, the ribbon itself is usually free because the cost to the mill is trivial. The buyer normally covers courier cost, which is often the larger number. For custom colour development, custom constructions, printing and embossing, a fee is normal because real set-up and development work is involved. Suppliers that give everything away frequently recover the cost in the bulk price instead, so a transparent fee structure is usually healthier for both sides than a policy of free samples with an unexplained margin.

2. How much should a ribbon sample cost?

There is no market rate, because the cost is driven by set-ups rather than by meterage. A stock swatch may cost only the courier. A lab dip set covering six shades in one round is charged per development round per shade. A new print screen or embossing die adds a specific one-off development charge. The useful question is not what a sample costs in general, but what set-ups this particular sample requires and whether those set-ups will be reused in the production order.

3. Can sample fees be refunded or credited against a bulk order?

Yes, and this is a common structure. The fee is credited against the first production order of the sampled colour and construction, usually up to a stated cap and within a stated validity period. For the credit to be worth anything, it should be applied automatically as a separate line on the qualifying invoice rather than requiring a claim. A credit that has to be claimed is a credit that gets forgotten.

4. Who should pay for shipping the samples?

The buyer, in almost all cases. Express courier to Europe or North America carries a minimum charge that far exceeds the material value of a swatch, and the supplier incurs it on every request. Paying for shipping also encourages the buyer to consolidate requests into a single dispatch, which reduces both freight cost and the number of production set-ups. If a supplier offers included shipping on an established account, that should be documented rather than assumed.

5. How do I avoid paying for more colour development rounds than I expected?

State the number of development rounds assumed in the quotation and the fee for each additional round before the first round begins. Then give consolidated feedback: one reviewer, one set of comments against all shades in the range, delivered once. Most extra rounds come from sending partial feedback and changing the brief mid-round rather than from any difficulty in matching a colour.

6. Should I approve colour from a stock swatch or from a production sample?

For anything moving to bulk production, approve from a production-representative sample. A swatch cut from a stock reel may have been produced in a different dye lot or on different equipment from your bulk run, so shade and handfeel can differ. Agree at the sample stage whether the sample is stock or production-representative, and require a retained master with a stated shade tolerance against dye-lot variation, so the bulk order has something objective to be judged against.

7. What happens if a sample order changes after it has been dispatched?

In most cases it cannot be amended, because the ribbon has already been made and the courier has been charged. Changes made before despatch are usually accommodated, sometimes with a fee if a development set-up has already been consumed. This is worth stating in a sample policy, because it makes the buyer concentrate on getting the specification right before approval rather than changing it while waiting for delivery.

8. How do I structure a sample request so I get a quotation rather than a clarification email?

Include construction, material family, width in millimetres, finish and edge treatment; the target colour as a Pantone reference or a physical swatch; the quantity and format required; the end application; any testing or certification the sample must carry; and indicative annual volume with target timing. The volume figure is the one most often omitted and the one that allows the supplier to advise on whether the programme is viable and whether a development fee should be waived.
