How to Read a Ribbon Quotation — The Nine-Layer Cost Waterfall and How to Compare Quotes That Were Never Built the Same Way 2026

Published: 2026-11-13  |  Updated: 2026-11-13  |  By Xiamen Smith Ribbon & Bow Co., Ltd.  |  Since 2004

The core principle: two ribbon quotations for the same article are rarely quoting the same thing. Normalise both against the same six inputs before you compare, and the price gap stops being a negotiation and starts being information.

A Quotation Is Not a Price, It Is a Set of Assumptions

Brand buyers frequently approach a ribbon quotation as a number to be reduced. They collect three or four quotations, compare the unit price, and award to the lowest. This process is straightforward, widely recommended, and almost always produces the wrong result, because two mill quotations for the same article rarely describe the same thing.

A quotation is a set of assumptions about material, conversion, decoration, packaging, risk, volume and timing, expressed as a price. Change any one of those assumptions and the price moves, sometimes by a factor of two. Two buyers who receive quotations of 0.42 and 0.68 US dollars per metre for a printed grosgrain ribbon are very often being quoted two genuinely different products, or the same product under two different commercial conditions, and no amount of negotiation will reconcile them.

Because a ribbon mill quotation is built from a specific, knowable structure, that structure can be read. This guide sets out the layers that make up a ribbon quotation, what each one actually represents, the questions that reveal the assumption inside each layer, and how to compare two quotations that were never built on the same basis.

Once you can see the layers, the negotiation becomes concrete. Instead of asking a supplier to lower a price, you can ask which layer is driving the difference and what would have to change for it to move. Those are very different conversations, and only the second one produces a durable result.

Layer One: Raw Material and Why Two Quotes for the Same Yarn Differ

Polyester filament price moves with the petrochemical chain, and the mill's own cost reflects when it bought, not what the market does today. A mill buying yarn on a spot basis in a rising market will quote a material cost that is current but fragile; a mill with contracted volume will quote a lower but more durable material cost. Neither is dishonest. The difference is that one price will move and one will not, and a buyer who does not know which is which will find out on the second order.

The useful question is not "what is your yarn price" but "is this material price indexed, and if so to what". A mill that will hold a material price for a stated period is offering something more valuable than a lower number, because it removes a variable from your cost model. Ask for the material component to be identified separately in the quotation, and ask whether it is quoted on a current index or a fixed basis for a stated validity period. If the quotation is valid for thirty days and the material is not fixed for the same period, the validity is largely theoretical.

There is a second reason material costs differ, and it is a quality reason. Yarn grade, denier, filament count and the number of ends in the construction determine how the fabric behaves. A ribbon that uses a lower denier in the weft may be perfectly adequate visually and fail in abrasion, drape or dimensional stability. Material cost comparisons are only valid when the construction behind them is identical.

Layer Three: Setup Cost and the Amortisation Question

Setup is the layer most often left ambiguous, and it is the layer where the largest single win or loss hides in a first order. A mill may charge a screen or cylinder fee per order, per colour, per design or once per season. A mill may include it in the unit price above a volume threshold, or exclude it entirely. The same physical job can therefore be priced at 0.38 or at 0.55 per metre depending on how the supplier structures the fee, and neither number is wrong.

Three questions resolve it. Is the setup fee one-time or per repeat order? Does it transfer to the buyer on payment, and if so with what documentation? And what is the volume threshold at which it is absorbed? These answers determine your real cost at three different volumes, and they are the inputs to a volume break table — which, if it is not written down, is a negotiation you will have to conduct again every time volume changes.

The tooling ownership question deserves particular attention because it is a genuine asset and it is routinely given away. If a buyer pays a cylinder fee, owns nothing, and must pay again on the next year, then the fee has been charged twice for one asset. The standard commercial position is that tooling paid for by the buyer is the buyer's property, transferable at a defined point and subject to defined confidentiality. That clause is worth more at the second and third order than the per-order setup charge it appears to cost.

Layer Eight: The Commercial Load That Never Appears on the Quotation

The most consequential layer is usually not printed on the quotation at all. It is the layer that reflects the mill's assessment of how much risk and cash they are carrying on the buyer's behalf, and it is the layer that moves most when the buyer's behaviour changes — often without the buyer doing anything that feels like a negotiation.

Consider a buyer who places an order with a confirmed date, pays an agreed percentage on order and the balance against documents, provides artwork on time, accepts the delivery without a claim, and repeats the same article without changing the specification. Now consider a buyer who requests the same order but leaves the date open, wants 60-day payment terms on a first order with an unknown supplier, amends the artwork after the material has been dyed, and inspects at a level well above the quoted AQL. The physical article is identical. The second order will cost noticeably more, and the reason will not appear on the quotation.

This is why the comparison between quotations is never really between mills. It is partly between behaviours. When a buyer asks why one quotation is lower than another for an apparently identical article, the honest answer is frequently that the supplier has priced the certainty differently, and that the certainty being bought is the buyer's own.

Layer Nine: Margin and Where the Volume Break Actually Sits

Margin is not the same as markup, and the difference matters when reading a quotation. A mill quoting at 0.42 per metre against a direct material and conversion cost of 0.31 has a 26 per cent markup on cost, which is a gross margin of about 26 per cent — but if the same order carries 0.11 of setup, the effective margin on that first order is far lower, and the mill knows it. This is normal and healthy, but it explains a behaviour buyers find confusing: the unit price does not fall in proportion to volume, because the fixed components are spread over more metres while the variable components move with commodity prices.

The useful way to read a volume break table is to calculate your own cost per finished unit at each volume, including the amortised setup, the testing, the packaging change and the freight, and then to see where the curve actually flattens. For most custom ribbon articles, the meaningful volume step is not where the per-metre price looks most attractive on the quotation, but where the total programme cost including development, the compliance documentation and the inventory carrying cost is lowest. Those two points are frequently different, and it is the second that determines whether a private-label programme makes money.

Reading Two Quotations That Were Never Built the Same Way

When quotations arrive with a visible price gap, the temptation is to assume the higher one is padded. A more reliable approach is to normalise the two quotations against a common set of assumptions before comparing anything. The normalisation has six inputs.

  • Construction. The same material, width, weave, edge treatment and selvedge. If the constructions differ, the prices are not comparable and the gap carries no information about efficiency or margin.
  • Colour basis. The same colour standard, the same tolerance, and whether the colour is a stock colour or a custom dye lot. A custom dye lot carries a setup and a colour-approval process that a stock colour does not.
  • Decoration. The same print method, the same number of colours, the same coverage percentage, the same registration tolerance, the same number of passes.
  • Finishing and packaging. The same slitting, the same cut lengths, the same carton configuration, the same core, the same moisture protection.
  • Quality level. The same AQL, the same inspection level, the same testing, the same documentation. This is the layer most often silently different between quotations.
  • Commercial terms. The same Incoterm, the same payment terms, the same capacity commitment, the same validity period.

Once normalised, a remaining gap is a real signal and can be interpreted. A 4 to 6 per cent gap on a normalised basis usually reflects a difference in operating efficiency, in material purchasing position, or in the plant rate applied to conversion. A gap of 15 per cent or more on a normalised basis is more likely to reflect a difference in what the supplier is actually including, and the productive question is not "why are you more expensive" but "which item in my normalisation is not in your quotation".

Questions to Ask Before Accepting Any Ribbon Quotation

A quotation should be treated as the start of a specification, not the end of a negotiation. The following questions, asked at quotation stage, prevent most of the disputes that would otherwise appear at the second or third order.

  1. What material and construction is this price based on, and which grade of yarn or substrate?
  2. Is the material price fixed for a stated period, and if it moves, does the quotation move and by what mechanism?
  3. What is the breakdown between material, conversion, decoration, setup, packaging and freight?
  4. Which setup charges are one-time and which are per order, and what are the volume thresholds at which each is absorbed?
  5. Who owns the screens, cylinders, dies and tooling that this order requires, and on what terms does ownership transfer?
  6. What inspection level and AQL is this price based on, and what testing is included versus quoted as an extra?
  7. What is the lead time assumed for this price, and what is the latest date at which the order could be placed and still meet it?
  8. What is the validity period of the quotation, and what changes if the order is placed after it?
  9. Which Incoterm and which payment terms are assumed, and what is the capacity commitment this price is conditional on?
  10. What is the price at the next volume step, and what exactly changes to reach it?

The tenth question is the one buyers most often skip and the one that determines whether the programme will actually get cheaper. If the supplier cannot answer it from a written table, the volume break is an aspiration rather than a commitment, and the programme's economics should be modelled on the current price only.

Building a Quotation Comparison Sheet Worth Using

A structured comparison sheet does more than save time; it forces the assumptions to be made explicit, which is where the real value lies. A workable sheet has one column per supplier and one row per quotation layer, with each cell holding either the figure or an explicit "not included". The "not included" cell is the important one, because it is where the hidden costs surface. It should carry rows for material and construction, colour basis, decoration method and coverage, setup and tooling, finishing, packaging and carton configuration, quality level and testing, Incoterm and freight, payment terms, lead time and capacity commitment, volume breaks and validity period, and a final row for the total programme cost at the buyer's realistic first-year volume.

Add two final columns. The first is "assumptions I had to make", because the gaps you filled yourself are the gaps that will cause a dispute later. The second is "questions outstanding", with an owner and a date against each. In practice the most valuable output of a quotation comparison is not the ranking but the list of questions, and it is worth sending that list to every supplier in the round rather than negotiating only with the one that appears cheapest on the face of the quotation.

When the Lowest Quotation Is the Right Choice

None of this argues for paying more than necessary. The lowest normalised quotation is usually the right award when the programme is stable and repeat-ordered, when the supplier has demonstrably run the article before, and when the volume break table is written down and honoured. The lowest *unnormalised* quotation is usually the wrong award, because it is rarely the lowest total cost and it very rarely survives the first repeat order without adjustment.

There is one circumstance in which the unnormalised low price is the correct choice, and it is worth naming because it is the exception buyers most often mistake for the rule: a genuine trial, a small exploratory order, or a first production run where the specification is still going to move. In that case you are buying the information as much as the goods, the specification risk is high, and the cheapest route to learning what the supplier actually does is often the right decision. What matters is that you know that is what you are doing, and that you write down what you will change once the specification settles.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. How should I compare two ribbon quotations that are very different in price?

Do not compare the numbers first. Normalise both quotations against the same six inputs — construction and material, colour basis, decoration method and coverage, finishing and packaging, quality level including AQL and testing, and commercial terms including Incoterm, payment and capacity commitment — and then compare. Once normalised, a remaining gap of roughly four to six per cent usually reflects genuine differences in operating efficiency or purchasing position, while a gap of fifteen per cent or more more often means an item is present in one quotation and silently absent from the other. The productive question is therefore which normalisation item is missing, not which supplier is more expensive.

2. What should a ribbon quotation itemise so that I can compare it properly?

At minimum nine layers: raw material cost, conversion cost, setup and development cost, decoration cost, finishing and conversion cost, quality control cost, packaging and logistics, the commercial and risk load covering working capital and capacity reservation, and margin. Also required are the stated AQL and inspection level, the Incoterm, the payment terms, the lead time and capacity assumption, the volume break table, the quotation validity period, and the tooling ownership position. A quotation that cannot be decomposed across these layers is not comparable to anything, including a previous quotation from the same supplier.

3. Who should own the screens, dies and cylinders used for my custom ribbon?

If the buyer pays the setup fee, the standard commercial position is that the tooling becomes the buyer's property, transferable at a defined point and covered by defined confidentiality. This matters more than it appears at first order, because tooling that the buyer does not own is tooling the buyer will be charged for again on the next seasonal run or the next reorder. Confirm ownership transfer in the quotation rather than assuming it, and ask what documentation is issued — a die number, a plate record or a serial reference — that identifies the specific asset and makes it traceable in the supplier's records.

4. Why does the unit price not fall proportionally when I increase volume?

Because a ribbon price contains both fixed and variable components. Setup, tooling, colour development, testing, artwork preparation and some documentation costs are fixed and are spread over more metres as volume rises, but material and decoration costs move with volume and with commodity prices, so the total falls in two different ways at once. The meaningful step is where your whole-programme cost is lowest, not where the per-metre figure looks best on the page. For most custom articles this includes the amortised setup, the compliance documentation and the inventory carrying cost, which frequently puts the real break point at a different volume from the one quoted as the headline break.

5. Should I always award to the lowest quotation?

No. Award to the lowest normalised quotation, which is the lowest once every quotation layer is held constant. On a stable, repeat-ordered programme with a supplier who has run the article before and a volume break table that is written down and honoured, that will usually be the right award. The exception is a genuine trial or exploratory order where the specification is still moving — there, buying the information cheaply is a legitimate objective, provided you record what you will change once the specification settles and you treat the trial price as temporary.

6. What is the commercial load in a ribbon quotation, and why is it rarely shown?

It is the portion of the price that reflects the mill's assessment of the risk and cash it carries — working capital tied up in the order, currency exposure, capacity reservation, the cost of your payment terms and the liability implied by your inspection level. It never appears as a line item because it is not a physical cost, but it moves the price substantially and it moves when the buyer's behaviour changes rather than when the product does. A buyer who confirms dates, pays as agreed, supplies artwork on time and inspects at the quoted level pays materially less over a programme than one who does none of these things, and the difference shows up as a price difference without ever being itemised.

7. How long should a ribbon quotation remain valid, and what changes after it expires?

A validity period should be stated explicitly, and the quotation should note what changes when it lapses. The important point is that the validity period and the material price basis must cover the same window. A quotation valid for thirty days that is based on a non-fixed material price is effectively valid only until the next material movement, because the mill can honour the commercial terms and still need to revise the number. Ask whether the material component is indexed, what it is indexed to, and whether the mill will hold it for a stated period — a fixed material price is worth more than a lower headline figure, because it removes a variable from your cost model.
