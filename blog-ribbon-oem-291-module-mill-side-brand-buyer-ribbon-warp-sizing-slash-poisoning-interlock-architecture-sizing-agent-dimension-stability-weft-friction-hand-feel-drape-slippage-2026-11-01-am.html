Sizing is one of the least visible decisions in a ribbon programme and one of the most consequential. It is a thin film of a polymer applied to the warp yarn before it is woven, and it determines whether the yarn can survive the reed, the shuttle and the pick-up at speed. It also determines what the finished ribbon feels like in the hand, how much it drapes, how readily the weft slides against the warp once the fabric is relaxed, and how the colour behaves when it is rubbed. Almost nothing about sizing appears on a purchase order, and almost every ribbon defect that buyers describe in general terms has a sizing contribution somewhere upstream of it.

This module treats warp sizing as an interlocked process rather than a chemical treatment. That framing matters because sizing is almost never a single-variable problem. A sizing change alters friction at the reed, which changes the warp tension profile, which changes the crimp ratio, which changes the width of the ribbon as it comes off the loom, which changes how the fabric relaxes on the roll. It also alters the stiffness of the yarn at the nip of the reed, which changes how much the weft is held, which changes weft slippage, which changes dimensional stability, which changes whether a ribbon cut for a bow hangs symmetrically. And it changes the surface chemistry, which changes whether the printed mark sits on the film or on the fibre, which changes crock fastness and which changes whether the ribbon still feels like velvet after it has been washed once.

The practical consequence for a brand buyer is that a sizing specification written as "warp sized, PVA, for polyester" is not a specification. It is a note. It cannot be verified, it cannot be re-approved when circumstances change, and it cannot be matched on a repeat order by a mill that did not keep the right records. This module sets out what a buyer should ask for, what a mill should be able to produce on request, and which changes should trigger a formal re-approval rather than a quiet adjustment.

What Sizing Actually Does at the Reed

Warp sizing is applied to the yarn cone, either in the sizing machine at the winder or in a separate pre-sizing operation before the yarn reaches the warper. The purpose is to protect the yarn through the mechanical path of weaving, and to control how the yarn behaves once it is in the fabric.

Size protects in three ways. It adds a film that resists abrasion, so the yarn does not abrade against the reed dents, the heald shafts and the guides. It adds cohesion, so the filaments in a spun yarn or the singles in a filament yarn do not separate and fray as they are bent around the reed. And it adds temporary stiffness, which raises the yarn's bending rigidity and therefore raises the friction coefficient at the reed. That last effect is the one that governs weaving behaviour, and it is the one that cuts both ways.

The size film must survive the warp path and then be removed, because a size that stays in the fabric makes the ribbon stiff, dull and stiff to the touch, and it can block dye penetration. In cotton warp sizing the classic agent is PVA, which is water-soluble and is removed by a hot pre-wash. In polyester warps the common agents are modified PVA, polyacrylate dispersions, or the newer self-settling polyacrylic acid systems used on continuous-filament warps where a soluble size would create too much effluent. The choice is not neutral: it determines the removal route, the dyeing behaviour, the final hand, and the mill's environmental load.

Two properties of the size film drive almost all of the observable behaviour, and both are worth naming precisely because they are the two things a buyer should be able to specify. The first is the add-on, which is the dry weight of size deposited on the warp yarn, normally expressed as a percentage of the yarn's dry weight. The second is the sizing percentage after removal, meaning how much of that size remains in the finished fabric. A typical weaving add-on for a ribbon warp might sit somewhere between 8% and 30% depending on yarn count and construction, but the useful figure for a buyer is the residual sizing in the finished ribbon, which for a good apparel-grade ribbon should be very low, and for a technical or craft ribbon may deliberately be higher.

The important point is that add-on and residual are different numbers separated by a removal process, and the removal process is where most of the risk lives. If removal is incomplete, the ribbon is stiff and dull and its drape is wrong. If removal is aggressive, the yarn loses its protective film, the loom runs worse on the next run of the same yarn, and the fabric can feel thin. Both failures are usually attributed to something else entirely.

Slash Poisoning: When the Reed Starts to Break the Yarn

Slash poisoning is the condition in which a reed dent breaks the warp yarn repeatedly at the same point, usually because a local defect in the yarn — a knot, a slub, a join, a thin spot — creates a high spot that the reed cannot pass smoothly. Every subsequent pick drives the dent against the same weak point, and the filaments there are progressively cut and crushed until the yarn fails and the loom stops.

This matters for a brand buyer for a specific reason: slash poisoning is what causes the loom to stop in the middle of a production run, and the way a mill recovers from a loom stop is one of the clearest indicators of how the programme will behave in a peak season.

Two things determine whether slash poisoning occurs. The first is yarn quality, which is the mill's problem and the yarn supplier's. The second is the sizing, which is a design variable. A warp with adequate add-on and adequate cohesion resists the damage; a warp with insufficient sizing is brittle at the reed contact point because the filaments are not held together, and it breaks under repeated flexing. This is the paradox of sizing: too little causes breakage, too much causes the yarn to be stiff and brittle rather than tough, because a thick, dry, brittle film transmits the reed force straight into the fibre instead of absorbing it.

There is a third factor, which is the reed itself and the loom's conditioning. A reed that has been over-used, or a loom running with a beat-up force set too high for the yarn count, will break yarns regardless of sizing. Sizing cannot fix that; it can only make the yarn more tolerant of it.

When slash poisoning does occur, the recovery options are limited and each has a quality cost. The weaver can tie the broken yarn and continue, which is fastest but leaves a knot that will show as a defect and can abrade. The weaver can piece in a length of new yarn, which is better but introduces a lap that may not match. The mill can stop and re-splice the whole warp, which is cleanest but may cost hours. A mill with a controlled sizing route will see far fewer of these events, and a mill that handles them well will splice rather than tie, document the splice position, and carry the defect forward into the inspection record rather than quietly hoping the eye of the bow will not find it.

Sizing and Weft Slippage: The Dimensional Stability Link

Weft slippage is the movement of the weft yarn within the fabric under load, usually from a pull in the warp direction. On a ribbon it shows up as a fabric that is narrower than it should be, or wider, or that grows in the middle of the ribbon and shrinks at the edges. For a brand that cuts bows or trims, it means that the ribbon a cutter measures is not the ribbon the finished bow has, and the symmetry of the bow drifts with the length of the ribbon.

The mechanism is straightforward. The warp yarns are the skeleton of the fabric. If they are held together by the weft with adequate friction, the fabric is dimensionally stable. If the warp yarns are too slippery relative to the weft, or the weft is too slippery relative to the warp, the weft slides and the structure deforms. Sizing sits almost entirely on the warp, so it is the primary lever on this friction relationship. A size that reduces warp surface friction allows weft slippage; a size that increases it resists slippage but at a cost in hand-feel and in the removal step.

There is a second, subtler mechanism that is worth naming because it is often missed: crimp balance. In a plain weave the warp and weft interlace, and each is crimped as it goes over and under. The fabric is in equilibrium when the crimp is distributed between the two systems in proportion to their relative stiffness. If the warp is very stiff because of heavy sizing, it resists crimping and forces more crimp into the weft, which reduces the effective width of the fabric for a given number of picks and makes the ribbon behave differently in the warp and weft directions. On a wide ribbon this shows as a barrelled or pinched centre; on a narrow ribbon it shows as a width tolerance problem and a bow that hangs unevenly.

For the buyer, the practical test is not a laboratory measurement in the abstract but a defined behaviour test on the finished ribbon: how much does it move when you pull it, how much does it recover, does it grow in length after being stretched, and does the width hold across the roll. Those should be specified as acceptance criteria with stated tolerances, and they should be measured on material that has been conditioned, because a ribbon that has just come off the loom and a ribbon that has been relaxed for 24 hours behave quite differently.

Hand-Feel, Drape and the Consumer Perception Problem

Hand-feel is the tactile character of the fabric: how soft or crisp it feels, how much it drapes, how it folds, how quickly it recovers from a crease. For a ribbon used in gift packaging, hair accessories and apparel trim, this is not a luxury specification; it is the difference between a product that feels premium in the hand and one that feels like a plastic band.

Sizing governs a substantial part of this, and the direction of the effect depends on the chemistry and on whether the size has been removed. Residual size makes fabric stiff, papery and slightly tacky if it has migrated; it raises the coefficient of friction at every fibre surface, which raises bending stiffness and reduces drape. This is why a ribbon that feels wrong on the cutting table usually gets blamed on the yarn or the finish when the actual cause is that the wash-off was incomplete or the wash-off recipe changed.

Drape is more sensitive than most buyers expect because it is a function of bending stiffness divided by areal density, and both of those change with the sizing route. A heavier size increases stiffness without increasing mass, so it reduces drape and also changes the fall of a bow so visibly that a consumer will notice. For the same reason, drape is a poor thing to specify as a number and a good thing to specify as a reference: an approved physical swatch, handled in the hand, retained by both parties, which becomes the arbiter when a new lot arrives.

There is also the interaction with finishing. Sizing affects how the ribbon responds to heat setting, and heat setting in turn affects how the size behaves, so the two cannot be optimised independently. A mill that changes the heat-setting route without re-checking the sizing will move the hand-feel, and a mill that changes the sizing without re-checking the heat-setting will find that the new size does not survive the finishing at all. The order of these operations and the parameters of each have to be considered together.

Surface Chemistry: Print Adhesion, Crocking and Wash Behaviour

The size film is a layer on the surface of the yarn, and it is therefore the first thing that printing, rubbing and washing interact with. A printed ribbon with residual size will often print differently from the same ribbon without it: the film affects surface energy, and surface energy determines whether the print paste wets the fibre or beads on the film. The result can be poor print adhesion that passes a visual check and fails a crocking test three months later.

Crocking — the transfer of colour from the ribbon onto another surface by rubbing — is affected by size in a way that is easy to misunderstand. A stiffer fabric with more residual size resists the initial penetration of the rubbing action and therefore shows less crocking in a short test. This can flatter a ribbon that is in fact badly dyed. Over time, under washing and flexing, the size comes out, the fabric softens, and the underlying dye fastness is exposed. A buyer who tests crock only on new ribbon, with residual size still in it, is testing the size rather than the dye.

Wash behaviour is the same story from the other direction. A ribbon that has residual size will feel different after a first wash, may lose body or become limp, and may behave differently in dimensions. If the brand's product claims to be washable, the relevant test is the ribbon after laundering, which means after the size has gone. If the ribbon is not intended to be washed, the relevant test is dry-cleaning resistance and long-term drape stability, which is a different question again.

Specifying the Sizing Route So It Is Verifiable

The following is a sizing specification clause set that a mill can actually work to and a buyer can actually audit. It is not exhaustive, but each line corresponds to something that can go wrong.

Warp sizing specification — ten clauses

  • Size chemistry: agent family (PVA, modified PVA, polyacrylate, PAA), supplier and product code
  • Warp add-on percentage, with the tolerance and the measurement method
  • Residual sizing in the finished ribbon, target and maximum
  • Removal route: pre-wash versus in-line wash-off, temperature, time and detergent
  • Yarn count, composition, blend ratio and warp tension at sizing
  • Reed condition and loom speed range for which the sizing route is valid
  • Hand-feel and drape reference: approved physical swatch, retained by both parties
  • Dimensional stability criterion after conditioning: width change and length growth limits
  • Surface chemistry criteria: print adhesion and crocking tested on finished, washed ribbon
  • Re-approval trigger list: sizing supplier, yarn supplier, removal route, reed type, yarn count

The last clause is the one that is most often missing and most useful. Sizing re-approval should be triggered by a change of size supplier or product, a change of yarn supplier for the same article number even where the count and composition are nominally identical, a change in the removal route or wash-off parameters, a change of reed type or loom, a significant change in loom speed, and a change in the fibre blend ratio. It should not be triggered by a repeat order of the same yarn lot, the same size product and the same removal route.

On measurement, the honest position is that there is no single universal way to measure residual sizing, and a buyer who specifies "residual sizing must be under 2%" without naming a method has specified nothing. The methods in common use include the formaldehyde test, the iodine test for starch, burn-off for the mass of organic deposit, and solvent extraction. Each has limitations. The important thing is that the mill and the buyer agree on one method, that the method is stated on the specification, and that the historical data for the programme is all generated by that method so the numbers are comparable over time.

What a Mill Should Be Able to Produce on Request

The record that makes a repeat order reproduce the first roll contains seven parts. The size product and supplier for the specific article number, and any change since the last order. The add-on achieved on the warp and the measurement method used to confirm it. The removal route and its parameters as actually run, including bath temperature, dwell time and detergent type. The residual sizing result for the finished lot with its method. The yarn lot used and its sizing condition. The physical retained swatch for each production lot, stored under defined conditions. And the loom records showing reed type, speed and any slash-poisoning or splice events on that production.

That last item is worth singling out. A mill that keeps a record of loom stops and splices has, without any extra paperwork, the beginning of a defect traceability system. A brand that receives that record with the shipment can trace a defect complaint to a specific position on a specific roll. Most mills keep this information informally and discard it; the difference between a controlled programme and an uncontrolled one is often just whether the information survives the week.

How This Becomes a Commercial Advantage

Sizing is invisible to the buyer and to the end consumer, which is precisely why it is worth controlling. The commercial effects are indirect but they are large and they compound.

The first effect is on loom efficiency, and therefore on price and on lead time. Slash poisoning and warp breaks are the single most common cause of an unplanned loom stop in a ribbon mill. A warp that is correctly sized for the loom it runs on has fewer breaks, which means fewer hours lost, which means the mill can quote a lead time it can actually hold. A mill that repeatedly runs a sizing route on a loom it is not suited to will be late in the season precisely when lateness is most expensive, and the brand will have no visibility of the cause.

The second effect is on the defect rate and therefore on the claim rate. Weft slippage, bow asymmetry, and width drift are all downstream of the sizing route to a greater degree than most buyers assume. A programme with a controlled sizing route has fewer of these claims, which means a lower cost of quality and, more importantly, a brand team that is not spending its capacity managing a supplier that cannot hold a dimension.

The third effect is on programme growth. Sizing is specified per article number and per loom speed, so a programme that is stable at 5,000 metres on one loom is not necessarily stable at 50,000 metres across two looms or three shifts. A mill that has written down its sizing route can scale deliberately, can move the programme to a different loom with the right re-approval, and can bring on a second supplier against a specification rather than against a sample. A mill that has been succeeding with judgement and a good operator can scale into a problem, and the brand will find out at the worst possible moment.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does warp sizing do, and why does a buyer need to specify it?

Sizing applies a polymer film to the warp yarn before weaving. It protects the yarn from abrasion and filament separation as it passes the reed, the heald shafts and the guides, and it controls how the yarn behaves in the finished fabric. It affects weaving efficiency, weft slippage, dimensional stability, hand-feel, drape, print adhesion, crock fastness and how the ribbon behaves after washing. A buyer specifies it because almost every attribute a brand complains about visually has a sizing contribution upstream, and because a sizing route that is not written down cannot be re-approved, audited or reproduced on a repeat order.

What is slash poisoning and what causes it?

Slash poisoning is the progressive breakage of warp yarn at a single reed position, usually caused by a local yarn defect such as a knot, slub, join or thin spot that the reed cannot pass smoothly. Every subsequent pick drives the dent against the same weak point, cutting and crushing the filaments until the yarn fails. The causes are a yarn defect, a reed condition or beat-up force set too high for the yarn count, and the sizing route. Both insufficient sizing, which leaves the filaments unprotected and brittle at the reed contact point, and excessive, dry, brittle sizing, which transmits the reed force directly into the fibre, increase the incidence. It matters commercially because it is a frequent cause of unplanned loom stops, and because the way a mill recovers from a stop reveals a great deal about its process control.

How does sizing affect weft slippage and dimensional stability?

The warp yarns form the skeleton of the fabric and the weft is held against them by friction. Sizing sits almost entirely on the warp and is therefore the main lever on that friction relationship. A size that reduces warp surface friction allows the weft to slide under load, so the fabric narrows, widens or grows in the middle. A heavier size increases friction and resists slippage but also raises the stiffness of the warp, which forces more crimp into the weft and changes the effective width and the directional behaviour of the ribbon. The result is that width stability and bow symmetry in a ribbon cut for assembly are substantially determined by a decision made at the sizing machine weeks earlier.

How should residual sizing be specified so it is measurable?

With a named method and a stated conditioning, not with a bare number. There is no single universal measurement, and a specification that says "residual sizing under 2%" without naming a method is not verifiable. The methods in common use include the formaldehyde test, the iodine test for starch, burn-off for organic deposit mass, and solvent extraction, each with its own limitations. The requirement should state the method, the conditioning applied to the sample before measurement, the target and the maximum, and the mill should generate all historical data for the programme using that same method so results remain comparable over time.

Does residual sizing affect print quality and crock fastness?

Yes, and it is a frequent source of confusion. The size film is the surface the print paste meets, so it affects surface energy and therefore wetting and adhesion. A printed ribbon with residual size may print acceptably and then fail an adhesion or crocking test later. Crocking is also affected in a way that flatters poor dyeing: a stiffer ribbon with residual size resists the initial rubbing action and shows less colour transfer in a short test, while over time and after laundering the size is removed and the underlying dye fastness is exposed. The correct practice is to test crocking and adhesion on finished, washed ribbon rather than on new ribbon with size still in it.

When should a sizing re-approval be triggered, and when is a repeat order unaffected?

A re-approval should be triggered by anything that can plausibly move the behaviour of the size film or of the warp: a change of size supplier or product, a change of yarn supplier for the same article number even where count and composition are nominally identical, a change of removal or wash-off route or of its parameters, a change of reed type or of loom, a significant change in loom speed, and a change in fibre blend ratio. It should not be triggered by a repeat order of the same yarn lot with the same size product and the same removal route. The reason the distinction matters is that re-approving on every order destroys the value of the approval and it gets ignored, while re-approving nothing until a complaint arrives is the failure this discipline exists to prevent.

Why does hand-feel change between production lots if the yarn and construction are the same?

Because residual sizing varies between lots, and residual sizing governs bending stiffness, which governs how the ribbon feels and how it drapes. The variation can come from the removal step rather than from the sizing machine: a wash-off bath that is weaker, shorter, cooler or differently loaded will leave more size in the fabric, and that shows up as a stiffer, duller, less draping ribbon. Since drape depends on bending stiffness divided by areal density, a change in residual size changes drape without changing the fibre content at all. This is why hand-feel and drape are best governed by an approved physical swatch rather than by a number, and why an unexplained hand-feel change on a repeat order should send the buyer to the wash-off records first.

How does a buyer verify a mill's sizing control without a laboratory?

Through three practical checks. First, ask for the production record and see whether it names the size product, the add-on achieved, the removal route as actually run, the residual result with its method, the yarn lot and the loom conditions; a mill that cannot produce this has not been controlling the parameter. Second, run a simple dimensional test on conditioned ribbon: measure the width across the roll and the centre, pull the ribbon in the warp direction and observe recovery and any length growth, and compare against the approved reference. Third, test the ribbon after a wash rather than only new, because hand-feel, drape and crocking all change once the size has left. A mill that passes all three is controlling sizing, regardless of what its paperwork says.
