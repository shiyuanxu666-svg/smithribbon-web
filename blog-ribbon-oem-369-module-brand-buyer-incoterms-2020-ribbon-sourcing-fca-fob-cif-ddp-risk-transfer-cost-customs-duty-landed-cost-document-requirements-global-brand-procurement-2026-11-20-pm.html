Incoterms for Ribbon Sourcing - What FCA, FOB, CIF and DDP Mean for a Brand Buyer (2026)

Published: 2026-11-20  |  Updated: 2026-11-20  |  By Xiamen Smith Ribbon & Bow Co., Ltd.  |  Since 2004

Incoterms decide two things in an international ribbon purchase: who carries the goods from which point, and who carries the cost of getting them there. Two buyers with identical specifications can find themselves in very different positions on the same purchase because they chose different terms - and the difference is often seen only after a container is delayed, damaged, or held at customs.

Normalising a quotation means building the buyer's full landed cost under each scenario: goods price plus sea freight plus inland transport at origin plus insurance plus import duty plus customs clearance fees plus destination charges plus inland delivery, expressed as a single figure per unit or per container. Two suppliers' numbers become comparable only at that point. A 4 per cent lower FOB price that requires the buyer's forwarder to book a full container for a 40 per cent utilised load may be more expensive in practice than a higher FCA price with a consolidated shipment.

The HS classification of ribbon is worth attention during this exercise, because it drives the duty and it is not always obvious. Narrow woven fabrics can fall under different headings depending on construction and composition - whether they are pile, whether the warp and weft are of the same material, and what proportion falls below a stated weight threshold. A ribbon that is 100 percent polyester, a ribbon with a cotton core and polyester face, and a ribbon with a wire or metallic component may all be classified differently. Classification should be agreed with the supplier and confirmed with the customs broker rather than assumed, and for any programme with meaningful volume it is worth obtaining a written classification opinion. Under DDP, this becomes the supplier's problem, which is part of why DDP prices are higher.

Documentation: The Part That Causes the Most Delay

Incoterms specify document obligations, and in ribbon trade the document set is where most delays originate. A container held at the destination port for a week because the commercial invoice does not describe the goods adequately is a more common event than a buyer would expect.

Two practical points. First, the compliance and material documentation should be requested at the order stage rather than at shipment stage - if the buyer needs an OEKO-TEX declaration, a recycled content certification or a material composition statement for a specific article, asking for it a week before the container is loaded usually produces a weaker document or no document at all. Second, the values and descriptions on the invoice need to be consistent with every other document in the set. Customs authorities and freight forwarders both check for inconsistencies, and an invoice that disagrees with the packing list on gross weight is a hold at the port.

Choosing the Term for Your Supply Chain

The choice follows from the buyer's actual situation, not from which term is most common.

  • If you buy in full containers and tender freight competitively, FCA at the factory with loading included is the natural term. It aligns risk with the handover, keeps freight tenderable, and removes the FOB quay-gap problem.
  • If you consolidate shipments from several suppliers, FCA is also the right term, because it accommodates a shared container and a buyer's nominated forwarder without a minimum volume condition.
  • If the supplier has its own strong freight arrangement and you want simplicity on the ocean leg, CIF can work, provided you understand that the risk point at the destination port means you need insurance that covers the inland leg, and that CIF is not door-to-door.
  • If you want a single landed cost and a single risk point at your warehouse, DDP is the term that delivers it - at a price that reflects the duty and clearance risk the supplier is taking on your behalf. DAP is the middle option, giving the seller the carriage to your premises but leaving import duty and clearance with the buyer.
  • If you are a first-time importer into a non-Asian market, DDP with a supplier who is genuinely established in your market may reduce risk considerably, because import clearance errors are a common cause of the delays and penalties that make first imports expensive.

Whatever term is chosen, the contract should state three things alongside it: the Incoterm code with the year and the precise named place, the version of the rules that applies, and the specific point in the delivery process where the risk passes. Naming the place properly is worth more attention than it usually gets, because "FOB China" or "CIF your port" is not a term that can be administered, and when something goes wrong the parties will spend more time arguing about where the delivery point was than about the actual problem.

Questions to Ask When a Supplier Quotes

  1. What is the Incoterm code and year, and what is the exact named place?
  2. Does the price include export clearance and origin inland handling to the port or container terminal?
  3. Is the container loading and securing included, and who pays for it?
  4. For DDP, what duty rate and HS classification does the price assume, and who confirmed it?
  5. Which documents will be provided, and in what form?
  6. What is the production lead time, and how much of it is after the order is confirmed rather than after the deposit?
  7. What is the payment schedule, and does it align with the delivery terms?

The last question connects the terms to the rest of the commercial structure. A 100 per cent prepayment against a FOB term in an unfamiliar supplier relationship is a different proposition from a 30 per cent deposit with the balance against documents, and the Incoterm should be read together with the payment terms, not separately from them. The most reliable structure for a new buyer-supplier relationship is a modest deposit, the balance against shipping documents, and a term that places risk at a point where the buyer can actually inspect the cargo.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which Incoterm should we use for a containerised ribbon order?

FCA is usually the best fit. It places the risk point at the moment the container is handed to your nominated carrier, which is the point at which you can actually take control, and it avoids the FOB problem of the risk passing on board a vessel while the container sits on the quay for days. It also lets you tender the ocean freight competitively rather than having it embedded in the product price, and it accommodates consolidated containers from several suppliers. FOB and CIF are terms designed for bulk, unpackaged sea cargo, and using them for containerised ribbon creates a risk gap in which you bear risk without practical control.

What is the practical difference between CIF and DDP for a ribbon buyer?

Under CIF the seller pays the ocean freight and provides minimum-cover insurance, but risk still passes when the goods are unloaded at the destination port - not when they reach your warehouse. So the container clearance, customs, and inland delivery all sit in your hands and at your risk under CIF. Under DDP the seller delivers to your premises with import clearance and duty on their account, giving you a single price and a single risk point at your door. DDP is the more convenient term for the buyer and the more expensive one for the seller, because the seller is carrying the duty and clearance risk in a jurisdiction they may not know well.

Does Incoterms 2020 change anything from the 2010 rules?

Yes, two obligations changed for containerised cargo. Under FOB and CIF in the 2020 rules, the seller is responsible for clearing the goods for export, which under the 2010 rules was the buyer's obligation in the classic port-to-port version. That is a real shift for a buyer who had assumed it would arrange export clearance itself, and it is the reason a contract should always state the Incoterms year rather than relying on the term code alone. The rest of the structure - where risk passes, who arranges carriage, and the treatment of insurance - is unchanged between the two versions.

Why is DDP more expensive than FOB when the supplier only has to add freight and duty?

Because the price of DDP reflects risk the seller is taking that FOB leaves with you. A supplier quoting DDP must price the import duty correctly, and ribbon HS classification is not always straightforward, because narrow woven fabrics can be classified differently depending on construction, pile, warp and weft composition and whether the width falls under a stated threshold. A supplier who misclassifies under DDP absorbs the duty and any penalty. There is also the working capital and administrative cost of clearing in an unfamiliar market. If a DDP price looks only slightly above FOB, it is worth asking what duty rate the price actually assumes.

Do Incoterms determine who owns the goods in transit?

No. Incoterms govern delivery, cost allocation and risk, and they do not determine title, which remains a matter for the contract and applicable law unless the contract states otherwise. This matters in a long transit where a buyer wants to insure the goods - the insurance policy needs to be written to start at the risk point, which may not be the title point. In most ribbon purchase contracts the two are aligned, but for consigned or free-issue material arrangements they can diverge, and the alignment should be checked rather than assumed.

What documentation should we require with an FCA ribbon shipment?

At minimum: commercial invoice with a description specific enough for customs classification, packing list with quantities and weights, the bill of lading or air waybill, and a certificate of origin where a preferential rate is being claimed. Add the material and compliance declarations your product programme depends on - composition statements, recycled content certification, chemical compliance documentation - and request these at order stage rather than at shipment stage, because a declaration requested a week before loading is usually unavailable or weaker. Check that the declared values and weights agree across the invoice, the packing list and the bill of lading, since a mismatch is a reliable way to lose a week at the destination port.
