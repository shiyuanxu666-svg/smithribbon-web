Custom Ribbon OEM Specification — How to Specify Decorations, Finishing, Cut Length, Packaging and Conversion So Two Mills Quote the Same Object 2026

Xiamen Smith Ribbon & Bow Co., Ltd. · 2026-11-05 · OEM Customisation & Specification · 12 min read

Ribbon OEMDecorationPackaging

Ribbon is usually bought as a material and costed as a material. The label on the cone says 15 mm grosgrain, and the purchase order says 15 mm grosgrain, and everybody behaves as though the specification is complete. It is not. The 15 mm identifies the substrate. Almost nothing that a buyer actually cares about lives in that number: the colour, the edge, the finish, the decoration, the presentation and the pack format are all specified separately, and all of them carry cost and all of them carry risk.

This is where OEM programmes go wrong, and they go wrong quietly. A buyer sources an attractive ribbon, approves a sample, places an order, and three weeks later the shipment arrives and it is technically the right ribbon and practically wrong for the product it is going into. The shade is right. The width is right. It is the wrong thing entirely, because the finishing, the cut length or the packaging were never properly specified, so the converting step now sits on someone’s desk and costs more than the ribbon did.

This guide walks through the specification decisions that actually determine whether an OEM programme works, in the order they are usually made, with an emphasis on where the cost sits and where the disputes come from. It is written for brand buyers and product teams rather than for purchasing specialists, because the decisions described here are mostly made before anyone opens a spreadsheet.

Where the Cost Actually Sits

The first thing to understand is that a decorative trim product is a stack of processes, and the base ribbon is usually the smallest part of the total cost. A rough distribution for a typical private-label trim programme looks like this, and it is worth keeping in mind whenever a quote looks low.

ElementTypical share of delivered unit costWhat drives it
Base ribbon material30–45%Fibre, width, construction, dye class, finishing
Decoration10–30%Print, foil, embroidery, jacquard, sublimation, spot colour count
Edge and cut treatment5–15%Overlock, selvedge, cut-to-length, heat-seal, wire insertion
Converting and assembly10–25%Bow assembly, folding, bundling, kitting, tagging
Packaging5–20%Paper bands, cartons, polybags, labels, barcodes
Freight, duty and overheads8–20%Mode, incoterm, volume density, port handling

The percentages vary enormously by programme, but the direction is consistent. A buyer who negotiates hard on the base ribbon and leaves decoration, packaging and freight loose has optimised the smallest lever and left the largest three untouched. Conversely, a programme where packaging has been specified by someone other than the person who owns the cost has a recurring, invisible problem that shows up in the landed cost of every unit and is very rarely attributed to packaging at all.

The practical rule: specify the whole unit before you negotiate any part of it. A quote is only comparable to another quote if both describe the same object. Comparing a mill-made ribbon quote against a converter-quoted decorated-and-packed ribbon is not a saving, it is a different product.

The Specification, Decision by Decision

The Substrate

Start with the material, because every downstream decision depends on it. Polyester satin, grosgrain, organza, velvet, herringbone twill and printed imitation are not interchangeable in feel, in drape, in price or in how they accept decoration. The practical questions are the width and the construction, the colour and its tolerance, the finish, and the certification coverage for that specific article.

Two details catch buyers out repeatedly. First, ribbon is sold by width in millimetres and by construction, and a 15 mm grosgrain is not simply a narrow 38 mm grosgrain. Grosgrain is woven on a specific reed profile, and a narrower width uses a different reed with different ends per centimetre, which changes the rib pitch and the perceived texture. If your product is visually sensitive to the rib, the width is a design decision and not just a measurement. Second, colour tolerance must be stated in a system both parties use. A shade band in a commercial colour space is a technical specification; “same as the sample” is not, because a sample ages, a lot drifts, and “the sample” is ambiguous after the fourth email.

Decoration

Decoration is where the money and the risk sit together, because it is the step with the most variables and the highest scrap rate. The options are worth separating clearly because they behave very differently under change.

Woven jacquard puts the pattern into the weave itself. It is the most durable option because there is nothing to abrade off, it produces a genuinely premium feel, and it carries the lowest risk of wear-related failure. It also carries the highest setup cost and the longest lead time, and it is difficult to change once tooling is committed, because the pattern is woven on a jacquard loom with a punched card or equivalent control. It suits a stable, long-running pattern and it punishes a fashion cycle that turns over quickly.

Printed sublimation or transfer printing penetrates the fibre and is nearly as durable as woven. It allows photographic reproduction and complex artwork, which woven jacquard cannot. Its limits are the polyester content and the colour gamut, and it is generally unsuitable for some natural fibres without a different process.

Direct rotary or screen printing applies pigment or dye on the surface. It is the most flexible in artwork and the most economical at volume, and the least durable. A surface-printed ribbon will scuff, crack and abrade along a folded edge where a woven jacquard will not. If the trim is on a product that is handled, folded, flexed or washed, surface printing needs a durability test before it is specified, not after.

Foil and metallic stamping adds a premium visual effect and raises questions a buyer should settle early: whether the metallic effect is hot-stamp foil, which is surface-applied and wears, or a metallic yarn woven or printed into the construction, which does not. They look similar in a photograph and behave very differently in use. Foil also raises the question of whether the article needs to be tested for restricted-substance requirements, because some foil adhesives and pigment systems carry documentation requirements that differ from the base textile.

Embroidery allows high-density, high-definition motifs and carries a per-design setup and a per-item run cost. For a bow or an appliqué it is often the only way to achieve a certain look. Its limitations are the minimum order quantity, the design-modification cost and the machine time, which is why embroidery on trim is typically used for a fixed design at stable volume.

Jacquard on woven, versus print on woven is the choice most often made on price and most often regretted on lead time. The rule of thumb that has held up across many programmes is simple: if the artwork is fixed and will be produced for more than a couple of seasons, woven jacquard costs less over the programme life and looks better at the end of it. If the artwork will change, print is cheaper to change even though it costs more per unit.

Colour Management

Colour is decided before decoration is chosen, and it constrains everything afterwards. Three questions have to be answered in writing before a quotation means anything.

What colour space and what standard? Pantone is a spot colour system and an excellent commercial shorthand, but a Pantone number does not describe a printed ribbon. It describes an ink. What matters on the ribbon is the resulting measured colour, and the question to ask is what tolerance around what reference applies. Where a brand has a physical ribbon standard from a previous supplier, the new programme can be specified against that physical standard and matched with a spectrophotometer, which removes an entire category of argument. Where the brand only has a digital Pantone value, the mill needs a physical target, and a physical swatch or a dip-dye lab dip becomes the first step of the programme rather than a formality.

What is the tolerance and who absorbs drift? Colour will drift between lots even within the same specification, particularly on different substrate constructions. A tolerance that is reasonable on solid woven ribbon may be unreasonable on a jacquard where the pattern contains fine detail. This needs to be a stated number, in a stated measurement condition, with a stated measurement method, and it needs to include the tolerance on the base colour and the tolerance on the decoration colour separately.

How is the master standard controlled? A colour approval is a physical artefact. If it lives in one email thread it will be lost, and six months later a dispute about shade will be settled by whoever has the better photograph. A sealed physical master, signed and held, with a reference to a calibrated measurement, is a very small administrative cost that prevents a very large argument.

Edge and Cut Treatment

Edge treatment is routinely omitted from specifications and then becomes a quality dispute, because it is visible, it is judged by the customer, and it is not described anywhere.

For woven ribbons, the raw edge is either overlocked to prevent fraying or selvedged as part of the weave. Overlock is cheaper and standard; selvedge is a cleaner edge, generally found on higher-grade and wider goods, and it costs more to produce. For cut-to-length ribbon used as gift-wrap or as a trim element, the cut quality determines how the piece presents, and a clean heat-sealed or precision-cut edge looks entirely different from a guillotined edge, particularly in a satin construction where the cut can leave a visible line and the sheen catches differently.

For trim used on a product that will be bent or folded repeatedly, edge fraying is a functional issue, not a cosmetic one, and the specification should say whether fraying over a stated number of flex cycles is acceptable. It also matters whether the ribbon is cut from a continuous web or from a pre-cut component, because the two have different economics and different consistency of length.

Packaging and Presentation

Packaging is the element most often owned by nobody. It sits between the ribbon, the merchandising team and the logistics team, and it is frequently specified in an email after the product has been developed, at which point it is too late to influence anything except cost.

Three decisions matter here and all three belong to the buyer rather than the mill. What is the unit of supply — a cone, a spool, a cut length, a set of bows on a card, a bundle? What travels with the product, meaning bands, labels, hang tags, barcodes, care and origin labels, and where they are applied, by the mill or by a later operation? And what happens to the pack downstream, meaning whether the packaging itself must be recyclable, whether it must carry specific labelling for a market, or whether the retailer requires a particular format for shelf presentation.

The cost of packaging is worth pricing explicitly rather than absorbing into a unit rate, because it scales differently. A paper band costs a fraction of a cent per unit and is essentially free at volume. A printed hang tag with a barcode and a die-cut shape costs several times more. Retailer-required formats can add cost that nobody attributes to packaging because it arrives as part of a freight invoice. Getting the packaging programme specified at the same time as the trim programme is one of the highest-leverage habits available, and the cost of doing it late is a rework cycle and a freight discrepancy.

Labelling, Barcodes and Compliance Marks

Any trim that leaves your control and enters a retailer's warehouse is subject to labelling requirements that vary by market, by retailer and by material content. Fibre content declarations, origin marking, care labelling where the trim forms part of a textile article, importer identification, and country-of-origin marking for various territories each have their own rules and their own placement conventions.

Two practical points. First, specify where the label goes, not just what it says, because a label placed on a ribbon that is later cut and folded may end up on a piece of the trim that is not visible to the customer, or in a position where it interferes with the assembly operation. Second, decide early whether labelling is applied by the mill or in a separate downstream operation, because if the mill applies it, the mill needs the artwork and the data, and that is a data feed and an artwork approval to manage; if it is applied downstream, it is a separate step with its own cost and its own failure rate.

Lead Time and the Real Programme Shape

Lead time for decorated ribbon is not the weaving time. It is the sum of artwork preparation, plate or screen or loom setup, material sourcing, dyeing, weaving, decoration, finishing, converting, packaging, inspection and freight. A programme that a mill quotes at six weeks may actually run to eleven, and the difference is almost always in the steps that were not in the quote.

Two practical warnings. Artwork preparation is the largest hidden variable, because artwork that arrives as a hand-edited file with an embedded raster image and an unresolved font costs far more to prepare than artwork that arrives as vector files with fonts outlined and a colour list attached. And the first production run is always longer than the repeat run, because everything is being learned at once: how the colour behaves on the actual substrate, how the decoration takes, how the packaging fits, how the labels are applied. Buyers who build the programme calendar on the repeat-run lead time will be disappointed in the first quarter and then quietly under-buy in the second.

Change Control and Re-Ordering

A decorative trim programme is rarely static. Designs change, colours are adjusted, pack sizes move and the product evolves. The question that determines whether a programme stays affordable is what happens to the setup costs when the design changes.

The mechanics are unglamorous but decisive. Which costs are one-time and which are per-unit. Which setups are reusable, and under what conditions. What minimum quantity triggers a new setup, and who bears it. What notice period applies. Whether a colour adjustment within an agreed tolerance is a change at all, or a tolerance breach, because these two have very different consequences and confusing them causes arguments that could have been avoided by defining the tolerance in the first place.

A workable arrangement records the setup inventory — which plates, which screens, which loom cards, which dies, which barcode formats are open and available — and agrees in advance what happens to each on a change. Without that record, every change is treated as a new programme by the mill, and the buyer pays setup again without knowing they were entitled to keep it.

Writing the Brief So It Can Be Quoted Identically

The test of a ribbon specification is whether two mills could quote it and deliver equivalent product. If the wording leaves genuine room for interpretation, you will get different products at the same price and compare them for ever. A brief that produces comparable quotes contains the following.

Material and construction, named specifically. Width, with the tolerance and the measurement method. Colour, with the standard, the reference and the tolerance. Decoration technique, with the artwork version and the spot colour count. Edge treatment. Cut or continuous, with cut length and tolerance where relevant. Component construction, including whether wire, foil, adhesive or elastic is present. Certification requirement for that specific article, not for the mill in general. Packaging, stated as a unit of supply with the label and barcode requirements. Testing required, with the method and the acceptance criteria. Inspection level and the sampling plan. The delivery terms, the packaging for shipment and the shipping carton specification.

And one more, which is the one most often left out: what happens when it goes wrong. The acceptance criteria, the defect classification, the corrective action route and the credit process. Decided when nothing is wrong, these are administrative. Decided after a container of subtly off-shade ribbon arrives, they are the difference between a resolved issue and a season's delay.

The one-page test: hand the specification to a colleague who has never seen the programme and ask them to describe the product back to you and to state what would constitute a defect. If they cannot do both, the specification is not finished.

The Short Version

Ribbon specification is not a width and a colour. It is a substrate, a colour with a reference and a tolerance, a decoration technique with a durability implication, an edge treatment, a converting format, a packaging format, a labelling scheme, a lead time that includes steps usually left out of the quote, and a change-control arrangement that records what setup cost is reusable. The base ribbon is often the smallest part of the delivered unit cost, so negotiating hard on it while leaving decoration, packaging and freight loose is optimising the smallest lever. The most common programme failure is not a bad ribbon; it is a good ribbon that was never fully specified, followed by a converting problem that is attributed to the wrong department. Specify the whole unit, control the colour master physically, price packaging explicitly, and decide the change rules before the first order.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between woven jacquard and printed ribbon for a custom trim programme?

Woven jacquard places the pattern into the weave, so there is no surface layer to abrade and it lasts as long as the fabric itself, at the highest setup cost and longest lead time. Printing applies the pattern to the surface, which allows photographic reproduction and complex artwork at lower setup cost and shorter lead time, but the surface layer can scuff, crack and wear along folded edges. The practical rule is that a fixed artwork produced for more than a couple of seasons is usually cheaper over the programme life in woven jacquard, while changing artwork argues for printing even at a higher unit cost.

How many spot colours should I specify to keep decoration costs down?

Fewer spot colours cost less, because each additional colour in a print design typically adds a screen, a plate, a setup and a colour-pass on the machine, and the saving from consolidating colours is not linear because the first colour carries much of the setup. The point at which it becomes worth asking is usually the third or fourth colour. Before reducing the count, though, check whether the design can be re-expressed in fewer colours without losing what the design is doing, because a modest simplification in the artwork frequently saves more than the per-colour cost and keeps the visual intent. Where a design genuinely needs many colours, an alternative such as woven jacquard or sublimation may be both cheaper and better at that colour count.

How tight should the colour tolerance be on custom ribbon?

Tight enough to be measurable and to have a defined action if breached, but not tighter than the process can reliably hold, because an unachievable tolerance generates continuous disputes and inspection rejects rather than quality. Two things must be specified together: the tolerance value and the measurement condition, including illuminant, geometry and the instrument, because a colour difference measured in one condition is not the same number as in another. For textured and jacquard constructions the tolerance should be expressed separately for the ground colour and the pattern colours, since fine pattern detail in jacquard normally supports a wider band than a solid ground in the same article. Where possible, specify against a physical master held by both parties and measured by spectrophotometer, because that removes the argument entirely.

What packaging should I specify for private-label ribbon supplied to a retailer?

Start from the unit of supply your receiving warehouse actually handles, because a cone, a cut length, a set of bows on a card and a bundled pack are four different operations for the retailer and only one of them will match their system. Then specify the ancillary items explicitly: paper or film bands, hang tags, barcode placement and symbology, care and fibre-content labels, origin marking and importer details. Finally, state the pack material and whether the retailer requires a specific recycling or labelling format for it. The frequent error is leaving packaging to a later stage, when it arrives as a freight discrepancy rather than a specification issue, and at that point the only available lever is cost.

Does ribbon decoration affect what certification I need?

Yes, and in ways that are frequently overlooked. The base ribbon certification covers the base article; a decorative process introduces new materials into the finished product that may require their own evidence. Foil stamping brings an adhesive and metallic pigment system into the article, embroidery thread is a separate textile, and fusible adhesives carry their own substance requirements. This matters for OEKO-TEX class selection, for restricted-substance declarations and for any chemical-safety compliance that applies to the finished article. The practical move is to require the certification to name the finished article with its decoration, rather than the base material with a colour reference, and to confirm that a certificate held for the substrate genuinely covers the decorated construction. A certificate for the base fabric does not extend to a decorated article.

How do I stop being charged for setup costs twice on a re-order?

By keeping and sharing a setup inventory. Record every one-time asset the programme creates: printing plates or screens, jacquard loom cards, embroidery digitising files, cutting dies, banding tooling, barcode formats and artwork masters, with the quantity and version of each. Then agree in advance, in the purchase agreement or a covering specification, what happens to those assets on a design change, what notice applies, and which changes reuse which assets. The problem is rarely contractual; it is informational, because most mills do hold your assets and are glad to reuse them, but they cannot invoice a saving that no one asked for. A short shared schedule, updated with each order, resolves it and also gives the mill better information for forecasting, which is in both parties' interests.

What is the most common mistake when specifying custom ribbon for a new product?

Specifying the ribbon and leaving the conversion, the packaging and the labelling to be decided later, typically by a different team. The result is a technically correct substrate delivered in a format the receiving warehouse does not want, with labels applied in the wrong place or not at all, and packaging that consumes a meaningful share of the unit cost without anyone having approved it. The second most common is approving a sample that was made on a different substrate, colourway or decoration process from the one that will be produced, so the approval proves nothing about the production article. Both problems are avoided by treating the ribbon, the conversion, the packaging and the labelling as one specification reviewed at the same time, and by requiring the approval sample to be made on production tooling.
